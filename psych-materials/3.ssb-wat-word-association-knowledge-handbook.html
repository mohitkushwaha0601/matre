Using this handbook

The Word Association Test looks trivial: a word appears, you write a sentence. Yet it is one of the hardest tests to fake and one of the easiest to improve, because it rewards a trained habit of thought, not a memorised answer.

Part I explains what the test is for and how your mind responds under a 15-second limit. Part II turns that into a repeatable strategy. Part III is the word bank: 120 words, each with three model sentences and the OLQ it shows. The toolkit closes with debates, scenarios, practice questions and a revision sheet. A shorter treatment of all psychology tests exists in the overview guide.

How the three versions work

Version 1 is personal: an "I" sentence from experience or intent. Version 2 is social: a sentence about a team, unit or community. Version 3 is a principle: a short, sound belief. All three are valid. Mixing them across your 60 answers gives a natural, varied profile; use the models to learn the pattern, then write in your own words.

Do not memorise the bank

Sentences copied from a book sound alike, and psychologists see the same lines repeatedly. Treat the models as worked examples of thinking. Your own true experiences, written in the same style, will always read as stronger.

Part I · Understanding the test

What the test is and what it reads

Format and purpose

On the psychology day, candidates usually take four tests in order: the Thematic Apperception Test (TAT), the Word Association Test (WAT), the Situation Reaction Test (SRT) and the Self Description Test (SDT). In the WAT, 60 words appear one at a time, each for about 15 seconds, and the candidate writes the first sentence that comes to mind on a response sheet. The whole test takes about 15 minutes.

The words are a mixture of positive (courage, team), negative (failure, fear), neutral (book, road) and ambiguous (order, train) words. The psychologist does not mark a sentence right or wrong. They read all 60 sentences together, along with the TAT, SRT and SDT, and look for consistent patterns in how you think and what you value.

The 15 OLQs in a sentence

SSB assesses 15 Officer Like Qualities (OLQs), grouped into four factors. A WAT sentence rarely shows all of them, but a good one shows at least one clearly. Knowing the list lets you steer, within the 15 seconds, toward the quality a sentence naturally expresses.

OLQs by factor, and how a sentence can show them
FactorOLQsHow a WAT sentence shows it
I · Planning and organisingEffective intelligence, reasoning ability, organising ability, power of expressionA sensible plan, logical cause and effect, clear and complete wording.
II · Social adjustmentSocial adaptability, cooperation, sense of responsibilityWorking with others, owning duties, respecting people.
III · Social effectivenessInitiative, self-confidence, speed of decision, ability to influence the group, livelinessActing first, deciding quickly, leading, positive energy.
IV · DynamicDetermination, courage, staminaPersisting, facing danger, enduring effort.
Reasoning

Take the word flood. "Floods cause damage" is accurate but shows nothing about you. "I joined volunteers to move families from the flooded area" shows initiative, cooperation and responsibility in ten words. The second is not more dramatic; it is simply an action by someone who acts.

The psychology of a 15-second answer

The test rests on free association: a stimulus word triggers the first related thought, shaped by your experiences, beliefs and habits of mind. Under time pressure there is little room to edit, so the sentence is a thin slice of how you naturally frame the world. This is why one sentence says little and sixty say a lot.

Three ideas help you work with this instead of against it.

  • Pattern, not point. Someone who writes "Failure is the end" for one word and "Defeat is shameful" for another shows a pattern of pessimism. A pattern of constructive response shows resilience.
  • Projection. Sentences carry attitudes you may not mean to show: blame ("Teachers are strict"), anxiety ("Fear stops me"), or ego ("I am the best").
  • The habit beats the act. You cannot fake a positive outlook in 15 seconds for 60 words. You can, however, practise a thinking habit until it is automatic: look for the action, the lesson, the others involved.

Practical implication

Do not aim to sound perfect or heroic. Aim for a realistic, active and balanced person, who faces problems, works with people and learns from setbacks. That is also what the other tests will show, so the picture stays consistent.

Part II · Strategy

Building the answer

Rules of a strong sentence

A strong sentence is complete, positive, specific and brief. Twelve to fifteen seconds allow roughly eight to twelve words; plan to write that, not an essay.

Do

  • Write a complete sentence with a subject and verb.
  • Keep it positive and constructive.
  • Show an action: helped, led, learned, planned.
  • Use "I" about a third of the time, and mix with team and principle.
  • Stay realistic: small true actions beat grand claims.
  • Write the first idea, then turn it positive.

Avoid

  • Definitions ("Courage means being brave").
  • Preaching ("We should always help").
  • Negative statements ("I never panic").
  • Blaming others or society.
  • Same sentence pattern 60 times.
  • Leaving blanks or overwriting to fix mistakes.

Word types and negative words

Words fall into four types, and each calls for a slightly different approach.

Approach by word type
TypeExamplesApproach
Positivecourage, team, dutyAvoid cliché and boasting; ground it in action or a shared outcome.
Negativefailure, fear, deathAcknowledge, then convert to learning or resolve.
Neutralbook, road, waterLink to a habit, a duty or a social good.
Ambiguousorder, train, waitPick the meaning that lets you show a quality.

The three Cs for negative words

When a word triggers something negative, do not suppress the thought; use it. Convert it into a lesson ("I learned from my failure"), Contain it by showing control ("I calm myself with deep breaths when anger rises"), or place it in Context where facing it is a duty ("Soldiers who face danger train for it"). The negative word then reveals maturity instead of distress.

Honesty check

Positivity does not mean denial. "I felt jealous, then asked him how he studied" is honest and constructive. "I never feel jealous" is not believable and invites doubt on all your other answers.

Common traps

  • TrapClichés. "Honesty is the best policy" appears in thousands of sheets. Say what you did or saw.
  • TrapHeroic exaggeration. Single-handedly saving a village in every sentence looks false; sprinkle small, believable actions.
  • TrapPhilosophical drift. "Life is a mystery" says nothing about you.
  • TrapRepetition. If every sentence starts "I help", the pattern looks scripted.
  • TrapHidden negativity. Sentences like "Politics is corrupt" show bitterness; shift to "I follow politics to vote wisely".
  • TrapIncomplete or illegible answers. A fragment or an unreadable word wastes the 15 seconds. Write clearly in short words.
  • TrapContradiction. Claiming a quality in the WAT that your SDT or interview does not support damages trust.
  • TrapFreezing. A blank gives the psychologist nothing; any safe, positive sentence is better.

Method and a worked example

Use a six-step loop that fits within 15 seconds once it is a habit. Practise it slowly first, then speed up.

Worked example: the word "Failure"

Read
See the word fully; do not guess before the whole word is read.
First thought
"Exam I failed" or "disappointment". Accept it; do not fight it.
Filter
Ask: which OLQ can this show? Determination, reasoning, responsibility.
Frame
Pick a version: personal (I learned), social (the team studied it) or principle.
Write
"I learned from my failure in the first test and scored better next time."
Check
Complete, positive, about ten words, one clear action. Move on.
Reasoning

The sentence admits the failure honestly, which builds credibility, then shows two OLQs: reasoning ability (it learned a cause) and determination (it came back and improved). A weaker version, "Failure is the pillar of success", sounds borrowed and shows no action.

Choose the reading that lets you show a quality and avoid anything that glorifies violence or cynicism.

Analysis, practice and revision

Myths and debates

Advice on the WAT is abundant and often contradictory. Tags separate what is well supported from what is opinion.

  • EstablishedSentences are read together, not alone. One odd answer is rarely decisive; patterns are.
  • EstablishedConsistency across tests matters. Claims in the WAT that the SDT or interview contradict damage credibility.
  • Interpretation"Always be positive." Mostly sound, but a purely sunny profile can look rehearsed; honest acknowledgement of a feeling plus a constructive response reads more real.
  • DisputedAttempt all 60 or fewer, better ones. Many mentors say complete as many as you can; a blank gives no information. Quality still matters, so a short, safe sentence beats a blank.
  • DisputedFirst person versus third person. Some advise "I" sentences for authenticity; others prefer general statements to avoid overclaiming. A mix works for most.
  • InterpretationMemorised sentences. They save thinking time but sound alike and may mismatch your other tests.
  • Re-verifyTest details. Timings, word counts and instructions can change; follow the call-up letter.
Lesser-known fact

Writing speed is part of the test: well-prepared candidates attempt more words with the same quality, so practising short, clean sentences also helps the overall impression.

Scenarios and trade-offs

Real tests throw up awkward moments. The options below show what is available and what each trades.

You freeze on a word

  • Write the first neutral, positive sentence about it, even a simple one.
  • Do not stare; losing the next word costs more.
  • Trade-off: a plain sentence over a blank.

You do not know the word

  • Guess from its roots, or write a general positive line about a related idea.
  • Do not invent a false meaning with confidence.
  • Trade-off: a brief sentence over a long mistake.

A word touches a painful memory

  • Write a short, honest, constructive line about how you coped.
  • Avoid blame or self-pity.
  • Trade-off: honest and brief over dramatic.

You fall behind the screen

  • Skip the one you are on and catch the next word.
  • Shorten sentences to six or seven words.
  • Trade-off: a few fewer answers over a late sheet.

Preparation timeline

A suggested eight-week plan, then the 15 seconds of a single word. Adjust to your calendar.

  • Week 1Read the 15 OLQs; write five sentences a day in untimed mode and tag the OLQ each shows.
  • Week 2Write 20 words daily, untimed; apply the three Cs to every negative word.
  • Week 3Move to 25 seconds per word; keep answers complete and under twelve words.
  • Week 4Move to 15 seconds per word; practise on a random 60-word list.
  • Week 5Do three full mock tests; review for repetition, clichés and negativity.
  • Week 6Share sheets with a mentor or friend; ask what your sentences say about you.
  • Week 7Align with your SDT and TAT stories; fix any contradictions.
  • Week 8Two final mocks; rest before the test and do not cram new sentences.
  • 0-3 sRead the word fully; accept the first thought.
  • 3-6 sFilter for an OLQ; choose personal, social or principle.
  • 6-14 sWrite one clear, positive, complete sentence.
  • 14-15 sGlance to check it, then eyes up for the next word.

Glossary

WAT
Word Association Test: a sentence for each of about 60 words, in about 15 seconds each.
TAT
Thematic Apperception Test: writing stories about pictures.
SRT
Situation Reaction Test: writing reactions to everyday situations.
SDT
Self Description Test: describing how others, and you, see you.
OLQ
Officer Like Quality: one of 15 traits assessed at SSB.
Free association
The first thought that arises in response to a word.
Projection
Revealing one's own attitudes through descriptions of others or things.
Cliché
An overused phrase that says nothing personal.
Convert
Turn a negative word's thought into a lesson or resolve.
Contain
Show control over a feeling instead of denying it.
Context
Place a hard word in a duty or service setting.
Version 1, 2, 3
Personal, social and principle sentences used in this handbook.
Psychologist
The assessor who reads your psychology day responses.
Consistency
Agreement across your tests and interview.
Social desirability
Writing what sounds good instead of what is true.
Holistic reading
Judging the whole set of answers together.

Rapid revision sheet

One-page summary

  • WAT: about 60 words, about 15 seconds each, one sentence per word; read as a whole.
  • Each sentence should be complete, positive, short (8-12 words) and show an action.
  • 15 OLQs in four factors: planning and organising, social adjustment, social effectiveness, dynamic.
  • Free association reveals patterns; you cannot fake 60 answers but you can train a habit.
  • Negative words: convert, contain or contextualise.
  • Use personal, social and principle sentences in a mix.
  • Avoid definitions, preaching, clichés, blame, exaggeration and repetition.
  • Be consistent with your SDT, TAT and interview.
  • Do not freeze: any safe positive sentence beats a blank.
  • Practise in stages: untimed, 25 seconds, then 15 seconds; review with a mentor.
  • Follow the call-up letter for current timings and rules.
