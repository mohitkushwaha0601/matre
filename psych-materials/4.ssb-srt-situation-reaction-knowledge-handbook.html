Using this handbook

The Situation Reaction Test puts a candidate in about sixty short, everyday and difficult situations and asks one thing: what would you do? It is the most direct window the psychologist has onto your judgement, speed and sense of duty, and it improves with a trained way of thinking, not a memorised script.

Part I explains what the test reads and how the mind decides under pressure. Part II gives a repeatable four-step strategy. Part III is the situation bank: 120 situations, each with three model reactions and the OLQ it shows. The toolkit closes with debates, scenarios, practice questions and a revision sheet. A shorter treatment of all four psychology tests is in the overview guide, and the Word Association Test is covered in its own guide.

How the three versions work

Version 1 is direct action: the person acts at once with what he has. Version 2 is through people and channels: he mobilises others or informs the proper authority and acts together with them. Version 3 is root cause and follow-up: he also fixes why it happened or checks the result. All three are sound. Use the style that fits the situation, and vary it across your 60 answers.

Writing convention

Situations are written about "he" or "she", and reactions are written as short past-tense actions about that person, for example "Gave first aid, called an ambulance and stayed with him till it came." The models follow that style. Do not memorise them: they teach the thinking, and your own genuine experience, in the same style, will always read as stronger.

Part I · Understanding the test

What the test is and what it reads

Format and purpose

On the psychology day, candidates usually take the Thematic Apperception Test (TAT), Word Association Test (WAT), Situation Reaction Test (SRT) and Self Description Test (SDT), in that order. In the SRT, a booklet carries about 60 short situations, and the candidate has about 30 minutes to write a reaction to each, which works out to roughly 30 seconds per situation. Most candidates finish only part of the booklet; completing as many as possible, in order, matters.

The situations are drawn from daily life, studies, travel, emergencies, friendship, family, work and moral choice. The psychologist does not mark an answer right or wrong; they look for how you naturally behave when something happens, what you prioritise, and whether your reactions fit the other tests.

The OLQs a reaction can show

SSB assesses 15 Officer Like Qualities (OLQs) in four factors. The SRT is unusually good at showing several of them at once, because a single decision involves reasoning, courage, cooperation and responsibility together.

OLQs by factor, and how a reaction shows them
FactorOLQsHow an SRT reaction shows it
I · Planning and organisingEffective intelligence, reasoning ability, organising ability, power of expressionA sensible order of steps, use of available resources, a clear and complete answer.
II · Social adjustmentSocial adaptability, cooperation, sense of responsibilityInvolving others, respecting people, owning the problem.
III · Social effectivenessInitiative, self-confidence, speed of decision, ability to influence the group, livelinessActing first, deciding quickly, taking charge calmly.
IV · DynamicDetermination, courage, staminaStaying with the task, facing danger sensibly, enduring effort.
Reasoning

Take "He sees a man collapse on the road." "Called for help" shows little. "Checked he was breathing, asked a passer-by to call an ambulance, loosened his collar and stayed till it came" shows speed of decision, organising ability, cooperation and responsibility in one line, simply because each action is concrete and in a sensible order.

The psychology of a 30-second reaction

The SRT is a projective test with a practical face. Faced with an unseen situation and almost no time, the mind reaches for the response that is most habitual: the one it has used before, or the one it believes in. That habitual response is what the psychologist reads.

  • Pattern, not point. Fleeing in one answer and fighting in the next means little; a consistent pattern of acting, informing and following up shows a reliable temperament.
  • Priorities show. When duty and comfort clash, what you write first reveals what you put first: life before property, duty before self, team before ego.
  • Judgement versus impulse. Heroics without thought look rash; endless caution looks timid. A balanced reaction acts quickly and sensibly.
  • The habit beats the act. You cannot fake 60 reactions in 30 minutes. You can train a thinking habit until it is automatic.

Practical implication

Do not try to sound like a hero. Aim to sound like a person who sees the problem, does the useful thing first, brings in the right people and finishes the job. This also matches what the group tasks and the interview will show.

Part II · Strategy

Building the reaction

Anatomy of a strong reaction

A strong reaction is realistic, complete, positive and in a sensible order. Remember four As: Assess the situation for safety and urgency, Act on the most important thing first, Assist by involving the right people or resources, and then Afterwards follow up or prevent a repeat. You do not need all four every time; one or two clear, ordered steps are usually enough.

Do

  • Put the most urgent action first.
  • Use available resources: people, phone, police, doctor.
  • Write two or three steps in order, not one vague line.
  • Show concern for people before property.
  • Be realistic about what one person can do.
  • Finish the thought: the result or the follow-up.

Avoid

  • "Ran away" or "ignored" in any form.
  • Lone heroics against armed or many people.
  • Vague words: "tried", "would see", "managed".
  • Waiting for others to act first.
  • Blaming others, or anger and revenge.
  • Giving up when the first step fails.

Situation types and how to read them

Before writing, spend two seconds classifying the situation. The type tells you what the psychologist most wants to see.

Approach by situation type
TypeExampleWhat to show
EmergencyAn accident, a fire, a drowningSpeed, calm, safety first, help from others.
Conflicting dutiesAn exam and a sick relativeClear priority and a way to cover both.
InterpersonalA quarrel between friendsEmpathy, fairness, firmness without anger.
Authority and ethicsAn order that seems wrong; a bribeIntegrity through proper channels.
Personal setbackFailure, loss, illnessResilience, a plan, learning.
Unfamiliar or impossibleLost in a forest, cut off by a riverCalm reasoning with the resources at hand.
Impossible situations

Some situations seem to have no good answer. The test is then of attitude and reasoning: do not declare it impossible. State the safest realistic step, such as waiting at a fixed place, signalling, or going for help, and do it calmly.

Common traps

  • TrapEscape. "Ran away", "left the place" or "avoided" suggest you step back from responsibility.
  • TrapSuperman answers. Fighting five armed robbers alone is not courage but poor judgement; call for help and act sensibly.
  • TrapPassive words. "Informed the police and went home" ends too early; stay, assist, follow up.
  • TrapVagueness. "Handled the situation" says nothing; state what was done.
  • TrapRepetition. "Informed the police" in every answer looks scripted.
  • TrapWrong priority. Saving a bag before a person, or a deadline before a life, shows poor values.
  • TrapRevenge or anger. Punishing or shouting at someone shows poor self-control.
  • TrapLeaving blanks. Skipped situations give the psychologist no information; a short sensible reaction is better than nothing.
  • TrapContradiction. Claiming extreme boldness here that your SDT or interview does not support damages trust.

Method and a worked example

A six-step loop fits within about 30 seconds once it is habit. Practise it slowly first, then speed up.

Worked example: "He is travelling by train at night and sees a man trying to snatch a woman's bag."

Read
Read the whole situation; note who is in danger and who is present.
Classify
Crime and emergency: speed, safety and help from others.
Priority
The woman's safety first, the bag second, catching the thief third.
Resources
Fellow passengers, the chain, the railway police helpline, the guard.
Write
"Shouted to alert others, with fellow passengers caught the man, returned the bag, called the railway police and gave a statement."
Check
Realistic, sequenced, safety-aware, complete. Move on.
Reasoning

The reaction does not claim that one person overpowers the thief. It first alerts others (cooperation and influence), acts together (safe and practical), then hands over to the proper authority and gives a statement (responsibility and sense of process). A weaker answer, "caught the thief and beat him", shows poor self-control and ignores risk.

Reasoning: why the "drowning child" answer works

The first version does not send the candidate into the water blindly. "Threw a rope or float, and pulled the child out only if he could swim safely" shows reasoning ability and sound courage: a drowning rescuer can add a second victim. Version 2 mobilises the best swimmer, which shows cooperation and influence. Version 3 solves the root cause by asking for a fence.

Duty and responsibility

These situations pit duty against comfort or two duties against each other. The psychologist reads your priorities and your ability to find a way to do both. The weakest answers drop one duty without effort; the best ones plan to fulfil both.

Friends, peers and conflict

Interpersonal situations test empathy, fairness and firmness. The balance to strike: care about the person, hold to the principle, and avoid both aggression and appeasement. Where a talk can solve it, talk first.

Authority and integrity

Here the test is integrity and respect for the chain of command. A strong answer holds to what is right, stays respectful and uses proper channels. It does not join wrong, and it does not take the law into its own hands.

Personal setbacks and pressure

Here the psychologist reads resilience and emotional maturity. Strong answers accept the setback honestly, then do something specific: find the cause, make a plan, seek help. Self-pity and blame are the failure modes.

Travel, nature and adventure

Outdoor situations test calm reasoning with limited resources. A strong reaction conserves energy, stays together, signals for help and avoids panic. Guessing and wandering are the usual errors.

Reasoning: why "lost in a forest" works

The instinct is to push on. The strong answer stops, thinks and conserves energy, then signals. This reads as reasoning ability and presence of mind, and it matches survival practice: the search party finds a stationary person faster than a wandering one.

Groups and leadership

These situations ask whether you can lead without bullying and follow without sulking. The ideal reaction listens, decides, shares credit and takes responsibility when things go wrong.

Tricky dilemmas

Dilemmas have no perfect answer. The psychologist reads values and judgement: do you weigh harm, honesty, loyalty and duty sensibly? Where possible, find a response that respects more than one value.

Knowledge toolkit

Analysis, practice and revision

Myths and debates

Advice on the SRT is plentiful and often contradictory. Tags separate what is well supported from opinion.

  • EstablishedAnswers are read as a set. One odd reaction rarely decides anything; the pattern does.
  • EstablishedConsistency matters. Reactions that clash with your TAT, SDT or interview damage credibility.
  • Interpretation"Always be positive and heroic." Positive and active, yes; heroic, no. A sensible, realistic person who calls for help reads better than a lone fighter.
  • DisputedLength: short versus detailed. Some mentors prefer one tight line to save time; others want two or three steps. A common compromise is one to three short ordered actions.
  • DisputedAttempt all situations or fewer, better ones. Most mentors advise finishing as many as you can in order; quality should not fall below a sensible minimum.
  • DisputedShould every answer involve others? Sometimes immediate personal action is best; a mix of direct action and teamwork looks more natural.
  • InterpretationMemorised model answers. They save thinking time but sound alike and can mismatch your other tests.
  • Re-verifyTest details. The number of situations and the time allowed can change; follow your call-up letter.
Lesser-known fact

Because the booklet is long and time is short, the later situations are often attempted in a rush. Candidates who practise a fast habit keep the quality of their last answers close to the first, which helps the overall impression.

Scenarios and trade-offs

Real tests throw up awkward moments. The options below show what is available and what each trades.

You do not know what to do

  • Fall back on the four As: assess, act, assist, afterwards.
  • Write the safest sensible first step.
  • Trade-off: a plain, safe answer over a blank.

The situation seems impossible

  • State the best realistic step with the resources at hand.
  • Do not write that nothing can be done.
  • Trade-off: calm and modest over dramatic.

The situation is unfamiliar

  • Think of the principle: safety, honesty, duty.
  • Seek help from people who know.
  • Trade-off: asking for help over guessing.

You are running out of time

  • Shorten to one line with the key action.
  • Skip a hard one and return if time allows.
  • Trade-off: more complete sheet over a few long answers.

Preparation timeline

A suggested eight-week plan, then the 30 seconds of a single situation. Adjust to your calendar.

  • Week 1Read the 15 OLQs; write five reactions a day untimed and tag the OLQs each shows.
  • Week 2Write ten situations a day; apply the four As to each and sort the situation types.
  • Week 3Write in about 60 seconds each; keep to one to three ordered actions.
  • Week 4Move to about 30 seconds each on a random 30-situation set.
  • Week 5Do two full 60-situation mocks in 30 minutes; check for escape, repetition and vagueness.
  • Week 6Share sheets with a mentor; ask what the reactions say about you.
  • Week 7Align with your SDT and TAT; fix any contradictions.
  • Week 8Two final mocks; rest before the test and do not cram new answers.
  • 0-6 sRead the whole situation; classify it.
  • 6-12 sFind the priority and the resources; choose direct, people or root-cause style.
  • 12-27 sWrite one to three clear, ordered actions.
  • 27-30 sGlance to check, then move to the next situation.

Glossary

SRT
Situation Reaction Test: a written reaction to each of about 60 short situations in about 30 minutes.
WAT
Word Association Test: a sentence for each of about 60 words.
TAT
Thematic Apperception Test: writing stories about pictures.
SDT
Self Description Test: describing how others and you see you.
OLQ
Officer Like Quality: one of 15 traits assessed at SSB.
Projective test
A test where responses to an open stimulus reveal habitual thinking.
Four As
Assess, Act, Assist, Afterwards: the response sequence used in this handbook.
Direct action
Version 1: the person acts at once with what he has.
Through people and channels
Version 2: involving others or the proper authority.
Root cause and follow-up
Version 3: fixing why it happened and checking the result.
Chain of command
The proper line of authority through which concerns are raised.
Presence of mind
Staying calm and thinking clearly in a crisis.
Speed of decision
Reaching a sound decision quickly, a Factor III OLQ.
Resourcefulness
Making good use of what is available.
Consistency
Agreement across your tests and interview.
Social desirability
Writing what sounds good instead of what is true.

Rapid revision sheet

One-page summary

  • SRT: about 60 situations, about 30 minutes, a short written reaction each; read as a whole.
  • A strong reaction is realistic, complete, positive and in a sensible order of one to three actions.
  • Four As: assess, act, assist, afterwards.
  • Priorities: life before property, duty before comfort, team before ego.
  • Three styles: direct action, through people and channels, root cause and follow-up; mix them.
  • Never run away, never lone-hero, never stay passive, never write vaguely.
  • Authority and ethics: stay honest, respectful and use proper channels.
  • Personal setbacks: accept, diagnose, act.
  • Impossible situations: state the best realistic step calmly.
  • Be consistent with your SDT, TAT and interview.
  • Practise in stages: untimed, 60 seconds, then 30 seconds; review with a mentor.
  • Follow the call-up letter for current timings and rules.
