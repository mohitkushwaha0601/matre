Using this handbook

The psychology tests reward a consistent, honest, action-oriented personality, not clever answers. This handbook explains each test, then gives you raw material (themes, seeds, approaches) to think with, never scripts to memorise.

Read Part I once, then work through one test at a time: read the brief, study the bank, and then practise under timed conditions on pictures, words and situations you have not seen. The sample stories, responses and personas are illustrations of a standard of thinking. If you reproduce them word for word, you will sound rehearsed, and a trained psychologist reads rehearsed material quickly.

The one habit that matters

After every practice set, ask: "Which OLQ did my answer show, and is that a real habit of mine?" If you cannot name an OLQ and a real incident behind it, the answer is decoration.

Scope and caveats

  • Established The five tests, their general order and the OLQ-based assessment are standard across the Army, Navy and Air Force boards.
  • Re-verify Timings, number of items and day-wise scheduling can change by entry, board and year. Confirm against your call-up letter and the official recruitment portals.
  • Interpretation Scoring is not published. Everything about "what assessors look for" is drawn from public preparation knowledge and the published OLQ framework.

Part I · Foundations

The board, the OLQs and the psychologist's lens

SSB structure and the 15 OLQs

The SSB is a five-day, multi-assessor process that judges whether you have the Officer Like Qualities, not whether you have marks or knowledge.

Stage I (Day 1) screens you with the OIR test and the PPDT. Those who pass go to Stage II, which tests you from three angles: psychology (TAT, WAT, SRT, SDT), group testing (GTO tasks) and the personal interview. A final conference of the assessors pools the three views. The psychology day is the one place where you are examined by your own writing, so it is also the place where you can be most consistent.

  • Day 1Reporting, documentation, OIR test and PPDT (screening). Result announced the same day.
  • Day 2Psychology tests in a fixed order: TAT, WAT, SRT, SDT. Interviews may begin from Day 2.
  • Days 3-4GTO tasks (group discussion, planning exercise, outdoor tasks, lecturette, command task, individual obstacles, final group task); interviews continue.
  • Day 5Conference and announcement of the result.

The 15 OLQs are grouped into four factors. Every psychological test is a different window onto the same list.

Read the list as behaviours, not adjectives

"Initiative" means acting without being told; "responsibility" means owning an outcome; "cooperation" means working toward a group goal. Every good answer in this handbook is the OLQ as a concrete action in one sentence.

How the psychologist reads you

The psychologist does not grade each paper in isolation. They look for the same person appearing in all four papers.

Think of your four papers as four photographs of one subject taken from different angles. TAT shows your imagination and how you resolve problems; WAT shows your first instinct; SRT shows what you do under pressure; SDT shows how you see yourself against how others see you. Contradictions between them (a heroic TAT hero but timid SRT answers, a confident SDT but gloomy WAT words) are what draw attention, not any single weak answer.

Principles that hold for every test

  • Be positive and realistic. Optimism with believable effort, not miracles.
  • Be consistent. Same values across all four tests and the interview.
  • Be action-first. The hero or you act; you do not wait or merely hope.
  • Be natural. Write what you would plausibly do, in your own words.
  • Be complete. Finish every item; a finished plain answer beats a polished unfinished one.

Habits that quietly hurt

  • Memorised or copied stories and phrases from coaching notes.
  • A single role for every hero (always a soldier, always a topper).
  • Escapist or passive endings: luck, prayer, "somebody helps".
  • Negative or violent first reactions, even when the situation is hostile.
  • Messy handwriting, cut-outs and unfinished sentences, which signal poor expression and organisation.

What "positive" does not mean

  • It does not mean every problem is solved instantly, or no one ever fails; a hero who fails, learns and tries again shows more determination than one who never struggles.
  • It does not mean pretending to have no weaknesses; the SDT specifically asks for them (see the SDT section).

Part II · Picture tests

PPDT and TAT: what you see, what you make of it

PPDT: brief and strategy

The Picture Perception and Description Test is the second Stage I screening test. It checks how accurately you perceive, how sensibly you imagine, and how you behave when a group must agree on something.

One hazy, ambiguous picture is flashed briefly. You note what you see, write a story, narrate it to your group, and then the group discusses the stories and builds one common story. Assessors watch the writing (perception, imagination, expression) and the discussion (cooperation, influence, speed of decision, liveliness).

  1. View (about 30 seconds)

    Do not start writing a story yet. Collect facts: how many people, approximate age and sex, what they are doing, the mood, the setting.

  2. Observe (about 1 minute)

    Fill the observation boxes (characters, sex, mood). Decide the main character, the problem and a positive outcome while the image is fresh.

  3. Write (about 4 minutes)

    Past in a line, present (what the picture shows) in two or three lines, future (resolution) in the rest. Roughly 8-10 short, clear lines.

  4. Narrate (about 1 minute each)

    Stand, speak clearly, describe the observation first, then the story. No reading from the paper.

  5. Group discussion (a few minutes to ten, depending on the board)

    The group must settle on one common story. Contribute ideas, listen, build consensus.

Re-verify The timings above are the widely reported pattern; confirm yours on the day.

Writing strategy

  • The story must match the picture; do not import a theme that the scene cannot support.
  • Make the main character a positive, young adult doing something ordinary but useful. Show the problem, the action and the result.
  • Mood: even if the picture looks sombre, the story's resolution should be constructive.
  • Describe the hero's action in the present, not only in the future.
  • Read your last line: does it end with an outcome and a quality (perseverance, teamwork)?

Discussion strategy

  • Open with a short, clear contribution. Speak early enough to be noticed but do not rush to dominate.
  • Acknowledge others by name or point ("building on what he said...").
  • If stories clash, extract common elements: characters, setting, mood. Offer a combined version.
  • Invite quiet members in; it shows leadership more than volume.
  • Stay calm if someone is aggressive. Do not argue; restate and move on.
  • Never give up your reasoning without a reason; never fight for your story either.
The usual PPDT failure

Candidates narrate a story that sounds good but ignores what the picture actually shows (wrong number of characters, wrong mood). Perception errors are a fault of observation, which the screening tests directly.

One picture, three plausible stories

Suppose a picture shows several young people around someone lying on the ground. The same observation supports different, equally good stories. Choosing the one that fits the details best, and showing action in each, is the skill.

Story A: Rally mishap

A volunteer faints in the heat at a cleanliness rally. Friends shade him, give water, call a doctor; the organiser reschedules the programme to the evening.

Story B: Sports injury

A teammate collapses during practice. The captain stops the drill, a trained member gives first aid, and the team adds a first-aid routine to practice.

Story C: Road accident

Cyclists see a fallen rider. They clear the road, call for help, accompany him to hospital and inform his family.

Themes to avoid

  • Crime, suicide, accidents ending in death, ghosts, miracles, dreams.
  • Romance as the central story, party politics, and religious or communal conflict.
  • A hero who succeeds with no effort or by luck.
  • A heavily military story attached to a civilian picture.

TAT: brief and strategy

The Thematic Apperception Test is the first Stage II psychology test. It is the longest paper and the richest in information, because a story reveals what you consider a problem, how you solve it and how it ends.

You are shown twelve slides, one at a time: eleven pictures and one blank. Each is shown for about thirty seconds, after which you have about four minutes to write a story. Pictures are deliberately vague so that your reading of them, and the hero you place inside, comes from you. The psychologist reads twelve stories together for recurring patterns: Does the hero act? Do other people matter? How does a crisis end?

The five-part hero frame

Who
One main character (age, role, a believable name), positive in nature.
Setting
Where and when; the link to the picture (what is happening now).
Problem
A real, ordinary difficulty, not a melodramatic one.
Action
What the hero does, with the people around, step by step. This is the OLQ-bearing part.
Result
A believable, positive outcome and what it changed.

Expert strategies

  • Use the 30 seconds to decide, not to scan. Pick the hero, mood and problem during the viewing so all four minutes go to writing.
  • One hero, one problem, one arc. Do not stack three crises in one story.
  • Give the picture its due. Your story's present moment should clearly be the scene shown.
  • Vary heroes and settings across twelve stories. Students, professionals, sportspersons, farmers, volunteers, an occasional uniformed role. Twelve identical heroes suggest a script.
  • Show effort before outcome. The hero thinks, plans, consults, acts, and then succeeds. A turn of luck is not an OLQ.
  • Write in the same tense and keep the pace. About 90-120 words fit in four minutes; finish all twelve even if some are shorter.
  • Handle disturbing pictures honestly. A sad or tense image can be the start of a problem; the story shows how it is handled.

Common errors

  • Describing the picture instead of telling a story.
  • Passive heroes: "he waited", "it was destiny".
  • Stories that end with an accident, death or a sad twist.
  • Over-heroics: the hero single-handedly defeats a gang.
  • The same plot repeated with different names.

Quick self-check after writing

  • Is there a clear hero and a clear action?
  • Does the outcome follow from the action?
  • Would a stranger find this plausible?
  • Is any OLQ visible without being named?

The blank slide

The twelfth slide is empty. You choose the picture in your mind, so the story reflects your own preoccupations more than any other. Take thirty seconds to pick a situation you can write with confidence, and hold to the same standard as the others: a positive hero, real effort, a believable result.

  • A school sports captain who turns a losing team around by reassigning positions and training slots.
  • A student who, when a train is cancelled, organises shared transport for a stranded group.
  • A teenager who starts a study circle for juniors in a village library.
  • A flood-relief volunteer who coordinates supplies with the local authorities.
  • A student who returns to competition after an injury through a staged recovery plan.
  • A first-time lead of a college event after the organiser falls ill, and how the plan is adjusted.
Blank-slide caution

Do not write a dream, a fantasy or an ambition fulfilled by chance. Your story should read like something that could be reported on a news page, with a personality like yours.

Part III · Word Association Test

WAT: the first thought, the right thought

WAT: brief and approaches

The Word Association Test reads your subconscious attitude. Sixty words appear one at a time; you write the first meaningful sentence that comes to mind in the few seconds available.

Each word is shown for about fifteen seconds, and the full test lasts roughly fifteen minutes. Words span positive ideas (courage, team), negative ones (failure, fear), neutral objects, abstract concepts, relations and Service-related terms. There is no time to compose; a lifetime of attitude comes out in your sentence. That is why the only practical preparation is to train your habits of thought, not to memorise sentences.

The golden rule

Write a short, complete, meaningful sentence that expresses a positive, constructive or action-oriented thought about the word. Six to ten words is the target. A response that mirrors the stimulus ("Fear is fear") or a bare synonym tells the psychologist nothing.

Six approaches to any word

1. General truth

A positive statement about the idea. "Failure is the first step to success."

2. Action / resolve

What one should do. "Analyse failure and try again."

3. Role or duty

What a person in a role does. "A leader shares the risk with his team."

4. Personal (only if true)

Your own habit. "I treat failure as feedback and revise my plan."

5. Transformation

For negative words, turn them constructive. "Fear fades with preparation."

6. Contrast

Set the word against its remedy. "Laziness ends where a schedule begins."

Expert strategies

  • Read the word, trust the first positive thought, and write. If nothing comes in two seconds, use a safe, neutral, positive sentence and move on; never leave a stack blank.
  • Mix approaches. If every sentence starts with "I", or every sentence is a proverb, the pattern becomes a signal.
  • Do not assume the word has a hidden trap. "Sex", "kill" or "defeat" are tests of maturity. Answer calmly and without sensationalism.
  • Keep a steady tone. No sarcasm, no humour that mocks people, no pessimism, no preaching.
  • Avoid sentences that reveal poor relationships or fears. "My boss is a bully" is not a WAT sentence.
  • Write legibly, aloud-test your phrasing. A readable sentence is evidence of expression and organisation.

Practice method

  • Use 15-second flashcards of 60 words, written in a notebook; review once a week for patterns (too many "I" sentences, too many negatives, repeated verbs).
  • Replace weak answers with two stronger approaches, and drill the better habit, not the sentence itself.

Part IV · Situation Reaction Test

SRT: what you actually do

SRT: brief and method

The Situation Reaction Test is the closest the psychology day comes to watching you act. Sixty everyday and emergency situations are presented in a booklet, and you write what you would do in each.

The test runs for about thirty minutes, roughly thirty seconds per situation. It cannot be passed by thinking slowly; speed is part of the test. The situations cover family, study, friends, work, travel, public emergencies and ethical pressure. The psychologist reads your sixty answers as one continuous sample of behaviour: how quickly you decide, whom you involve, what you prioritise, and whether you take ownership.

A four-step response habit

Read the problem
Who is at risk? What is time-critical? What is in my control right now?
Act first
Take the immediate practical step yourself (stop, help, inform, secure).
Involve the right people
Call the specific authority or helper the situation needs, not a generic "somebody".
Close the loop
Follow up so the problem is actually resolved: accompany, report, correct, check.

Expert strategies

  • Start with a verb. "Gave first aid...", "Informed the supervisor...", "Called a meeting...". The first word signals initiative.
  • One or two lines, 15-25 words. Name two or three concrete actions, in logical order.
  • Finish all sixty. A short answer to every situation beats a perfect answer to forty.
  • Use your own resources first, then bring in authority where needed (hospital, fire service, police, teacher, senior).
  • Be proportionate and safe. A rescuer who cannot swim does not jump into deep water; he throws a rope and calls help. Good judgement is an OLQ.
  • Balance people and task. In team situations, address the task and the person: fix the problem, protect the relationship.
  • Be honest in ethical situations. Refuse wrongdoing politely, offer a correct alternative, and escalate only if pressure continues.

Weak patterns to avoid

  • "I will pray", "I will wait and see", "I will try."
  • Handing over responsibility: "I will tell someone to handle it."
  • Running away, or choosing fights or revenge.
  • Always the same response ("call the police") regardless of the situation.
  • Conditional answers ("if possible, I might...").

Strong patterns to build

  • Immediate action plus right authority plus follow-up.
  • Calm, courteous tone, even toward wrongdoers.
  • Visible planning and delegation in team situations.
  • A sense of ownership for the outcome.

Use your own idiom

Notice that none of these answers is clever. They are plain, sequenced and safe. That is the standard: write what you would actually do, in the same level of detail, sixty times in thirty minutes.

Part V · Self Description Test

SDT: telling the truth about yourself

SDT: brief and strategy

The Self Description Test asks you to describe yourself through five pairs of eyes. It tests self-awareness: do you see yourself as others do, and do you know what to improve?

You write five short paragraphs in about fifteen minutes, roughly three minutes each: what your parents think of you, what your teachers or employers think, what your friends or colleagues think, what you think of yourself (strengths and weaknesses), and what qualities you would like to develop. Assessors compare the five answers for internal consistency and for fit with your other papers and your Personal Information Questionnaire. The interviewing officer often uses the SDT to open lines of questioning, so every sentence is a hostage to fortune.

The five paragraphs and what each reveals
ParagraphQuestionWhat it reveals
1. ParentsWhat do my parents think of me?Responsibility at home, values, relationships, emotional maturity
2. Teachers or employersWhat do my teachers/employers think of me?Discipline, effort, reliability, learning attitude
3. Friends or colleaguesWhat do my friends/colleagues think of me?Social adjustment, cooperation, liveliness, influence
4. SelfWhat do I think of myself: strengths and weaknesses?Self-awareness, honesty, self-confidence
5. AspirationsWhat kind of person do I want to become?Direction, motivation, willingness to improve

Expert strategies

  • Write only what is true and demonstrable. For every strength, you must be able to tell the interviewer an incident.
  • Be balanced. Four or five good qualities and one or two honest, manageable weaknesses, each with a concrete improvement step.
  • Show the same person from five angles. If your parents say you are responsible, your teachers should not say you are careless, unless you explain how you changed.
  • Prefer specific behaviours over adjectives. "I complete projects before deadlines" is better than "I am disciplined".
  • Keep paragraphs short and written in complete sentences. Four or five lines each; avoid lists unless the paper format asks for them.
  • Make the aspirational paragraph action-based. Name a quality, then the step you are taking to build it.

Avoid

  • "I have no weaknesses" or a weakness disguised as a strength ("I work too hard").
  • Core OLQ failings: cowardice, dishonesty, laziness, uncontrolled anger, habits like addiction.
  • Claims you cannot explain or that contradict your PIQ.
  • Borrowed phrases from coaching notes.

Prefer

  • Mild, real, improvable weaknesses (see the next section).
  • Qualities with evidence: sport, family duties, group work, a failure survived.
  • Others' views stated in their voice: "My teachers feel that..."
  • A forward-looking final paragraph.

SDT: building your own persona

You cannot copy a persona. You can build one from your own evidence. This section gives you the method, the frames and the directions to think in.

  1. Make an incident inventory

    List ten real incidents: academic, sports, family duties, travel, hobbies, a failure, a conflict, a time you led and a time you followed. One line each.

  2. Tag each with OLQs and an observer

    For each incident, name the OLQ it shows and who saw it (parent, teacher, friend). That gives you material for paragraphs 1 to 3.

  3. Select strengths and one or two weaknesses

    Pick strengths that appear in more than one incident. Pick weaknesses that are real, mild and being worked on.

  4. Draft with the frames below

    Write in your own words; keep each paragraph to four or five lines.

  5. Cross-check with all other tests and the PIQ

    Does any statement contradict your TAT heroes, SRT answers, or interview facts? Remove or reconcile.

  6. Prepare to be questioned on each sentence

    For every strength and weakness, have a true example and your improvement action ready.

Paragraph frames (fill with your own facts)

Parents: "My parents feel that I am [trait] and [trait], as I [real behaviour]. They would like me to [mild improvement]."

Teachers: "My teachers consider me [trait] because I [behaviour]. They have advised me to [improvement]."

Paragraphs 4 and 5: self and aspirations

Self: "I believe my strengths are [A] and [B], shown by [incident]. My weakness is [C]; to correct it I [action] and have seen [small result]."

Aspirations: "I want to become a [kind of person] who [quality]. I am building [quality] by [step]."

Choosing weaknesses well

A good weakness is real, minor in the OLQ framework, and visibly improving. Examples that tend to work: slow to speak up in large unfamiliar groups, over-planning, taking time to trust new people, impatience with delays, spending too long on perfecting a task. For each, name a specific corrective step, not a vague wish.

Illustration only: a self-paragraph in one candidate's voice

"I believe my main strengths are persistence and teamwork. I trained for two years for the 800 m and cut my time steadily through regular practice, and as vice-captain I coordinate our school relay team. My weakness is that I sometimes take on too much and then rush. To correct this I now write a weekly plan and ask friends to share the load. I want to improve my public speaking, so I have joined the debate club."

Knowledge toolkit

Analysis, practice and revision

Myths, perspectives and the integrated picture

Few subjects collect as many myths as SSB psychology. Separate what is established, what is a sensible interpretation, and what is folklore.

Common beliefs and a measured reading
BeliefStatusMeasured reading
The tests assess the 15 OLQs, not knowledge or marks.EstablishedThis is the published basis of assessment across the Services.
One weak paper rejects you.InterpretationAssessors pool results from psychology, GTO and interview; patterns and contradictions matter more than a single poor answer.
Memorised "best" stories and sentences work.DisputedStructured practice helps; verbatim memorisation is easily detected and clashes with the interview.
You must be ideal and have no weaknesses.InterpretationThe standard is adequate OLQs plus self-awareness and potential, not perfection.
Every story or hero should be military.InterpretationVariety of heroes and settings suggests range; a few Service-linked stories are fine if the picture supports them.
Exact scoring formulas and cut-offs are known.Re-verifyScoring is not published; treat any claimed cut-offs as unofficial.

View 1: "Be yourself"

Natural, consistent answers survive the interview. Over-engineered personas break under follow-up questions. The only safe strategy is to write what you would truly do or think.

View 2: "Present your best self"

Raw first thoughts may include negativity or sloppiness; structured practice shapes habits so that your first thought is a better one. Preparation is legitimate; pretence is not.

Reconciling the two views

Train the habit, not the sentence. Practise until constructive, action-first thinking is how you actually react. At that point, "being yourself" and "presenting your best self" are the same thing.

Problems arise on the day. Decide in advance how you will handle each; do not improvise in a panic.

  • If one test goes badly, move on completely. Each paper is fresh.

Practice bank: questions

Fifteen practice tasks. Do each under the real time limit and then review for OLQs and consistency.

  1. PPDT: A hazy picture shows four youths bending over a person near a bicycle. Write a four-minute story and name two OLQs it shows.
  2. PPDT discussion: Your group has three stories (a sports injury, a relief effort, a lost wallet). Draft the first two sentences you would speak to merge them.
  3. TAT: An older man hands a file to a young woman in an office. Write a story with the woman as hero and a believable positive outcome.
  4. TAT: Rewrite a story from your earlier practice in which the hero was passive, so that the hero acts and the result follows from the action.
  5. TAT blank slide: Write a story from a real incident you witnessed or took part in, keeping the same positive standard.
  6. WAT: Write three sentences for "Responsibility": one general truth, one action, one personal (true) example.
  7. WAT: Convert each into a constructive sentence: failure, panic, revenge, lazy, defeat, alone.
  8. WAT: Do 15 words at 15 seconds each. Review for repeated sentence patterns and negative tone.
  9. SRT: Your bus is stopped by a landslide at dusk with thirty passengers including elders. Write three different responses.
  10. SRT: A junior asks you to mark his attendance falsely at a camp. Write two responses with different approaches.
  11. SRT: Your key teammate drops out the night before an important event. What do you do?
  12. SDT: Write paragraph 1 (parents) using one real incident and one honest improvement point.
  13. SDT: Identify your weakness, its improvement step and a small result. Explain why it is not a core OLQ failing.
  14. Integration: Which three OLQs do your last five stories and ten SRT answers show most? Which one do they show least? How will you evidence it in real life?
  15. Consistency: List any contradiction between your TAT heroes, SRT answers and SDT. Resolve it by changing the answer or the behaviour.

Debate motions

Use these to practise PPDT-style group discussion: argue both sides, listen, and then build a common position within ten minutes.

  1. Courage matters more than intelligence in a leader.
  2. Failure teaches more than success.
  3. A leader must put the team's welfare above personal ambition.
  4. Honesty should never be compromised, whatever the cost.
  5. Social media has weakened young people's ability to concentrate.
  6. Discipline is more important than talent.
  7. Every young citizen should undertake a period of national service.

Preparation chronology

A twelve-week plan that builds habits first, then speed, then consistency.

  • Weeks 1-2Learn the OLQs, build the incident inventory and take a baseline: one PPDT, three TAT, 20 WAT words, 10 SRT situations and a rough SDT. Note patterns.
  • Weeks 3-4Picture tests: two stories a day on fresh pictures; vary heroes and settings; hold two PPDT group discussions a week.
  • Weeks 5-6WAT and SRT: 30 words and 20 situations a day under time; review for passive or negative habits.
  • Weeks 7-8SDT: draft five paragraphs, test for consistency, get feedback from a parent, a teacher and a friend.
  • Weeks 9-10Full-day mocks: TAT, WAT, SRT and SDT in order, in one sitting, reviewed by a mentor or psychologist.
  • Weeks 11-12Fix only weak spots; sleep, fitness and rest. No new material in the last two days.
  • Test dayEat, sleep, arrive early, write legibly and finish every paper.

Glossary

SSB
Services Selection Board; the multi-day officer selection process of the Indian armed forces.
OLQ
Officer Like Qualities; the 15 traits, in four factors, against which candidates are assessed.
Screening
Stage I (OIR test and PPDT) that decides who continues to Stage II.
OIR
Officer Intelligence Rating test; verbal and non-verbal reasoning in Stage I.
PPDT
Picture Perception and Description Test; a hazy picture, a story and a group discussion.
TAT
Thematic Apperception Test; twelve slides including one blank, each producing a story.
WAT
Word Association Test; sixty words, a sentence for each in about fifteen seconds.
SRT
Situation Reaction Test; sixty situations answered in writing in about thirty minutes.
SDT
Self Description Test; five paragraphs on how parents, teachers, friends and you see yourself, and your aspirations.
PIQ
Personal Information Questionnaire; the form you fill at the start of Stage II, used by assessors alongside the tests.
GTO
Group Testing Officer; conducts the outdoor and group tasks.
Interviewing Officer
The assessor who conducts the personal interview.
Conference
The final meeting where assessors pool their findings and decide the result.
Hero
The main character in a PPDT or TAT story; the vehicle for your OLQs.
Apperception
Interpreting an ambiguous picture in terms of your own thoughts and experience.
Consistency
Agreement across all tests, the PIQ and the interview; the strongest signal of an authentic personality.

Rapid revision sheet

Last-day summary

  • OLQs: 15, in four factors (planning and organising, social adjustment, social effectiveness, dynamic).
  • PPDT: Observe, then write past-present-future in about 8 lines; positive hero; narrate clearly; in discussion, contribute, listen, merge.
  • TAT: 12 slides, about 4 minutes each; one hero, one problem, effort, believable result; vary heroes; never an accident or death ending.
  • WAT: 60 words, about 15 seconds each; short, positive, meaningful sentences; mix general truth, action, role, personal.
  • SRT: 60 situations, about 30 minutes; verb-first, act then involve the right authority then follow up; finish all.
  • SDT: Five paragraphs; true, balanced, evidence-based; one honest weakness with a fix; consistent with PIQ.
  • Overall: Be positive, realistic, consistent, action-first and natural; write legibly and finish every paper.
