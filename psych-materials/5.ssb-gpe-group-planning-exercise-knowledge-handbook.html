Using this handbook

The Group Planning Exercise (GPE) hands a group a map or model, a short story and four or so urgent problems, with too few people, vehicles and hours to do everything. It is not a puzzle with one right answer. It shows how you sort, decide, argue and present when resources run short, and that is exactly what an officer does.

Part I explains the format and what the Group Testing Officer (GTO) reads. Part II gives a priority framework, a way to read the model, a layout for the written plan, rules for the group discussion and the narration, and a worked example. Part III is the exercise bank: 24 exercises, each with three model plans and the OLQs they show. The toolkit closes with debates, scenarios, practice questions and a revision sheet. Related guides: overview for the psychology tests, and WAT, SRT and TAT for the WAT, SRT and TAT.

How the three versions work

Every exercise is set for a group of eight on cycles unless stated. Version 1 keeps one team and tackles problems in strict priority order. Version 2 splits into parallel teams, each with a leader and a meeting point. Version 3 alerts the right authority first, hands over what the group cannot do and keeps a reserve. All three are sound. Which fits best depends on the map, so learn to choose.

Plans, not scripts

Each cell is a compressed plan. A real plan lists assumptions, times and a contingency, and uses the figures in your own narration. The exercises here are invented practice material, not past test content. Use them to learn how a plan is built, and do not memorise.

Part I · Understanding the test

What the exercise is and what it reads

Format and purpose

The GPE is one of the outdoor and indoor tasks conducted by the Group Testing Officer on the group testing days of the SSB, along with the group discussion, progressive and half group tasks, the lecturette, the individual obstacles, the command task and the final group task. Candidates sit in a group of about eight to ten around a model or large sketch of an area. The GTO describes who the group is (often young people on a cycling trip or a camp), the area, the problems at different points, the resources and the rules and time limit.

The test then runs in stages: a short period to study the model and take notes, a written individual plan, a group discussion to agree a common plan, and finally a narration of that plan to the GTO by one member. The GTO watches throughout, and the written sheet, the discussion and the narration are read together.

The OLQs a plan can show

SSB assesses 15 Officer Like Qualities (OLQs) in four factors. The GPE is unusually broad: the written plan mainly shows Factor I, the discussion Factors II and III, and the effort through a long exercise Factor IV.

OLQs by factor, and where the GPE shows them
FactorOLQsWhere it shows
I · Planning and organisingEffective intelligence, reasoning ability, organising ability, power of expressionThe written plan, the priorities, the time check, the narration.
II · Social adjustmentSocial adaptability, cooperation, sense of responsibilityHow you listen, build on others' ideas and own a part of the plan.
III · Social effectivenessInitiative, self-confidence, speed of decision, ability to influence the group, livelinessStarting the discussion, proposing a structure, bringing the group to one plan.
IV · DynamicDetermination, courage, staminaHolding a sound idea under pressure; staying fresh through the final minutes.
Reasoning

"We will rescue everyone" shows only goodwill. "Life first: the drowning child, then the burning hut, then the road; two teams of three, one of two to alert the fire brigade; meet at the school at 4:30" shows reasoning ability, organising ability, sense of responsibility and speed of decision through the structure alone.

The psychology of planning under pressure

The GPE is built on scarcity. The group has fewer people, vehicles and minutes than it needs, so every plan is a set of trade-offs. How you trade, and whether you can explain the trade, is what is being read.

  • Prioritising. Under pressure, weak planners treat all problems as equal or take them in the order given. Strong planners rank them by danger to life, urgency and distance.
  • Working with others. A plan has to be agreed, not imposed. The discussion shows whether you can propose firmly, listen and give way on details without dropping the aim.
  • Realism. The plan must fit the resources and the clock. An elegant plan that needs ten cycles when there are eight is a failed plan.
  • Humility about risk. Officers do not take needless risks with others' lives. A group that sends one weak swimmer into a flood has not understood the job.

No single right answer

Most centres' narrations allow several workable plans, and the GTO is mainly interested in how you think, including whether you can spot and defend a sensible priority. Interpretation Accept a good plan that is not yours; adopting a better idea shows maturity, not weakness.

Part II · Strategy

Building and defending the plan

The priority framework

A five-step loop works for almost any exercise. Run it first in your head while the narration is read, then on paper.

Five steps from narration to plan
StepQuestionOutput
1 · Aim and assumptionsWhat must the group achieve, and what is fixed?One-line aim; assumptions stated openly.
2 · Sort the problemsWhich threaten life, which property, which only convenience? Which are urgent, and how far is each?A ranked list.
3 · Match resourcesHow many people, vehicles and tools; what can each do?Teams and kit allocation.
4 · Sequence or splitOne team in order, or parallel teams? Does the time fit?A route and a time table.
5 · Contingency and reportWhat if a vehicle fails or a task overruns? Who informs whom, and where do we meet?A back-up and a rendezvous.

The priority rules

  • Life before property, property before convenience.
  • Urgent before important within the same class: drowning before a broken arm.
  • When equal, nearer first and tasks on the same route together.
  • Alert authorities early: fire brigade, police, hospital, as the resources allow.
  • Never send one person alone; keep teams of at least two.

Planning speeds

  • Walking: about 4 to 5 km/h.
  • Cycling: about 10 to 12 km/h on rural roads.
  • Motor vehicle on a rough road: about 25 to 30 km/h.
  • Allow time to load, treat and hand over, not only to travel.
  • If the narration gives speeds, use those and no others.

Reading the model and narration

The narration is short and is usually given once, so the first minutes are about capturing facts. Make a quick table as you listen, then check it against the model.

A quick capture table
ProblemWhere and how farDangerWhat it needs

B. Hut fireEast, 3 kmProperty, may threaten lifeBuckets, 3 people, fire brigade
C. Broken bridgeOn the road to the hospitalConvenience, delays other tasksWarning signs, authority
  • Note the rules: the group stays in the area, nobody goes alone, outside help is or is not available.
  • Note the clock: the start time, the finish time, and any sunset or tide.
  • Note the resources and their limits: eight cycles, one rope, one phone, a boat for two.
  • Ask the GTO if a detail is unclear, if the rules permit. A reasonable assumption is allowed when the narration is silent; state it in the plan.
Use the model

Candidates often write a plan that ignores the sketch. A route that crosses a river with no bridge, or a distance guessed instead of read from the scale, shows poor attention. Point to the model when you speak: it proves you used it.

Writing the individual plan

The written plan is short and structured, so that the GTO can read it in a minute and see your thinking. A layout that works:

A plan layout

Aim
Save life, protect property and restore the road before 5:00 pm.
Assumptions
The phone works; the boat holds two; a doctor is at the dispensary.
Priorities
1. Child in the well. 2. Hut fire. 3. Injured cyclist. 4. Broken bridge.
Teams
A (3): well. B (3): fire. C (2): phone and warning.
Timing
2:00 start; A reaches 2:15; B reaches 2:20; C calls 2:10.
Contingency
If A is delayed, B sends one to help; C stays as reserve.
Meet and report
School at 4:30 pm; leader reports to the village head.

Do

  • Write short lines, not paragraphs.
  • Give times and numbers: who, how many, when.
  • Show the reason for each priority in a few words.
  • Include a contingency and a meeting point.
  • Leave room to change when the group agrees a better idea.

Avoid

  • Plans that need more people or cycles than exist.
  • Hero acts: one person doing everything.
  • Ignoring the time limit.
  • Leaving the injured alone or unsupervised.
  • Blaming the narration for a gap; fill it with a stated assumption.

The group discussion

Here the OLQs of Factors II and III are most visible. You are not trying to win; you are trying to help the group reach one good plan inside the time. The best contribution is often structure: "Let us list the problems, rank them, then decide teams."

  • Start early, but not loudly. A short, structured opening beats a long monologue. Waiting for ten minutes and then speaking is too late.
  • Listen and build. "I agree with Rohit on the well first; may I add that Team B needs the rope as well?"
  • Keep time. Remind the group of the clock gently and propose when to decide.
  • Close. Summarise the agreed plan so that the narrator has it clearly.

Reasoning

The GTO cannot credit what you do not say, but volume is not influence. A candidate who speaks four times, each time moving the group forward (a structure, a correction, a summary), shows ability to influence the group, cooperation and self-confidence. A candidate who speaks twenty times to repeat a point shows the reverse.

Presenting the plan

One member narrates the group's plan to the GTO. If you are chosen, the aim is clear, complete and calm. If you are not, you are still assessed in the discussion, so there is no penalty for not narrating.

  1. Opening: "Sir, our group of eight will deal with four problems before 5 pm."
  2. Aim and assumptions: one line each.
  3. Priorities: in order, with a few words on why.
  4. Teams and timings: who goes where and when, pointing to the model.
  5. Contingency: one or two lines.
  6. Close: "That is our plan, sir."

Sample narration (about 120 words)

"Sir, our aim is to save life and protect property in the village before dark. We assume the post-office phone works. Our priorities are: first, the child in the well, as it threatens life; second, the hut fire; third, the injured cyclist; fourth, the broken bridge, which we will warn about. We will split into three teams. Team A of three, with the rope, goes north and reaches the well by 2:15. Team B of three goes east with buckets and reaches the hut by 2:20. Team C of two phones the fire brigade and the hospital, then puts warning signs at the bridge. If Team A is delayed, one member of Team C joins it. We meet at the school at 4:30. That is our plan, sir."

Speak slowly, face the GTO, use "we" for the group plan, and avoid fillers. If you lose the thread, pause and glance at the sheet; a pause reads as control, and rushing reads as panic.

Common traps

  • TrapOrder of narration. Solving the problems in the order they were told, not by priority.
  • TrapUnrealistic plans. Using vehicles, people or phones that do not exist; ignoring the time limit.
  • TrapHero acts. One member to fight a fire alone or swim a flooded river.
  • TrapNo contingency. A plan that fails if one thing goes wrong.
  • TrapDominating the group. Imposing your plan, or talking over others.
  • TrapSilence. Saying nothing in the discussion, then agreeing with everything.
  • TrapWasted minutes. Arguing minor details such as who will lead each team while major tasks are unplanned.
  • TrapIgnoring the injured. Leaving a victim without care or without a plan to reach a doctor.
  • TrapReading from the sheet. A narration that sounds copied, with no eye contact.

Method and a worked example

Practise the loop slowly, then at test speed. Below is one exercise worked end to end.

Worked example: the hill hamlet

A group of eight on cycles, at the school (point S) at 2:00 pm, must finish by 5:00 pm. Problems: (1) a boy has fallen into a dry well, 2 km north; (2) a hut is on fire, 3 km east; (3) a cyclist has a broken leg, 4 km south; (4) the bridge on the road to the hospital, 5 km west, is washed away. Resources: eight cycles, one rope, buckets in the school, a first-aid box, a phone at the post office beside the school. No outside help unless the phone is used.

Aim
Save lives first, then property, and inform authorities, within three hours.
Sort
Life: well, broken leg, hut (may hold people). Property: hut. Convenience: bridge.
Resources
One rope: used at the well. One first-aid box: with the leg. Phone: 5 minutes, two people.
Teams
A (3): well with the rope. B (3): hut with buckets. C (2): phone, then leg with first aid and a hand-cart.
Timing
C calls fire brigade, hospital and the panchayat by 2:10; A at the well 2:15; B at the hut 2:20; C at the leg 2:35.
Contingency
When A finishes, it joins C; B stays until the brigade arrives. The bridge is left to the panchayat, with warning signs put by A on return.
Meet
School at 4:30 pm; leader reports to the village head.
Reasoning

The bridge is the largest problem on the map but the least urgent, so the plan hands it over instead of chasing it. That is the central skill: ranking by danger, not by size. The plan shows reasoning ability (ranking), organising ability (teams, timings), sense of responsibility (informing authority) and cooperation (A joins C when free). A weaker plan sends all eight to the bridge because it looks hardest.

Reasoning: "the washed-out bridge"

A washed-out bridge is dramatic and, in all six village exercises, an obvious distraction. The strong plan hands it over to the panchayat with a warning sign and spends the group on people. That choice shows reasoning ability and sense of responsibility: the group is judged on lives protected, not on obstacles removed.

It can feel braver to push on. The strong plan sits out the storm together and moves afterwards. That shows reasoning ability and sense of responsibility: an officer does not risk the group to look decisive, but takes action when the situation allows.

It is tempting to treat a town scene as a group heroics task. The strong plan raises the alarm first, secures the scene, helps where it is trained, and hands over. That shows sense of responsibility and reasoning ability: the group is useful, not a second emergency.

School, camp and events

Here the group often carries a duty of care: children, cadets, a crowd. The plan must protect the many while treating the few, and keep a headcount. Group of eight unless stated.

Reasoning: "treat the water first"

The contaminated tank is the quietest problem in exercise 24 and the one that can hurt the most people. The strong plan deals with the widest hazard first, then the visible ones. It shows reasoning ability and effective intelligence: urgency is about numbers at risk, not about noise.

Myths and debates

GPE advice is plentiful and often contradictory. Tags separate what is well supported from opinion.

  • EstablishedThe assessment is of qualities, not of the plan. The GTO reads the way you think and work with others, not whether your plan matches a key.
  • EstablishedLife comes before property. This priority is accepted across rescue practice and is the safest default.
  • Interpretation"Speak first to score." Opening the discussion helps, but only if what you say adds structure. A weak opening hurts more than a late, sound one.
  • DisputedShould you nominate yourself to narrate? Some mentors say yes if prepared; others say a forced bid looks eager. Offer if you can do it well; support the group's choice if not.
  • DisputedOne team or split. A single team is simpler; splitting saves time but needs leaders and communication. Choose by distance and time, not by habit.
  • DisputedStating assumptions. Some think they look like loopholes; most agree that clear, modest assumptions show maturity.
  • InterpretationMemorised plans. They sound alike and often miss the specific map.
  • Re-verifyTest details. Group size, timings, the number of problems and the rules differ; follow your Group Testing Officer.
Lesser-known fact

The strongest contributions in a discussion are often small and exact: a corrected distance, a missed resource, a conflict between two teams' timings. Spotting these shows careful attention, which the whole test rewards.

Scenarios and trade-offs

Awkward moments arise in the real exercise. The options below show what is available and what each trades.

Your mind goes blank

  • Fall back on the five steps: aim, sort, match, sequence, contingency.
  • Write the problems and their danger first.
  • Trade-off: a plain ranked list over an empty sheet.

The group is chaotic

  • Propose a short structure: list, rank, split.
  • Ask one person to note the points on paper.
  • Trade-off: calm direction over loud argument.

Your plan differs from the group's

  • Explain once with the reason, then test it against the rules.
  • Accept the group's plan if it is sound, and make it work.
  • Trade-off: group unity over personal credit.

Time is nearly over

  • Propose a decision, then agree the narrator.
  • Give a clear summary of teams and times.
  • Trade-off: a complete, simple plan over a perfect, unfinished one.

Preparation timeline

A six-week plan, then the roughly 40 to 50 minutes of a single exercise. Adjust to your calendar.

  • Week 1Read the 15 OLQs and the five-step framework; plan two exercises from this book untimed.
  • Week 2Practise capturing problems, distances and resources in a table from a read-aloud narration.
  • Week 3Write plans in ten minutes; check each for ranking, teams, timings and contingency.
  • Week 4Practise group discussion with friends; rotate the roles of starter, builder and timekeeper.
  • Week 5Practise three-minute narrations; record and review.
  • Week 6Two full mocks with feedback; rest before the test and do not cram new plans.
  • NarrationListen; capture problems, places, resources, rules and the clock.
  • About 10 minWrite the individual plan: aim, priorities, teams, timings, contingency.
  • 15-20 minDiscuss: propose a structure, listen, build, keep time, summarise.
  • 3-5 minNarrate the common plan clearly and calmly if chosen.

Glossary

GPE
Group Planning Exercise: planning, in a group, the handling of several problems with limited resources.
GTO
Group Testing Officer: the officer who conducts and assesses the group tasks.
OLQ
Officer Like Quality: one of 15 traits assessed at SSB.
Model
The map, sketch or miniature of the area on which the problems are described.
Narration
The spoken description of the situation, problems, resources and rules; also the final presentation of the plan.
Individual plan
The plan each candidate writes before the discussion.
Common plan
The single plan agreed by the group in discussion.
Priority
The rank of a problem by danger to life, urgency and distance.
Assumption
A reasonable, stated fact that fills a gap in the narration.
Resource
People, vehicles, tools and time available to the group.
Constraint
A limit on the plan, such as a time limit or a rule.
Contingency
A back-up plan if something goes wrong.
Rendezvous
The agreed place and time for the teams to meet.
Reserve
A small team held back to cover gaps or surprises.
Hand-over
Transferring a task to the proper authority, with information.
Consensus
General agreement reached in the group, without one person imposing.

Rapid revision sheet

One-page summary

  • GPE: a model, a narration, about four problems, limited resources; individual plan, group discussion, narration.
  • The GTO reads how you think and work with others, not a fixed answer.
  • Five steps: aim and assumptions, sort, match resources, sequence or split, contingency and report.
  • Priorities: life before property before convenience; urgent before important; nearer first.
  • Alert authorities early and hand over what the group cannot do.
  • Never send one person alone; keep a reserve and a rendezvous.
  • Use the model, the scale and the clock; state assumptions openly.
  • Discussion: structure, listen, build, keep time, accept better ideas.
  • Narration: aim, priorities, teams, timings, contingency, calm close.
  • Three versions of any exercise: one team in order, parallel teams, alert and hand over.
  • Avoid hero acts, unrealistic plans, silence and domination.
  • Follow your call-up letter and the GTO's instructions for current rules and timings.
