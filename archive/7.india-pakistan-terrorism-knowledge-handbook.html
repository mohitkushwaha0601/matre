Using this handbook

This is a reference document, not a narrative. Read Section 2 (the answer framework), Section 3 (the snapshot) and Section 51 (the revision sheet) first; then go to whatever the board is likely to press you on. Of all the subjects in this series, this is the one on which candidates are most likely to be emotionally fluent and analytically poor. The premium on calm is correspondingly higher.

Four labels used throughout

On this topic more than any other, casualty numbers, intentions and attributions are contested by design. Four tags are used:

Established Documented and broadly agreed — treaty texts, dates, official statements, convictions in court, force dispositions acknowledged by both sides.

Interpretation A reasonable reading that serious analysts dispute. Adopt one if you like, but say you are adopting one.

Disputed Figures or claims where the two governments assert different facts, or where evidence is deliberately withheld — enemy casualties, aircraft losses, damage assessments, intrusion statistics.

Re-verify Fast-moving current affairs. Anything after 2024 in this document must be re-checked against the Ministry of External Affairs, Ministry of Defence and Press Information Bureau in the week before your interview.

The single most useful habit

Separate the state, the army, the terrorist groups and the people of Pakistan in every sentence you speak. “The Pakistan Army's strategic calculus,” not “the Pakistanis.” This is not politeness for its own sake — it is analytical accuracy, because the four have genuinely different interests, and a candidate who collapses them into one shows he has not understood the problem. It also, incidentally, is how Indian official statements are worded, which is a useful thing for an assessor to notice.

What this handbook deliberately does

  • Treats terrorism as a policy instrument, not as madness. Cross-border terrorism directed at India has been cheap, deniable and — from the sponsor's point of view — rational for three decades. You cannot counter an instrument you insist on calling irrational.
  • Distinguishes the Kashmir dispute from the terrorism problem. They interlock but are not the same thing. A settlement of one would not automatically dissolve the other, and saying so is a mark of seriousness.
  • Gives the Pakistani case as Rawalpindi and Islamabad make it, so that you can answer it rather than caricature it.
  • Refuses the two lazy poles. Neither “nothing can be done, they only understand force” nor “just talk and it will resolve itself” survives contact with the record. Both are positions you will hear in a GD; knowing why each fails is worth more than holding either.
  • Keeps the Kashmiri civilian in the frame. Every counter-terrorism operation happens in someone's village. Indian doctrine itself says the population is the centre of gravity. Say so.
  • Presents India's options neutrally. Section 45 lays out choices and their costs. It does not tell you which to prefer, and neither should you in a GD — argue a position, but name its price.
  • Does not repeat unverified casualty claims from either side. Where a number is contested it is tagged and a range is given. In an interview, “the verified figure is X; the claimed figure is Y; I would not assert Y” is a strong answer, not a weak one.

A word on maps. Every schematic in this document is a teaching diagram, not a survey map. Relative positions are approximate and deliberately simplified. Do not reproduce them as authoritative depictions of any boundary. For official depictions, use Survey of India material. India's official position is that the whole of the former princely state of Jammu and Kashmir, including the territory under Pakistani and Chinese occupation, is an integral part of India; the diagrams here show lines of control, which are a different thing from claimed boundaries and are labelled as such.

A word on vocabulary. Terminology is itself contested here, and the words you choose will be heard. This handbook uses the standard Indian official vocabulary — Pakistan-occupied Jammu and Kashmir (PoJK), cross-border terrorism, Line of Control — and notes the alternative usage where it matters for comprehension, not for endorsement. Use Indian official terminology in your interview. Knowing what the other side calls the same thing is preparation; using it is confusion.

The nine-step answer framework

One structure answers almost every question on this subject. Learn the ladder, not the essays. Whether a board member asks about Article 370, the Indus Waters Treaty, Balakot or FATF, he is asking the same nine questions in a different order — and a candidate who climbs the ladder audibly sounds organised even on a topic he has never seen before.

  1. What?

    State the thing itself in one or two sentences — what, where, when, who. No adjectives, no verdict, no history.

    Trap: opening with 1947. The board has not yet confirmed that you know what the subject is. Establish that in fifteen seconds and everything afterwards is believed.

  2. Why?

    Why is this happening, and why now? Separate the trigger from the driver — the incident that set it off from the interest that made it worth setting off.

    Trap: treating the trigger as the cause. Kargil's trigger was a snow-season vacation of posts; its driver was a deliberate plan to internationalise Kashmir by making the LoC untenable.

  3. Background?

    The minimum history that makes step 2 intelligible. Three dates, not thirty. If a date does not change the listener's understanding of the present, leave it out.

    Trap: this is where candidates drown, and on this topic they drown faster than on any other, because everyone knows a little 1947. Discipline here is the single clearest marker of a prepared mind.

  4. India's interest?

    Name the interest specifically: territorial integrity, deterrence credibility, the safety of citizens, water, the precedent set, the cost of the deployment, reputation, or the freedom to grow economically without interruption. Say which one, and say whether it is vital, important or merely desirable.

    Trap: the phrase “Kashmir is an integral part of India.” True and official — but it is a statement of position, not an argument. Say it once, then explain what follows from it.

  5. Security implication?

    What does it change on the ground — infiltration rates, force levels, escalation thresholds, warning time, the two-front calculation, the risk to civilians, the risk to the deterrent?

    Trap: stopping at “it is a threat to our security.” Say which security, where, and how soon.

  6. Economic implication?

    Cost of the deployment, cost of a disrupted Kashmir economy, tourism, the opportunity cost of an army tied down in internal security, trade forgone, investor perception of a “nuclear flashpoint,” and the cost to Pakistan of its own choices.

    Trap: skipping this rung because the topic looks military. The most powerful long-run Indian instrument against Pakistan is the growth differential, and that is an economic fact, not a military one.

  7. Argument for?

    The strongest case for the policy, action or position under discussion — stated as its own advocates would state it, not as a straw man.

    Trap: arguing for the side you personally hold and against a caricature of the other. Assessors notice this immediately.

  8. Argument against?

    The strongest case against, stated with equal force. If you cannot make it convincingly, you do not yet understand the question.

    Trap: omitting this rung because the topic is emotive and you fear sounding disloyal. It has the opposite effect — conceding the best opposing point and then answering it is what makes a position sound earned.

  9. What should India do?

    A recommendation with three properties: it is sequenced (first this, then that), it is affordable, and it names its own cost. Then stop talking.

    Trap: “India should give a befitting reply.” That is a sentiment, not a policy, and every board has heard it two hundred times.

Worked example

What
Re-verify On 7 May 2025 India conducted Operation Sindoor, striking terrorist infrastructure at nine locations in Pakistan and Pakistan-occupied Jammu and Kashmir, in response to the killing of 26 civilians at Baisaran meadow, Pahalgam, on 22 April 2025. India described the strikes as “focused, measured and non-escalatory” and directed at terrorist infrastructure, not at Pakistani military or civilian targets. Exchanges of fire, drone and missile activity followed over the succeeding days until an understanding to stop firing was reached on 10 May 2025.
Why
Trigger: the Pahalgam massacre, in which tourists were reportedly identified by religion before being shot — an act calculated to produce maximum communal effect. Driver: a standing Indian decision, dating from 2016 and hardened in 2019, that mass-casualty terrorism will be answered by visible kinetic action rather than by diplomatic protest, in order to raise the cost to the sponsor and to restore a deterrence that a decade of restraint had eroded.
Background
Three dates are enough. 2008 — after 26/11 India chose not to strike, and the absence of a response became part of the adversary's planning assumption. 2016 — after Uri, India conducted and publicised ground raids across the LoC. 2019 — after Pulwama, India struck across the international boundary at Balakot with air power, breaching a threshold that had held since 1971.
India's interest
Three, in descending order of vitality: restoring the credibility of deterrence against mass-casualty terrorism; protecting the recovery of J&K's tourism-dependent economy, which the attack was designed to destroy; and denying the precedent that an attack of that scale can be absorbed without consequence.
Security
Demonstrated capability for stand-off, multi-domain strikes and, on the Indian account, the effectiveness of layered air defence against drone and missile retaliation. Also demonstrated the opposite: that each round begins at a higher rung than the last, that both sides now employ armed drones at scale, and that the window between first strike and mutual escalation is measured in hours, not days. Disputed Loss claims on both sides — aircraft, air defence systems, damage assessments — remain contested; do not assert them.
Economic
Airspace closures, cancelled flights and a sharp short-term hit to J&K tourism in peak season. Markets recovered quickly, which itself carries a lesson: the Indian economy is now large and diversified enough that a short exchange does not move it much — an asymmetry that did not exist in 1999 and which is, in the long run, India's strongest card.
For
The case that it was right: restraint after 2008 was read as weakness and produced more attacks, not fewer; a state that cannot impose costs invites repetition; the strikes were narrowly targeted on terrorist infrastructure and avoided military and civilian targets, keeping the action legally and diplomatically defensible under the “unwilling or unable” doctrine; and India controlled escalation and stopped.
Against
The case for caution: each round normalises a higher threshold, and the ceiling is nuclear; strike-and-stop does not dismantle the infrastructure, which is human and ideological before it is physical; the deterrent effect is unproven — Pulwama followed Uri, Pahalgam followed Balakot; and every crisis invites exactly the third-party mediation that India's Simla position exists to prevent.
India should
Keep the response calibrated and pre-planned rather than improvised under public pressure; invest more heavily in the unglamorous half — intelligence penetration, counter-drone systems, the NIA's evidentiary chain and FATF-standard financial pressure — because that is what actually reduces attacks; prosecute the diplomatic case with evidence in the weeks after, not only in the hours; and keep the Kashmiri civilian economy insulated, because the attack's real target was tourism. The cost, stated honestly: a declared policy of retaliation transfers the timing of the next crisis to the adversary, and India must therefore be ready to fight one at a moment of his choosing, not ours.
Three rules that make the ladder work
  • Never skip rung 8. A position that has survived its own best counter-argument is the only kind worth holding, and saying so out loud is what distinguishes conviction from noise.
  • Rung 9 must cost something. If your recommendation has no trade-off, you have not made one.
  • On this topic, add a fourth: never quote an enemy casualty figure you cannot source. The moment you say “we killed 300 terrorists” without a caveat, an informed board member knows exactly how much of your knowledge came from television.

The four words that created Siachen

The 1949 Karachi Agreement described the ceasefire line as running up to map reference NJ 9842 and “thence north to the glaciers.” Nobody thought it mattered: nobody was going to fight at 20,000 feet. Pakistan later interpreted the line as running north-east from NJ9842 to the Karakoram Pass, which would have placed the whole Siachen glacier on its side; India interpreted it as running north along the Saltoro watershed. In 1984, India moved first and occupied the Saltoro passes. The whole of the world's highest battlefield exists because a 1949 ceasefire agreement used a phrase instead of a coordinate. If you remember one thing from this section, remember that — it is the single most economical illustration available of why ambiguity in a military agreement is not diplomacy, it is a deferred casualty list.

The four crossing points you should be able to name

The LoC is not entirely sealed. Two cross-LoC travel and trade points were opened as confidence-building measures — Salamabad / Kaman Post on the Srinagar-Muzaffarabad axis (bus service from April 2005) and Chakan-da-Bagh on the Poonch-Rawalakot axis (from 2006). Cross-LoC trade was suspended by India in April 2019 on the ground that the route was being used for weapons, narcotics and counterfeit currency. Separately, the Kartarpur Corridor, opened on 9 November 2019, crosses the international boundary in Punjab and allows visa-free pilgrim access to Gurdwara Darbar Sahib — remarkably, it has generally continued to operate through subsequent crises. Part I

Partition, accession and the first war

The idea, Partition and the princely states

Pakistan was not a border dispute that hardened into an ideology. It was an ideology that produced a border — and then a lawyer who had never been to India drew that border in ten weeks, across a map of 562 princely states whose future was formally left to their rulers. That sequence explains almost every India-Pakistan quarrel since.

Where the two-nation theory came from

The proposition that the Muslims of the subcontinent constituted not a minority but a separate nation had a long incubation: Sir Syed Ahmad Khan's vocabulary of Muslim separateness; the Morley-Minto reforms of 1909, which hardwired communal identity into the constitution through separate electorates; Iqbal's Allahabad address of 1929; and the name itself, coined by Choudhry Rahmat Ali in a Cambridge pamphlet of 1933. The Lahore Resolution of 23 March 1940 gave the demand formal shape — and note two things candidates routinely get wrong: it spoke of independent states in the plural, and it did not specify boundaries, which is precisely why a Muslim-majority princely state like Kashmir was left structurally unresolved.

The argument that has never gone away — and the three that answer it

If Muslims and Hindus are two nations, a Muslim-majority territory that remains in India is an anomaly requiring correction, and a secular India in which more Muslims live than in Pakistan is an existential refutation. Interpretation Many scholars on both sides argue this, rather than water or land or strategic depth, is the deepest driver of the dispute's persistence — it explains why Pakistan has accepted enormous costs for territory of limited economic value. But offer it as the sole explanation and you will be pushed, so know the three alternatives: the elite-interest reading (Ayesha Jalal — Jinnah used the demand as a bargaining counter for parity within a loose federation and was outmanoeuvred into a “moth-eaten” state he did not want); the institutional reading (Pakistan inherited a disproportionate share of the army and almost none of the administrative capital, so army dominance — and the permanent enemy that justifies it — was near-structural); and the 1971 reading (a Muslim-majority territory seceding from a Muslim state on linguistic and economic grounds is the strongest empirical refutation of the theory, and Pakistani strategic thought has never fully absorbed it).

How to use this in a GD without giving offence

“I would argue the dispute is ideological before it is territorial — which is why economic logic has not resolved it. But I would add immediately that this cuts two ways: if the dispute is ideological, then the most powerful Indian response is not military at all. It is to make Indian secular democracy work so visibly that the founding premise looks absurd. That is a slower instrument than an airstrike, and a more decisive one.” That formulation is analytically strong, entirely loyal, and impossible to characterise as communal.

The mechanics

  • 16 May 1946Cabinet Mission Plan — a three-tier federation preserving a united India; accepted, then effectively abandoned by all sides
  • 16 Aug 1946Direct Action Day; the Great Calcutta Killings. The point at which Partition became, in practice, unavoidable
  • 20 Feb 1947Attlee announces British withdrawal by June 1948; Mountbatten appointed
  • 3 June 1947Mountbatten Plan — partition of Punjab and Bengal, date advanced to 15 August 1947
  • 18 July 1947Indian Independence Act receives royal assent. Paramountcy over the princely states lapses — it does not devolve to either dominion
  • 17 Aug 1947Radcliffe Award published — two days after independence. Cyril Radcliffe had arrived on 8 July and had roughly five weeks

Radcliffe's terms of reference were to divide on the basis of contiguous majority areas, while taking into account “other factors.” Those four words did the work. The most consequential exercise of them was the award of the Gurdaspur district, with its Muslim plurality, largely to India — which gave India its only practicable road access from Pathankot into Jammu and Kashmir. Disputed Pakistani historiography treats this as a decisive act of collusion that made Indian intervention in Kashmir possible; Indian and most British accounts treat it as defensible on the boundary commission's own criteria, noting the Muslim majority in Gurdaspur district was thin and that the tehsil-level distribution supported the award. What is not in dispute is the consequence: without the Pathankot link there is no road to Jammu, and without a road to Jammu the events of October 1947 unfold very differently.

The princely states: the rule and the exceptions

The Indian Independence Act left each of the 562 princely states technically free to accede to India, accede to Pakistan, or remain independent. Sardar Patel and V. P. Menon, through the States Department, secured accession of the overwhelming majority before 15 August by an instrument that ceded only three subjects — defence, external affairs and communications. Three cases were not settled that way, and it is worth setting them side by side, because the comparison is the single most useful thing in an argument about Kashmir.

The Junagadh argument

This is the single most effective factual point available to an Indian candidate on this topic, and very few use it. “Sir, Pakistan's position is that a ruler's accession cannot override the wishes of a Muslim-majority population. But in Junagadh, Pakistan accepted the accession of a Muslim ruler over an overwhelmingly Hindu population with no territorial contiguity — and India was the party that demanded a plebiscite, held one, and abided by it. Both states cannot have it both ways, and only one of them actually held the referendum it demanded.” Deliver it calmly, as a point of law, not as a taunt.

The human cost, which is not a footnote

Disputed Estimates of deaths during Partition range from roughly 200,000 to 2 million; the scholarly mid-range is commonly cited as around one million. Somewhere between 12 and 20 million people were displaced — the largest forced migration in recorded history. Tens of thousands of women were abducted on both sides; the two governments ran a joint recovery operation into the 1950s under the Abducted Persons (Recovery and Restoration) Act. This matters strategically as well as morally: the reservoir of grievance created in 1947 was the recruiting ground for everything that followed, and the memory of it on both sides is why leaders on both sides have always found it easier to escalate than to compromise.

Kashmir 1947: the accession

Twenty-two days in October 1947 determined the next eight decades. Get this sequence right and you can answer almost any legal question the board puts to you; get it wrong and every subsequent argument collapses.

The two days at Baramulla

The raiders reached Baramulla, thirty-five miles from Srinagar, on 24 October, with the airfield effectively undefended. They stopped to loot. Interpretation Almost every historian of the campaign, Indian and Western alike, concludes that those two days lost Pakistan the Valley: had the column pressed on, Srinagar airfield would have fallen before a single Indian aircraft landed, and there would have been nowhere to land. The local NC worker Maqbool Sherwani, who misdirected the raiders about Indian troop strength and was tortured and shot for it, is part of the same story. Kashmir's accession was made physically enforceable by indiscipline on one side and one young man's courage on the other.

The legal question, argued honestly

The Indian case

  • The Instrument of Accession was executed by the legally competent authority under the Indian Independence Act 1947 — the ruler — in terms identical to those used by every other acceding state. No state's accession was made conditional on a referendum.
  • India intervened only after accession, at the request of the lawful government, which is the textbook basis for lawful assistance.
  • Pakistan was in breach of the Standstill Agreement it had itself signed, both through the economic blockade and through the invasion launched from its territory.
  • Mountbatten's reference to the people's wishes was in a covering letter of acceptance, not in the Instrument; it expressed a policy preference, not a condition precedent.
  • The J&K Constituent Assembly ratified the accession in 1954 and the state's own Constitution of 1957 declared J&K an integral part of India. The plebiscite offer, in any case, was conditional on a Pakistani withdrawal that never occurred.

The Pakistani case, as Pakistan makes it

  • The Maharaja had lost effective control of much of his state before he signed, and an accession signed under those conditions cannot bind the population.
  • The principle applied at Partition was contiguity and majority; on that principle a Muslim-majority state adjoining Pakistan should have gone to Pakistan. India applied that principle at Junagadh and abandoned it at Kashmir.
  • India itself took the matter to the United Nations on 1 January 1948 and accepted resolutions envisaging a plebiscite; a state cannot invoke an international process and then treat the question as closed.
  • Nehru repeatedly and publicly committed to a reference to the people, including in broadcasts and in Parliament.
  • The 1954 Constituent Assembly ratification was produced by an assembly elected under conditions that excluded the plebiscite option and much of the opposition.

What an assessor is testing when he raises this

Not whether you will defend India — he assumes that. He is testing whether you know that the strongest Indian argument is legal and procedural (competent authority, identical instrument, prior aggression, unfulfilled condition precedent) rather than emotional, and whether you can state the opposing case without flinching. The weakest possible answer is volume. The strongest is: “Sir, the Indian case rests on four points of law, and I think the strongest single one is that the UN resolutions India accepted were sequenced — Pakistan was to withdraw first. That step was never taken, so the condition on which the rest depended never arose. I would argue that point rather than any other.”

The first war, 1947-48

Fourteen months of fighting across terrain that had no roads, in which the Indian Army improvised an airlift, a tank assault at 11,500 feet and a year-long defence of a besieged town, and at the end of which a line was drawn that is still there.

The five things this war established
  • Air mobility decides mountain campaigns. The Srinagar airlift and the Poonch air maintenance were the war. Every subsequent Indian investment in tactical airlift descends from that lesson.
  • Deniable forces first, regulars later. The 1947 pattern — tribal irregulars with regular support, disowned at the diplomatic level — is the template repeated in 1965 (Gibraltar) and 1999 (Kargil). Recognising the pattern is the point.
  • Roads are strategy. India held what it could reach. Everything beyond the road-head — Gilgit, Skardu — was lost.
  • Ceasefires freeze lines. The line where the guns stopped on 1 January 1949 is, with modifications, still the line today. Seventy-seven years later, the military status quo of a single night remains the political reality.
  • Referral to the UN transferred the initiative. Whatever one concludes about the decision, its effect was to convert a question India considered settled into an item on an international agenda.

The ceasefire and the Karachi Agreement

India referred the matter to the UN Security Council on 1 January 1948 under Article 35 of the Charter, complaining of Pakistani aggression. The Council established the United Nations Commission for India and Pakistan (UNCIP). A ceasefire took effect from one minute before midnight on 1 January 1949. The Karachi Agreement of 27 July 1949, signed by military representatives under UNCIP auspices, delineated the Ceasefire Line in detail on the ground — and ended it at NJ9842 with the phrase “thence north to the glaciers.”

The United Nations Military Observer Group in India and Pakistan (UNMOGIP) was deployed to supervise. Established It still exists. India's position since Simla is that UNMOGIP has been overtaken by the 1972 bilateral agreement and has no role; Pakistan continues to lodge complaints with it. India has not formally expelled it but has progressively restricted its function.

Casualties, 1947-48

Disputed Indian official figures for the campaign are commonly given as roughly 1,100 killed and 3,150 wounded; Pakistani figures are of a similar order but differently compiled and include irregulars whose numbers were never recorded. Civilian deaths in the Valley, Jammu, Mirpur and Poonch — including large-scale communal massacres on both sides of the eventual line, of which the Mirpur killings of November 1947 and the violence in Jammu district are the most documented — are not reliably known. Treat all of these as ranges.

The UN, the ceasefire line and the plebiscite

The plebiscite question is the one on which candidates most often stumble, because the popular version of it — “India promised a plebiscite and never held it” — is a compressed half-truth. The full version is more favourable to India and far more impressive to state.

What the resolutions actually said

The four facts that settle this argument
  1. The resolutions were sequential, and the first step was Pakistan's. Withdrawal of tribesmen and Pakistani nationals was step one. It was never carried out. Every subsequent obligation, including India's, was expressly conditional on it.
  2. They were passed under Chapter VI, not Chapter VII. They are recommendations for pacific settlement, not binding enforcement decisions. This is a point of black-letter Charter law and it is worth knowing precisely.
  3. The territorial basis has changed beyond restoration. Pakistan ceded the Shaksgam Tract to China in 1963; demographic change in Gilgit-Baltistan and PoJK over seventy-five years means the electorate contemplated in 1948 no longer exists.
  4. Simla superseded the framework. In 1972 both states agreed to settle differences bilaterally by peaceful means. India's consistent position since is that this replaced the UN track; Pakistan's position is that a bilateral agreement cannot extinguish a Security Council process.

The honest complications

A candidate who gives only the four points above will be pushed, and should be ready:

  • Nehru did make public commitments. In his telegram to Liaquat Ali Khan of 27 October 1947, in All India Radio broadcasts, and in Parliament, he spoke of a reference to the people of Kashmir. He also consistently tied them to the removal of the invaders. Both halves are true and a serious answer includes both.
  • India's own position evolved. By the mid-1950s, with Pakistan's entry into SEATO and CENTO and the receipt of American military aid, India argued that the strategic premises of the 1948 offer had been altered. Whether that is a legitimate rebus sic stantibus argument or a convenient one is a genuine question, and saying so does not weaken your position — it strengthens it, because it shows you are not reciting.
  • A plebiscite was never the only option contemplated. The Dixon Plan of 1950 — Owen Dixon, the Australian jurist appointed UN Representative — proposed regional plebiscites or partition along regional lines, with the Valley alone voting. Neither side accepted it. It is the most-cited “road not taken” in the literature and is worth knowing by name.
The best available answer to “why no plebiscite?”

“Sir, because the condition precedent was never met. The resolutions were sequential: Pakistan was to withdraw its nationals and tribesmen first, India was to reduce forces second, and the plebiscite came third. Step one never happened, so the offer lapsed on its own terms. Beyond that, the resolutions were Chapter VI recommendations rather than binding decisions; the territory itself has since been altered by the 1963 cession to China; and after Simla in 1972 both countries undertook to settle the matter bilaterally. I would also add, in fairness, that Prime Minister Nehru did make public statements about a reference to the people — but he always tied them to the withdrawal, and that is the part usually left out of the quotation.” That is a complete, legally literate, self-aware answer in about forty-five seconds.

Pakistan's founding dilemma and its army

You cannot predict a state's behaviour without understanding who inside it makes decisions. In Pakistan, the answer has been unusually stable for seventy-five years, and it is the most important single fact for anyone trying to anticipate the next crisis.

The inheritance

  • Territorially split. Two wings separated by 1,600 km of India, with different languages, cuisines, climates and political cultures, bound only by religion. The eastern wing held the majority of the population; the western wing held the capital, the army and the bureaucracy.
  • Militarily over-endowed, fiscally under-endowed. Pakistan received about a third of the British Indian Army's manpower and a disproportionate share of its recruited “martial” classes, but roughly a sixth of the revenue base and almost none of the ordnance factories — sixteen of seventeen lay in India.
  • Administratively thin. A new capital, an improvised civil service, and a Muslim League with no organised presence in much of the territory it now governed.
  • Founder-dependent. Jinnah died on 11 September 1948, thirteen months after independence; Liaquat Ali Khan was assassinated on 16 October 1951. Pakistan lost both its founding figures within four years, before a constitution existed. India's first constitution came in 1950; Pakistan's first came in 1956 and was abrogated in 1958.

How the army became the state

  • 1951Rawalpindi Conspiracy Case — the first attempted military coup, foiled; Gen Ayub Khan becomes the first Pakistani C-in-C in 1951
  • 1954-55US Mutual Defense Assistance Agreement; Pakistan joins SEATO and the Baghdad Pact/CENTO. Alliance money and weapons flow to the army, not to the civil state
  • 1958First coup. Iskander Mirza declares martial law; Ayub Khan removes him within three weeks and rules until 1969
  • 1965 · 1971Two wars initiated or precipitated under military-dominated decision-making; the second costs Pakistan half its population
  • 1977-88Zia-ul-Haq's coup and eleven years of Islamisation — Hudood Ordinances, sharia courts, madrassa expansion, and the fusion of the army's institutional interest with a religious ideology. This decade is the hinge of the entire terrorism story
  • 1979-89The Afghan jihad. The ISI becomes the conduit for American and Saudi money and weapons to the mujahideen — and learns, at scale, how to run a deniable proxy war
  • 1999 · 2007Musharraf's coup, three months after Kargil · emergency and the lawyers' movement
  • 2008-presentFormally civilian government, with the army retaining decisive control over national security, nuclear policy, India policy and Afghanistan policy — what analysts call a “hybrid regime” or the “establishment”

The often-quoted line, and its actual significance

“Most states have an army; in Pakistan, the army has a state.” The aphorism is usually attributed to Voltaire's remark about Prussia, adapted; whoever coined the Pakistani version, the analytical content is what matters. The Pakistan Army is not only the dominant security actor but a very large economic one, through the Fauji Foundation, the Army Welfare Trust, the Defence Housing Authorities and extensive landholdings. Interpretation The consequence that matters for India is this: an institution whose primacy, budget and economic position rest on the existence of an existential Indian threat has a structural interest in that threat's continuation. This is not a claim about individual malice; it is a claim about incentives — and it is the single best explanation of why periods of genuine civilian-led rapprochement (1999, 2004-07, 2015) have repeatedly been interrupted by events originating outside the civilian chain.

The pattern of the interrupted opening

Set these four side by side and the pattern is hard to miss. February 1999: Vajpayee takes the bus to Lahore; the Kargil infiltration is already under way. July 2001: the Agra summit; the Parliament attack follows in December. 2004-2007: the most substantive back-channel in the relationship's history, reportedly close to a four-point framework; it dies with Musharraf's domestic collapse, and 26/11 follows in 2008. December 2015: Modi's unannounced Lahore stop; Pathankot is attacked eight days later. Interpretation Correlation is not proof, and each case has its own explanation. But a candidate who can lay out the sequence and then say “I would not assert a single hand behind all four, but I would say that any Indian government must now price in the probability that an opening will be tested” is reasoning like a planner rather than a commentator.

What this means for Indian policy

Three operational conclusions follow, and they are worth having ready because they turn historical knowledge into a recommendation — which is rung 9 of the ladder:

  1. Identify the actual interlocutor. Agreements reached with a Pakistani civilian government that the army has not underwritten have a poor survival record. This is an argument for military-to-military channels such as the DGMO hotline being maintained even when political relations are frozen — as they were, notably, throughout 2019-2025.
  2. Expect the test. Build the assumption of an interruption into the design of any opening, so that the opening survives the incident rather than the incident killing the opening.
  3. Target the incentive, not only the actor. Anything that reduces the army's institutional return on the India threat — international financial pressure, the growth differential, Pakistan's own internal security crisis — does more long-run work than any single strike. This is the strongest argument available for the FATF and economic instruments discussed in Sections 38 and 39.

Part II

The wars: 1965, 1971, Siachen, Kargil

1965: Kutch, Gibraltar, Grand Slam, Tashkent

A war that Pakistan began with a covert infiltration and ended having lost more ground than it gained, but which both countries have claimed ever since. The interesting question is not who won — it is what each side learned, because 1971 and 1999 both follow from the answers.

The Rann of Kutch, April 1965 — the misreading that caused the war

In the Rann of Kutch, a seasonally flooded salt marsh on the Gujarat-Sindh border where the boundary had never been settled, Pakistani forces moved into the disputed area around Kanjarkot in April 1965. The terrain overwhelmingly favoured Pakistan — its approaches were firm, India's were flooded and roadless. India chose not to escalate, accepted British mediation, and a ceasefire took effect on 30 June 1965. The dispute went to an international tribunal, whose 1968 award gave Pakistan roughly 350 square miles — about a tenth of its claim. India accepted and implemented it.

Why this matters far more than its size suggests

The Pakistani leadership drew three conclusions from Kutch and all three were wrong: that Indian forces would not fight well (they had been at the end of an impossible logistic chain); that India would not widen a local conflict (Shastri had explicitly warned it would choose its own time and place); and that international mediation would freeze gains in Pakistan's favour. Five months later Operation Gibraltar was launched on those conclusions, and on 6 September India crossed the international boundary towards Lahore. Interpretation Kutch is the cleanest case available of a successful tactical action producing a disastrous strategic inference — an outstanding point in any GD on deterrence: the signals you send are not the signals that are received.

Operation Gibraltar

Established In August 1965 Pakistan infiltrated several thousand irregulars — army regulars, Azad Kashmir forces and volunteers, organised into forces named after figures from early Islamic history — across the Ceasefire Line into the Kashmir Valley. The plan assumed a local uprising. It did not occur. Kashmiri villagers reported the infiltrators to the Indian authorities, and the operation collapsed within weeks. Know this precisely: it is the most direct refutation of the claim that the 1965 infiltration expressed a popular Kashmiri movement — and a useful corrective in the other direction, since the same population's attitude had changed markedly by 1989.

India's response was to seize the infiltration routes. On 28 August 1965 Indian troops captured the Haji Pir Pass, shortening the Poonch-Uri link and closing the principal infiltration corridor.

Operation Grand Slam

On 1 September 1965 Pakistan launched a conventional armoured thrust in the Chhamb-Jaurian sector, aimed at Akhnoor and the bridge over the Chenab. The objective was sound and dangerous: cutting Akhnoor would have severed the only road to Rajouri, Poonch and the whole western Jammu front. India committed the Air Force the same day.

The command change. Established With the attack making progress, Pakistan's GHQ replaced the attacking commander, Maj Gen Akhtar Hussain Malik, with Maj Gen Yahya Khan mid-operation. The handover cost roughly a day. Interpretation Most military historians on both sides regard that pause as the moment Akhnoor was saved — a first-rate example for any discussion of command continuity in battle, and a rare instance where Indian and Pakistani professional literature broadly agree.

6 September: India crosses the international boundary

India opened a new front in Punjab, advancing towards Lahore and in the Sialkot sector. The logic was to relieve Akhnoor by forcing Pakistan to defend its heartland — the oldest manoeuvre in the book, and it worked.

Tashkent, and the verdict

The Tashkent Declaration was signed on 10 January 1966 by Lal Bahadur Shastri and Ayub Khan, mediated by Soviet Premier Alexei Kosygin. Its central provision was a return to the pre-5 August 1965 positions — which meant India gave back the Haji Pir Pass. Shastri died of a heart attack in Tashkent in the early hours of 11 January 1966, hours after signing.

The case that Tashkent was right

  • India's war stocks were significantly depleted; a longer war favoured no one.
  • The US and Britain had suspended supplies to both parties, which hurt Pakistan more but hurt India too.
  • Soviet mediation locked in a relationship that proved decisive in 1971 — arguably the most valuable outcome of the whole exercise.
  • Territory held at a ceasefire is not a bargaining asset unless you will fight to keep it, and India would not.

The case that it was a mistake

  • Haji Pir commanded the principal infiltration route into the Valley. Every subsequent decade of infiltration used ground India had held and given back.
  • Nothing was obtained in exchange — no commitment on infiltration, no movement on the Ceasefire Line's status.
  • It established a pattern: India wins tactically, then returns gains at the table. Pakistani planners could, and did, treat territorial loss as temporary and therefore acceptable.
  • Indira Gandhi's refusal to repeat this at Simla in 1972 is best understood as a direct reaction to Tashkent.
Who won 1965?

“Militarily it was close to a draw with the balance of territory in India's favour; strategically Pakistan lost, because it had begun the war to change the status quo in Kashmir and ended it with the status quo intact. But I would not claim it as a clean Indian victory, because we returned Haji Pir and gained nothing durable at Tashkent. The most important consequence was not on the ground at all — India concluded it needed a decisive war rather than a limited one, and Pakistan concluded that the conventional route had closed. One of those conclusions produced 1971; the other, eventually, produced the proxy war.”

Institutionally, 1965 also produced the Border Security Force, raised on 1 December 1965, because state armed police could not hold an international border and the Army should not be tied down doing it in peacetime; and it pushed India decisively towards Soviet suppliers and Pakistan towards China — the alignments of the next fifty years are visible in that single embargo.

1971 and the Simla Agreement

Thirteen days, a new country, and the largest surrender since the Second World War. Also the most instructive single case in modern Indian military history of how political direction, intelligence, diplomacy, service jointness and campaign design can be made to work together — and of what happens when the victory is not converted at the table.

Why it happened

The war's origin was internal to Pakistan. In the general election of December 1970 — Pakistan's first on universal adult franchise — the Awami League under Sheikh Mujibur Rahman won 160 of 162 East Pakistani seats and an absolute majority in the National Assembly. The West Pakistani establishment refused to transfer power. On 25 March 1971 the Pakistan Army launched Operation Searchlight in Dhaka and across East Pakistan.

Disputed Casualty figures for the nine months that followed are among the most contested in modern history: Bangladesh's official figure is three million dead; the Pakistani Hamoodur Rahman Commission's figure was of the order of 26,000; independent scholarly estimates cluster in the range of 300,000 to 500,000 or higher. Large-scale sexual violence is documented by all sources. Roughly ten million refugees crossed into India, overwhelmingly into West Bengal, Tripura and Assam — a burden India described at the time, accurately, as a form of aggression by other means.

The preparation, which is the real lesson

In April 1971 Indira Gandhi asked General Sam Manekshaw whether the Army could move immediately. He said no, and set out why: the Himalayan passes were open to Chinese intervention, the monsoon was coming, the armoured and infantry formations needed to be regrouped from the western front, and the rivers of East Pakistan would be impassable. He asked for time and, by most accounts, offered to resign if overruled. He was given until the winter. Established This episode is the standard Indian teaching case for professional military advice to political authority, and it is worth being able to narrate in three sentences.

In the interval India did four things:

  1. Trained and equipped the Mukti Bahini, so that by December the Pakistan Army in the east was already dispersed, exhausted and fighting an insurgency in hostile country.
  2. Signed the Indo-Soviet Treaty of Peace, Friendship and Cooperation on 9 August 1971 — which, through its consultation clause, neutralised the risk of Chinese intervention and provided cover against American pressure. This was the decisive diplomatic act of the war.
  3. Prepared the international case through a sustained diplomatic campaign on the refugee burden.
  4. Planned a campaign of manoeuvre — bypassing fortified towns, using riverine and heliborne mobility, aiming at Dhaka rather than at territory.

The campaign, 3-16 December

Pakistan struck first, launching Operation Chengiz Khan — pre-emptive air strikes on Indian airfields in the west — on the evening of 3 December 1971. India had its casus belli and moved immediately.

The American dimension

Established The Nixon administration “tilted” towards Pakistan, partly because Pakistan was the channel for Kissinger's secret opening to China in July 1971. In December the United States ordered Task Force 74, centred on the carrier USS Enterprise, into the Bay of Bengal. A Soviet naval group shadowed it. The task force did not intervene, and the war ended before the question was tested. Interpretation Its lasting effect was psychological: it is the founding episode of Indian strategic distrust of American reliability, it is a stock reference in Indian naval thinking about sea denial, and it is the most commonly cited justification for the pursuit of strategic autonomy. It is also worth noting the counterpoint — that declassified records show significant internal American dissent, including the “Blood telegram” from the consulate in Dhaka protesting against the policy.

Why India won decisively

  • A political objective that was militarily achievable. Not “capture East Pakistan” but “create conditions for Bangladesh” — a limited, defined, attainable end-state.
  • Time was taken and used. Eight months of preparation converted a crisis into a campaign, and the diplomatic flank was secured first — the Soviet treaty preceded the war by four months.
  • Jointness in practice. Air superiority over the east within days, naval blockade on both coasts, army manoeuvre inland, and an insurgent force shaping the ground in advance.
  • Manoeuvre over attrition. Fortified towns were bypassed; the objective was Dhaka and the collapse of command.
  • A population that was on India's side. The single largest force multiplier in the campaign, and the clearest possible contrast with every subsequent counter-insurgency India has fought.

Simla, 1972

India held 93,000 prisoners and roughly 15,000 km² of Pakistani territory. It came away with a principle. Whether that was statesmanship or the greatest missed opportunity in Indian diplomatic history is the most durable argument in the subject, and you should be able to argue both sides.

The Simla Agreement was signed on 2 July 1972 by Indira Gandhi and Zulfikar Ali Bhutto. Its operative provisions:

  • Both governments will settle their differences by peaceful means through bilateral negotiations or by any other peaceful means mutually agreed. This is the clause India invokes to exclude third-party mediation, and it is the most important sentence in the India-Pakistan relationship.
  • Neither side shall unilaterally alter the situation, and both shall prevent the organisation of acts detrimental to the maintenance of peaceful relations.
  • The Ceasefire Line of 17 December 1971 shall be respected as the Line of Control, without prejudice to the recognised position of either side, and neither shall seek to alter it unilaterally, irrespective of mutual differences and legal interpretations.
  • Troops to be withdrawn to their own side of the international boundary.
  • Steps to be taken to resume communications, travel, trade and cultural exchange.

The LoC was delineated in detail on maps signed by the Directors General of Military Operations of both countries in December 1972 — which is why the LoC, unlike the LAC with China, is a line both armies know precisely.

The case that Simla was a success

  • It established bilateralism as the governing principle — the legal basis on which India has refused third-party mediation for half a century. Given the UN resolutions of 1948-49, this was a real gain.
  • It converted the Ceasefire Line into a Line of Control that neither side may unilaterally alter — moving the framework from a UN-supervised arrangement to a bilateral one.
  • Prisoners of war are protected persons under the Third Geneva Convention and must be repatriated after the cessation of active hostilities. Holding them indefinitely would have been unlawful, expensive and internationally damaging.
  • A humiliated Pakistan with no path back would have been more dangerous, not less. India's aim was a stable subcontinent, not a permanent grievance.
  • Bhutto's private argument — that a leader who signed away Kashmir in 1972 could not survive to implement anything — was probably true. A dead agreement with a dead government is worth nothing.

The case that Simla was a failure

  • India held the greatest bargaining position it will ever hold and obtained no settlement of Kashmir — the one issue the whole relationship turns on.
  • There is credible testimony that Bhutto gave a verbal understanding to convert the LoC into an international border in due course; nothing was written down. An unwritten undertaking from a leader under duress is not an asset.
  • Pakistan has since sought internationalisation at every opportunity — the OIC, the UN General Assembly, the UNSC in August 2019 — which shows bilateralism was not durably secured.
  • Within twelve years Pakistan was pursuing a nuclear weapon and within seventeen was running a proxy war. The agreement changed the vocabulary of the conflict, not its content.
  • Interpretation The deepest criticism is not about Kashmir at all: India traded a physical asset (prisoners, territory) for a procedural one (a principle). Procedural assets are only worth what the other party's compliance makes them worth.
How to answer “was Simla a mistake?”

“Sir, I would separate the prisoners from the settlement. Holding 93,000 prisoners indefinitely was not a real option — they are protected persons under the Geneva Conventions and the cost, legal and diplomatic, would have mounted every month. So I do not criticise the repatriation. What I would question is the sequencing: India gave up the tangible assets in exchange for a principle, and did not require any verifiable step on Kashmir first. The lesson I take from it is that leverage decays, and if you do not convert it into something written and verifiable while you hold it, you will not hold it long. That is a lesson about negotiation generally, not only about 1972.” That answer is fair, non-partisan, and arrives at a transferable principle — which is what the assessor is actually listening for.

The 1972-73 aftermath you should know

The Delhi Agreement of 28 August 1973, between India, Pakistan and Bangladesh, settled the repatriation of prisoners and civilians; repatriation was largely complete by April 1974. Pakistan recognised Bangladesh in February 1974. Bangladesh dropped its demand for war-crimes trials of 195 identified officers as part of the package — a decision still contested in Bangladesh today.

Siachen and the AGPL

The highest battlefield on earth, occupied because of four words in a 1949 agreement, held at enormous human and financial cost by both sides, and the one place where both militaries privately agree the position is absurd and neither can afford to move first.

How India got there

  • 1949Karachi Agreement ends the Ceasefire Line at NJ 9842 with the words “thence north to the glaciers”
  • 1970sPakistan begins authorising foreign mountaineering expeditions to peaks in the Siachen region, and American and European atlases begin showing the line running north-east from NJ9842 to the Karakoram Pass — placing the glacier in Pakistan. Indian officers call this “cartographic aggression”
  • 1978 · 1981Colonel Narendra “Bull” Kumar leads Indian expeditions to the Siachen region, confirming Pakistani-sponsored activity and mapping the approaches. His reports are the evidentiary basis for what follows
  • early 1984Indian intelligence learns of Pakistani purchases of Arctic-weather clothing and equipment from European suppliers, indicating an intended occupation
  • 13 Apr 1984Operation Meghdoot. Indian troops — Kumaon Scouts and 4 Kumaon, with IAF and Army Aviation lift — occupy Bilafond La and subsequently Sia La and the Saltoro passes, days ahead of the Pakistani plan
  • 1987Pakistan establishes the Quaid post at about 21,150 ft overlooking Bilafond La. In Operation Rajiv, on 26 June 1987, Naib Subedar Bana Singh of 8 JAK LI leads a four-man party up an ice wall and captures it. He is awarded the Param Vir Chakra — one of the few living recipients — and the post is renamed Bana Post
  • 1987-2003Continuous artillery and small-arms exchange; both sides sustain heavy non-battle casualties. The 2003 ceasefire largely ends the firing

The cost

  • Established The great majority of deaths on both sides have been caused by weather, altitude and avalanche, not by enemy fire. Indian official figures given in Parliament have indicated well over 1,000 Indian personnel lost on the Siachen glacier since 1984; Pakistani losses are of a comparable order and less well documented.
  • The Gayari avalanche of 7 April 2012 buried a Pakistani battalion headquarters, killing about 140 — the single worst incident on either side, and the event that briefly revived demilitarisation talk in Pakistan.
  • On 3 February 2016 an avalanche at the Sonam post killed ten Indian soldiers; Lance Naik Hanamanthappa Koppad was found alive after six days under the snow and died in hospital — the episode that fixed Siachen in Indian public consciousness.
  • Disputed Cost estimates vary widely and are frequently exaggerated in the press; figures in the range of several crore rupees per day are commonly cited but are not officially confirmed in a consistent form. Quote it as “very high and officially unquantified,” not as a number.
  • Medical and logistic realities: oxygen at roughly a third of sea-level partial pressure at the highest posts, temperatures to -50°C, kerosene and rations delivered by helicopter, and HAPO/HACE as a constant risk. India developed specialised clothing, the Siachen Battle School, and a dedicated air-maintenance system to sustain it.

The demilitarisation argument

For withdrawal

  • Enormous cost in lives and money for terrain of no economic value and limited tactical value now that the 2003 ceasefire holds.
  • The glacier is retreating and the environmental damage from four decades of military occupation is real and documented.
  • It would be a genuine confidence-building measure with a visible humanitarian dividend on both sides.
  • Both militaries privately acknowledge the absurdity; several retired Indian officers, including former Chiefs, have argued for a negotiated withdrawal.

Against withdrawal

  • The Kargil precedent. India vacated posts seasonally in 1999 and Pakistan occupied them. No Indian government after 1999 can ignore that.
  • Pakistan has refused to authenticate current ground positions on a map as a precondition to disengagement. Without authentication, a withdrawal cannot be verified and a re-occupation cannot be legally characterised.
  • Holding the Saltoro denies a Pakistan-China physical link across the Karakoram and protects the approaches to Ladakh — a consideration that has grown, not shrunk, since 2020.
  • Re-occupying the passes if they were lost would be far costlier in lives than holding them.
The one word that unlocks this question: authentication

Almost every candidate argues Siachen on cost and compassion. The professional answer turns on a technical point: “Sir, the Indian position is not that we will never withdraw. It is that any withdrawal must be preceded by the authentication of current Actual Ground Position Line positions on a map signed by both sides. Without that, there is nothing to return to and no way to characterise a violation. Pakistan has declined to authenticate, because to do so would be to concede that India holds the Saltoro. That is why the talks have failed nine times, and it is a question of verification, not of sentiment.” That answer will be remembered.

Kargil, 1999, and what it reformed

The first war between two nuclear-armed states. Fought uphill, in summer, against prepared positions above 16,000 feet, within India's own territory, by a country that had welcomed the other's Prime Minister three months earlier. Also the war that produced the most far-reaching reform of Indian national security since independence.

The setting

On 21 February 1999, Prime Minister Vajpayee travelled to Lahore by bus. The Lahore Declaration was signed, along with a Memorandum of Understanding on nuclear risk reduction — advance notification of ballistic missile tests, a commitment to prevent accidental use, and an undertaking to discuss respective nuclear doctrines. It was, at the time, the most substantive nuclear confidence-building arrangement in South Asian history.

The infiltration into the Kargil heights, under the Pakistani codename Operation Badr, was already in preparation.

The plan and the terrain

Indian posts along the LoC in the Kargil sector — at altitudes from 14,000 to over 18,000 feet — were vacated in winter by long-standing practice on both sides and reoccupied in spring. From the winter of 1998-99, Pakistani troops of the Northern Light Infantry, accompanied by irregulars, crossed the LoC and occupied these heights across a frontage of roughly 160 km in four main sub-sectors: Mushkoh, Drass, Kaksar/Kargil and Batalik, with related activity in the Turtuk sector.

The objective was specific and dangerous: the heights dominate National Highway 1A, the Srinagar-Leh road — the only land link to Ladakh. Interdicting it would have made Ladakh untenable, forced India to the table, and internationalised the Kashmir question. Interpretation Most analysts assess the plan was also intended to relieve pressure on Pakistan's position in Siachen by threatening its lifeline.

The war

The numbers

Established Indian fatalities are officially given as 527 (some compilations cite 524 or 533 depending on the cut-off and inclusion criteria), with over 1,300 wounded. Disputed Pakistani fatalities: Pakistan initially denied involvement of its regular troops altogether and refused to receive many of the bodies; later Pakistani statements, including by Prime Minister Nawaz Sharif and by the Pakistan Army itself in acknowledging NLI casualties and awarding gallantry decorations, confirmed regular involvement. Figures cited range from Pakistan's own ~450 to Indian estimates of 700-1,200. Quote the Indian figure as established and the Pakistani figure as contested.

Four Param Vir Chakras were awarded: Capt Vikram Batra and Lt Manoj Kumar Pandey (posthumous), Gdr Yogendra Singh Yadav and Rfn Sanjay Kumar. This is the largest number for any single operation since 1971.

The two decisions that defined the war

Not crossing the LoC

India imposed an absolute restriction on ground and air forces crossing the Line of Control, at a real tactical cost — it meant frontal assaults uphill where flanking movements through Pakistani territory would have been cheaper in lives.

For: It made India unambiguously the victim of aggression, secured near-total international support including, unusually, from the United States and China, and denied Pakistan the wider war that would have internationalised the crisis. It also kept the conflict firmly below the nuclear threshold.

Against: It cost Indian lives that a shallow envelopment would have saved, and it established a self-imposed constraint that an adversary can plan around. Several serving officers argued against it at the time.

The verdict most analysts reach: strategically correct, tactically expensive, and a price consciously paid — which is what makes it a good decision rather than a lucky one.

Who knew in Rawalpindi?

Disputed Nawaz Sharif has maintained he was not fully briefed on the scale and nature of the operation. Pakistani accounts identify a small group around General Musharraf — including Lt Gen Aziz Khan, Lt Gen Mahmud Ahmed and Maj Gen Javed Hassan — as its architects. Musharraf's own memoir defends the operation as a legitimate response to Indian positions.

What is established: India intercepted and later released a telephone conversation between Musharraf, then in Beijing, and his Chief of General Staff, discussing the operation — which was used to demonstrate direct army involvement.

Why it matters analytically: if a major military operation with strategic consequences could be planned without full civilian knowledge, then agreements signed by Pakistani civilian governments carry limited assurance. This is the strongest evidentiary basis for the argument in Section 9.

What Kargil reformed

More than any other event in this handbook, Kargil is a story about institutions. The battlefield outcome took ten weeks; the reforms it triggered are still being implemented, and one of them was only completed in 2019.

The government appointed the Kargil Review Committee under K. Subrahmanyam in July 1999. Its report — an unusually candid public document — found a systemic intelligence failure, an absence of a coordinated national security structure, gaps in surveillance of the LoC, and shortfalls in high-altitude equipment. It was followed by a Group of Ministers report in 2001 with four task forces: intelligence, internal security, border management and defence management.

The best single answer on Kargil

If asked “what was the significance of Kargil?”, resist the temptation to narrate Tiger Hill. “Sir, three things. Militarily, it proved that Indian infantry could take prepared positions above 16,000 feet, and that artillery used in the direct-fire role was decisive. Diplomatically, the decision not to cross the LoC won India near-universal international support and forced a withdrawal through Washington rather than through mediation — which vindicated the Simla position. But institutionally it did the most: the Kargil Review Committee led to the NTRO, the DIA, the Strategic Forces Command, the Andaman and Nicobar Command and, eventually, the Chief of Defence Staff. The reform mattered more than the battle. The uncomfortable part is that the last of those recommendations took twenty years to implement.”

The question that follows, and how to handle it

A good board will ask: “Did the reforms work? We were surprised again in 2008 and in 2019.” Do not become defensive. “Partly, sir. Technical intelligence and surveillance of the LoC improved measurably — there has been no repeat of an undetected occupation of that scale. What 26/11 and Pulwama showed is a different failure: not collection but collation and action — the last mile between an agency holding a piece of information and a commander acting on it. That is why the Multi Agency Centre and NATGRID were created, and why the NIA was set up in 2008. I would say collection has improved more than integration has.” That is an honest, informed and confident answer to a hostile question.

Part III

Kashmir from within: politics, insurgency, 370

1953-1987: the politics that preceded the gun

The insurgency of 1989 did not arrive from nowhere, and it is not adequately explained by Pakistani sponsorship alone. Pakistan supplied the weapons, the training and the sanctuary. The grievance was largely manufactured in Srinagar and New Delhi over the preceding thirty-five years. A candidate who can say that calmly and accurately is doing exactly what a counter-insurgency officer is required to do.

The 1987 election, and why you should mention it

This is the most uncomfortable fact in the Indian account, and stating it is a mark of maturity rather than disloyalty. “Sir, the insurgency had an external sponsor and an internal cause, and I do not think you can counter it effectively while denying either. Pakistan provided the training camps, the weapons and the sanctuary — without which there is no insurgency. But the young men who crossed over in 1989 were largely the people who had contested and lost a rigged election in 1987. The Government of India's own subsequent handling — the Prime Minister's 2010 statement, the interlocutors, the restoration of the assembly in 2024 — reflects a recognition that political process is part of the answer. If I am ever deployed there, I would want to understand both halves.” That is precisely the answer a counter-insurgency-experienced officer wants to hear from a candidate.

1989-90: the eruption and the exodus

Within eighteen months, a state that had not seen organised political violence since 1947 had an armed insurgency, an exodus, a collapsed administration and a security response that would take fifteen years to stabilise.

The trigger sequence

  • 8 Dec 1989Rubaiya Sayeed, daughter of the Union Home Minister Mufti Mohammad Sayeed, is kidnapped by the JKLF. The government releases five detained militants in exchange. The most important structural fact about the insurgency

The ISI's deliberate substitution of pro-Pakistan Hizbul Mujahideen for pro-independence JKLF between 1990 and 1994, and later the substitution of Punjabi-dominated Lashkar-e-Taiba and Jaish-e-Mohammed for Kashmiri groups altogether, tells you what the campaign was for. Interpretation A movement genuinely about Kashmiri self-determination would not have had its most self-determination-minded faction destroyed by its own sponsor. This is the single strongest argument available against the “indigenous freedom struggle” characterisation, and it is an argument from the sponsor's own conduct rather than from assertion. It is also why, from the late 1990s, the movement lost much of its local political character and became — in Indian assessment and in much independent scholarship — an externally directed campaign using local recruits.

Why it grew so fast

  1. Political blockage. Thirty-five years in which every avenue of electoral expression had been closed, culminating in 1987.
  2. Demography and unemployment. A very young population with few jobs and an education system producing graduates for an economy that had none.
  3. The Afghan effect. The Soviet withdrawal from Afghanistan in February 1989 released a trained, ideologically mobilised and weapon-rich network, and an ISI that had just spent a decade learning how to run proxy war at scale. The AK-47 became available in quantity, and the belief that a superpower could be defeated by jihad became available too.
  4. State collapse. The police and civil administration in the Valley ceased to function effectively in 1990; the intelligence apparatus was blind.
  5. A grievance narrative with real content. The most dangerous kind, because it cannot be answered by denial.

The Pandit exodus

The event most likely to be raised, most likely to be answered emotionally, and most in need of precision. Handle it with facts and with dignity.

Established Beginning in late 1989 and accelerating catastrophically around 19 January 1990, the overwhelming majority of the Kashmiri Pandit community left the Valley. Government of India and J&K relief figures record on the order of 60,000 to 65,000 registered migrant families, corresponding to roughly 200,000 to 300,000 people; community organisations cite higher totals. The precipitating factors were targeted killings of prominent Pandits from September 1989, threatening notices and press advertisements, and loudspeaker campaigns from mosques on the night of 19 January. Killings continued after the exodus — the massacres at Wandhama (January 1998, 23 killed) and Nadimarg (March 2003, 24 killed) among them.

How to speak about this in an interview

Three rules. One: use the verified numbers — registered migrant families and the recorded massacres — rather than the inflated figures that circulate online, because the verified numbers are appalling enough and cannot be challenged. Two: do not generalise. Many Pandits were sheltered and warned by Muslim neighbours; that is documented in the community's own testimony and does not diminish the crime. Three: connect it to policy — the Prime Minister's Development Package for return and rehabilitation, transit accommodation at Sheikhpora and elsewhere, and reserved government posts under the 2008 and 2015 packages, alongside the honest fact that return has been very limited and that targeted killings of returnees and minorities resumed in 2021-22.

The formulation that works

“Sir, it was an ethnic cleansing of a community from its own homeland, and the Indian state failed to prevent it. I think it is important to say both halves of that. I would also say that the community's return has not happened in any meaningful way in thirty-five years, and that any honest assessment of normalisation in the Valley has to be measured against that fact rather than against tourist arrivals.” That is unflinching, accurate, and demonstrates that you measure policy by outcomes rather than by announcements.

The trend line, stated carefully

Disputed Violence data in J&K comes from several sources — MHA annual reports, the South Asia Terrorism Portal, and J&K Police — which use different definitions and do not agree. What they do agree on is the shape of the curve: a catastrophic peak around 2001, with total fatalities above 4,000 in that year; a steep fall after the 2003 ceasefire to a trough around 2012-13; a renewed rise from 2015 to a local peak around 2018; and a marked decline after 2019 in total incidents alongside a shift in character towards fewer, more targeted attacks including on minorities and on soldiers in forested terrain. In an interview, describe the shape and name your source; do not quote a single number as if it were settled.

The doctrine

The Indian Army's published Doctrine for Sub-Conventional Operations (2006) and the Army's internal “Ten Commandments” for operations in J&K rest on a small number of principles that are worth stating in an interview because they show you understand what the job actually is:

  • The people are the centre of gravity, not the terrorist. Every operation is assessed by its effect on the population, not by body count.
  • Minimum force, always. The soldier operates in his own country among his own citizens under the law of the land, not under the law of armed conflict.
  • No collateral damage as a planning assumption, which is why cordon-and-search operations in J&K are slow, manpower-intensive and frequently allow the escape of the target — a cost deliberately accepted.
  • Operations are police-led where possible, with the Army in support, because legitimacy is built by civil institutions.
  • The political process is the objective, not an interruption of operations. Military action buys time for politics; it does not substitute for it.

Operation Sadbhavana

The Army's civic action programme in J&K, running since 1998: Army Goodwill Schools, medical camps, vocational training centres, women's empowerment centres, infrastructure in remote villages, and national-integration tours taking Kashmiri students to other parts of India. Interpretation Assessments of its effect vary: supporters point to genuine access and goodwill in specific districts; critics argue civic action by an armed force cannot substitute for civil governance and can blur the line between the two. Both are worth knowing. What is not in doubt is that it is a deliberate doctrinal choice rather than public relations, and that its existence is a good answer to a board member who asks what the Army does in Kashmir besides fight.

AFSPA, rights and the legitimacy problem

A question you may well be asked, and one where the temptation is to give a slogan. Don't. This is the clearest opportunity in the whole interview to demonstrate that you understand what it means to hold power over civilians.

What AFSPA actually provides

The Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act — the J&K version enacted in 1990 — applies only in areas formally declared “disturbed” by the government. In such areas it empowers a commissioned, warrant or non-commissioned officer to use force, including causing death, against a person acting in contravention of a prohibited order; to arrest without warrant; to enter and search without warrant; and to destroy arms dumps and fortified positions. Its most contested provision is Section 7 (Section 6 in the north-east Act): no prosecution may be instituted against a person acting under the Act except with the prior sanction of the Central Government.

The case for AFSPA

  • Soldiers are not police and are not trained or equipped for evidentiary policing; deploying them without a legal framework would be worse, not better.
  • Without protection from routine prosecution, junior leaders will hesitate in situations that require split-second decisions, and hesitation costs soldiers' lives.
  • The Act's powers are triggered only by a civilian declaration of a disturbed area — the political authority, not the Army, decides where it applies.
  • The Supreme Court upheld its constitutionality in Naga People's Movement of Human Rights v. Union of India (1997), subject to binding guidelines including the Army's own Do's and Don'ts, which the Court made enforceable.

The case against

  • The sanction requirement has meant that very few prosecutions have proceeded, producing a widespread perception of impunity — and perception is the currency of counter-insurgency.
  • An extraordinary law in force for over three decades ceases to be extraordinary and becomes a permanent constitutional exception.
  • Specific documented incidents — Pathribal (2000), Machil (2010), the 2021 Nagaland killings in Mon district — have each had a measurable and adverse effect on the operating environment.
  • The Justice Jeevan Reddy Committee (2005) recommended repeal, with necessary powers folded into the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act; the Second Administrative Reforms Commission made a similar recommendation.
The answer to give

“Sir, I would separate the legal instrument from the conduct under it. The Army needs a legal framework to operate in an internal-security role; without one you would be asking a young officer to make lethal decisions with no legal basis, and that is not a kindness to anybody. But I think two things are true at the same time: the Act is necessary where it is genuinely required, and it should be reviewed and progressively withdrawn district by district as the situation permits — which the government has in fact done, in Tripura in 2015, in Meghalaya in 2018, and in phased reductions across Assam, Nagaland and Manipur since 2022. On accountability, I would say the sanction provision is the part that does the most damage to us, because impunity — real or perceived — is what the adversary's propaganda runs on. The Army's own Do's and Don'ts and its record of court-martialling its own people is a stronger argument for us than any legal immunity.”

That answer is loyal, legally accurate, aware of the operational reality, and arrives at the point that actually matters — legitimacy is a weapon system.

Where AFSPA currently applies

Re-verify AFSPA remains in force in Jammu and Kashmir. In the north-east, the “disturbed area” notification has been progressively reduced since 2022 and now covers only parts of Assam, Manipur, Nagaland and Arunachal Pradesh, with Tripura (2015) and Meghalaya (2018) fully removed. The precise district list changes every six months; check the Ministry of Home Affairs notification before your interview rather than quoting a figure.

Article 370, 5 August 2019, and after

The single most probable question on this entire subject. Know the mechanism, not just the headline — because almost every candidate says “Article 370 was removed” and almost none can describe how.

What was actually done

Article 370 was not repealed. It was rendered inoperative from within, using its own machinery. The steps, in order:

  1. Constitution (Application to Jammu and Kashmir) Order, 2019 (C.O. 272), issued on 5 August 2019 under Article 370(1), extended the whole of the Indian Constitution to J&K, superseding the 1954 Order — and thereby extinguishing Article 35A, which existed only by virtue of that Order.
  2. C.O. 272 also amended Article 367 (the interpretation clause) so that the reference in Article 370(3) to the “Constituent Assembly of the State” would be read as the Legislative Assembly of the State — and, since the state was under President's Rule with the assembly dissolved, the powers of the Legislative Assembly were exercisable by Parliament.
  3. Both Houses of Parliament passed a statutory resolution recommending that the President declare, under Article 370(3), that Article 370 shall cease to be operative. C.O. 273 of 6 August 2019 gave effect to it.
  4. The Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019 split the state into two Union Territories: Jammu & Kashmir, with a legislature, and Ladakh, without one. It came into force on 31 October 2019 — the birth anniversary of Sardar Patel.

The Supreme Court's verdict

Established In In Re: Article 370 of the Constitution, decided 11 December 2023, a Constitution Bench of five judges led by Chief Justice D. Y. Chandrachud unanimously upheld the abrogation. Its principal holdings:

  • Article 370 was a temporary provision, placed in Part XXI (“Temporary, Transitional and Special Provisions”), intended to serve a transitional purpose during the state's integration.
  • J&K retained no element of internal sovereignty after accession distinguishable from that of other states; the Instrument of Accession and the 1957 state constitution completed the integration.
  • The President's power under Article 370(3) survived the dissolution of the state's Constituent Assembly in 1957 — otherwise the provision could never have been ended, which would defeat its temporary character.
  • The Court did not endorse the amendment of Article 367 as a route to amending Article 370, holding that route impermissible — but upheld the outcome on the independent ground of the President's direct power under 370(3).
  • It directed that statehood be restored “at the earliest” and that elections to the J&K Legislative Assembly be held by 30 September 2024. It upheld the separate carve-out of Ladakh as a Union Territory under Article 3.

The argument for

  • Full constitutional integration. Roughly 890 central laws, including the Right to Information Act, the Right to Education Act, the Prevention of Corruption Act and the SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, now apply in J&K as elsewhere.
  • Rights extended to excluded groups: West Pakistan refugees of 1947 and their descendants, Valmiki and Gorkha communities, and — following the removal of the permanent-resident disability — women marrying non-residents.
  • Reservations and representation extended to Scheduled Tribes including Gujjars, Bakarwals, Paharis and Valmikis; nine assembly seats reserved for STs for the first time.
  • Economic argument: the bar on outside investment in land had, on this view, kept the territory dependent on central transfers rather than developing an industrial base.
  • The separatist argument was removed. The claim of a distinct constitutional status had been the legal scaffolding of the separatist case; removing it removed the scaffolding.

The argument against

  • Process. A provision requiring the concurrence of the state government was operated while the state was under President's Rule, with the Governor — a central appointee — standing in for the government whose concurrence was required. The Supreme Court upheld the outcome but pointedly declined to endorse the Article 367 route.
  • Consent. It was done without consulting the state's elected representatives, with the political leadership under detention, communications suspended and a large security deployment in place.
  • Precedent. A state was converted into a Union Territory, and divided, without its assembly's consent — a first in Indian constitutional history, and a precedent other states have noticed.
  • Outcomes. Large-scale outside investment has been slower than projected; unemployment remains high; and targeted killings of minorities and migrant workers in 2021-22 and the attack at Pahalgam in 2025 indicate the security problem was altered rather than resolved.
  • Statehood. The reduction of a state to a Union Territory remains, in the view of all J&K parties and of the Supreme Court's own direction, a condition to be reversed.
How to answer “was abrogating Article 370 right?”

This is a political question put to a candidate for a non-political service, and how you handle that is itself the test. “Sir, it is settled law — the Supreme Court upheld it unanimously in December 2023, so as far as the Constitution is concerned the question is closed, and as an officer I would implement the law as it stands. On the merits, the strongest argument for it is that it extended the full body of constitutional rights, including to groups that had been excluded for seventy years, and removed the legal scaffolding of the separatist case. The strongest argument against is about process and about consent, and the Court itself declined to endorse one part of the method used. What I would watch is outcomes rather than arguments — restoration of statehood, which the Court directed, the investment actually realised, and whether local recruitment into militancy continues to fall. Those will settle the question better than either side's rhetoric.”

What has actually changed since 2019

Measured rather than asserted. This is a current-affairs passage and the fastest-ageing part of the handbook.

Political

  • Delimitation Commission (Justice Ranjana Desai), report March-May 2022: the assembly raised to 90 elected seats — 43 for Jammu, 47 for Kashmir, with 9 reserved for Scheduled Tribes and 7 for Scheduled Castes, plus 24 seats notionally reserved for PoJK. Contested in the Valley as altering the regional balance of representation.
  • Assembly elections held 18 September - 1 October 2024 — the first since 2014. Turnout was substantial. The National Conference-Congress alliance won a majority and Omar Abdullah took office as Chief Minister of the Union Territory on 16 October 2024.
  • Statehood has not been restored. Re-verify This is the central political demand of every J&K party and was the subject of a Supreme Court direction; check its status before your interview.
  • Panchayat and District Development Council elections have been held; local government functions with a substantially expanded elected tier.

Security

  • Total incidents and local recruitment have declined markedly from the 2018 peak, on both MHA and independent datasets.
  • The character of violence changed: fewer large encounters, more targeted killings of minorities, off-duty policemen and migrant labourers (a documented wave in 2021-22); “hybrid” operatives with no prior record; and a shift of the centre of gravity southwards into the forested Rajouri-Poonch-Kathua belt, where a series of ambushes on convoys and patrols from 2021 onward has cost significant Army casualties. That shift is the single most important security development since 2019.
  • Separatist organisations have been systematically dismantled: the Jamaat-e-Islami J&K banned (2019), the JKLF banned (2019), the Hurriyat effectively defunct with several constituents formally dissociating from separatism from 2023-25, and terror-funding prosecutions by the NIA — including the conviction of Yasin Malik in May 2022.
  • Re-verify The Pahalgam attack of 22 April 2025, in which 26 civilians were killed at Baisaran, was the worst attack on civilians in J&K in many years and demonstrated that tourism-dependent normalisation is itself a target.

Economic and social

  • Tourism reached record levels between 2021 and 2024 — over two crore visitors in some years on official figures, including the Amarnath and Vaishno Devi yatras — and is the most-cited indicator of normalisation. It is also the most fragile, as April 2025 demonstrated.
  • Infrastructure: the Udhampur-Srinagar-Baramulla Rail Link, including the Chenab Bridge — the world's highest railway arch bridge — and the Anji Khad cable-stayed bridge; the Banihal-Qazigund and Z-Morh/Sonamarg tunnels; four-laning of NH-44. Re-verify commissioning dates.
  • Investment: large figures have been announced under the new industrial development scheme; realised investment and employment generated have lagged the announcements, and this gap is the fair point of criticism.
  • Cinemas reopened in Srinagar in 2022 after more than three decades; the Muharram procession in Srinagar was permitted in 2023 after 34 years. These are small facts with disproportionate signalling value, in both directions.

How to answer “is Kashmir normal now?”

“Sir, I would answer it on three indicators rather than as a yes or no. Violence is down substantially from the 2018 peak and local recruitment has fallen — that is real. The elected assembly has been restored, which matters, though statehood has not. But the character of the threat has changed rather than ended: it has moved into the forested Rajouri-Poonch belt, it uses operatives with no record, and Pahalgam in April 2025 showed that the tourism recovery is itself a target. So I would say the security situation has improved and the political process has restarted; I would not say it is settled, and I would measure it by whether Pandit families return and by whether statehood is restored, not by tourist numbers.”

Part IV

The terror machine: doctrine, sponsors, groups, money

Terrorism as statecraft, and the ISI

The central analytical claim of this handbook: for the Pakistani security establishment, support to militant groups operating against India has been a deliberate, cost-effective and — on its own terms — successful instrument of policy for more than thirty years. Understanding why it works is the precondition for making it stop working.

The logic of the instrument

The single best point you can make in a GD on terrorism

“The attack is the least important part of the system. What the sponsor has actually achieved — and failed to achieve

Achieved

  • Kept Kashmir on international agendas for thirty-five years.
  • Imposed an enormous, permanent and recurring cost on the Indian exchequer and on Indian manpower.
  • Prevented the LoC from being treated as a settled border.
  • Avoided, until 2016, any direct military cost to itself.

Not achieved

  • No territory has changed hands in thirty-five years — the objective of the whole enterprise.
  • India's constitutional position hardened rather than softened, culminating in 2019.
  • India's growth was not meaningfully impeded; the economic gap between the two countries widened enormously over exactly the period of the campaign.
  • Blowback. Groups nurtured as instruments of external policy contributed to an internal insurgency that has killed tens of thousands of Pakistanis — the TTP, sectarian killings, and the loss of state writ in parts of the country.
  • International isolation, FATF grey-listing, and a reputation that complicates every Pakistani economic negotiation.

Interpretation Put the two columns together and the honest conclusion is uncomfortable for both sides: the instrument has been tactically effective and strategically bankrupt. It has cost Pakistan more than it has gained and has not moved the map by a metre — but it has also imposed a permanent tax on India that India has not found a way to stop paying. Any recommendation you make at rung 9 has to engage with both halves of that.

India's formal position is stated in terms of evidence, and you should follow it. The first sounds like an officer; the second sounds like a television panel — and an informed interviewer will know the difference immediately. Where a court has found the link — as with the Kasab conviction, the David Headley deposition in the Chicago and Mumbai proceedings, or the Tahawwur Rana extradition — say so and name the proceeding.

Ideology, recruitment, and beyond Kashmir

Weapons can be intercepted; a belief cannot. This section is about the part of the problem that has no military solution — and about the fact that India's record here is, on the evidence, far better than almost anyone gives it credit for.

The pipeline

  1. Grievance — real or curated: a death in the family, humiliation at a checkpoint, an encounter in the village, unemployment, a viral video. The grievance does not have to be false to be exploited.
  2. Ideological framing — the grievance is reinterpreted as part of a cosmic religious struggle, converting a local complaint into an absolute obligation. This is the decisive step, and it is the one that makes the violence unnegotiable.
  3. Social reinforcement — peer group, mosque, online community, sometimes family. Almost nobody radicalises alone.
  4. Handler contact — recruitment, tasking, and a weapon. In the post-2015 Valley the classic pattern was: a photograph with a rifle posted on social media, followed by a short operational life.
  5. Action.

The Zia inheritance

The ideological infrastructure did not grow spontaneously. Between 1977 and 1988, General Zia-ul-Haq's Islamisation programme — the Hudood Ordinances, the Federal Shariat Court, the zakat system, and an enormous expansion of state-supported and Gulf-funded madaris — created a permanent institutional base. Estimates of madrassa numbers in Pakistan grew from the low hundreds at independence to tens of thousands. Interpretation The overwhelming majority teach religion and produce no militants at all, and it is both inaccurate and counter-productive to say otherwise. The significant minority that did become recruitment pipelines, concentrated in particular networks in Punjab and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, are the relevant fact — and Pakistan's own National Action Plan of 2014 committed to regulating them, a commitment its own analysts describe as largely unimplemented.

The Indian counter-radicalisation record

Established Despite having the world's third-largest Muslim population — more Muslims than Pakistan — India has contributed a very small number of recruits to the Islamic State, in the low hundreds at most by Indian and international estimates, compared to tens of thousands from other countries with far smaller Muslim populations. This is one of the most striking data points in global counter-terrorism and it is almost never mentioned by Indian candidates.

Interpretation The usual explanations are: constitutional citizenship with genuine political representation and avenues of redress; the plural, syncretic character of South Asian Islam, particularly Barelvi and Sufi traditions; economic integration and mobility; effective community leadership that has consistently and publicly rejected the ideology; and capable, locally-embedded state policing. Deployed carefully, this is the single most powerful argument available for Indian secular democracy as a security asset — and it directly answers the two-nation theory on evidence rather than on rhetoric.

How to use it

“Sir, I think our best counter-terrorism achievement is one we never claim. India has more Muslims than Pakistan, and our contribution to the Islamic State's foreign fighter flow was a couple of hundred people at the outside — against tens of thousands from countries a fraction of our size. That is not an accident of geography. It is constitutional citizenship, economic opportunity, community leadership that refused the ideology, and good local policing. It is also fragile: the thing most likely to break it is communal polarisation at home. So when I say internal harmony is a national security issue, I mean it operationally, not sentimentally.”

That answer is factual, patriotic, and shows the assessor a candidate who understands that national security has a domestic dimension. It is also an honest caution delivered without any political edge.

Beyond Kashmir: Punjab, D-Company, Indian Mujahideen

Framing this as a Kashmir problem understates it by about half. Three other campaigns — one largely defeated, one criminal, one home-grown — show the same sponsorship pattern operating outside the Valley, and knowing them makes your answer a category above the standard one.

Punjab, 1978-1995

  • The Khalistan insurgency was substantially indigenous in origin — rooted in the Anandpur Sahib Resolution's federal demands, river-water and territorial disputes with Haryana, and the politics around Jarnail Singh Bhindranwale — but received weapons, training and sanctuary from Pakistan from the early 1980s.
  • Key events: Operation Blue Star (1-8 June 1984) at the Golden Temple; the assassination of Indira Gandhi (31 October 1984) and the anti-Sikh massacres that followed; the Kanishka bombing (23 June 1985), which killed 329 people and remains the deadliest act of aviation terrorism before 9/11; the assassination of General A. S. Vaidya (1986); Operation Black Thunder (1988).
  • The insurgency was defeated by roughly 1993-95 through a combination of relentless police-led operations — notably under DGP K. P. S. Gill — the restoration of elections in 1992, and a decisive loss of popular support after the militants turned on their own community. This is India's most complete counter-insurgency success and the most instructive case in the entire subject.
  • Re-verify Residual activity persists: pro-Khalistan groups abroad (Sikhs for Justice's “Referendum 2020”), ISI-linked modules using drones to deliver weapons and narcotics across the Punjab border, targeted killings, and the Amritpal Singh episode of 2023. It is a live but marginal problem, and it is also a live diplomatic problem with Canada, the United Kingdom and the United States.
Why Punjab worked and Kashmir did not

This comparison is the best analytical material in the section. Four differences. One — geography: Punjab is flat, densely roaded and fenced, with a short border; Kashmir is mountainous with a 740 km line through forest and snow. Two — the population turned: militants' extortion, sectarian killing and attacks on Sikh civilians destroyed their base, whereas in Kashmir the alienation had deeper political roots and was not reversed. Three — police primacy: Punjab was won by the Punjab Police, locally recruited and locally knowledgeable, with the Army in support; a locally-owned campaign has a legitimacy an external one cannot buy. Four — politics was restored early: the 1992 elections, however imperfect, gave a political route back. State those four and add the honest caveat — that the Punjab campaign involved serious human-rights costs which are still before the courts, and that the lesson is not "do exactly that again."

D-Company and the 1993 Bombay blasts

On 12 March 1993, thirteen coordinated bomb blasts across Bombay — the Stock Exchange, the Air India building, hotels and markets — killed 257 people and injured over 700. Established Indian courts have convicted numerous accused; the TADA court's judgments named Dawood Ibrahim and Tiger Memon as the principal absconding conspirators, and Yakub Memon was executed in 2015. Dawood Ibrahim was designated by the UNSC 1267 Committee in 2003 and by the US Treasury as a Specially Designated Global Terrorist. The significance of the case: it is the clearest documented instance of an organised criminal syndicate being used as a terrorist delivery mechanism, with the RDX moved through established smuggling routes on the Konkan coast. The crime-terror nexus is a category, not an anomaly.

Indian Mujahideen and the home-grown module

Active roughly 2005-2013, the Indian Mujahideen emerged from elements of the Students Islamic Movement of India (SIMI, banned 2001) with documented links to LeT and to handlers in Pakistan and the Gulf. It conducted serial IED attacks in Jaipur, Ahmedabad, Delhi, Varanasi, Pune, Hyderabad and Bodh Gaya, and claimed responsibility through emails sent before or after attacks. Its leadership — the Bhatkal brothers, Yasin Bhatkal arrested in 2013 — was largely dismantled by the NIA and state police by 2014.

Why the IM matters to your answer. It is the strongest counter-example to a purely external framing: some of the recruits were Indian citizens with Indian grievances, several explicitly citing the 2002 Gujarat violence and the Babri Masjid demolition. Interpretation The sponsor's role was real — handlers, funds and training — but the recruitment pool was domestic. The operational conclusion is the one in Section 15: external sponsorship needs a domestic grievance to work, so denying the grievance is a counter-terrorism measure. Saying that in an interview shows you understand the problem in three dimensions rather than one.

Reading the table correctly

Two patterns are visible and both are worth stating. One: the highest-casualty attacks — 1993, 2006, 2008 — produced no military response, and the lower-casualty attacks on military targets after 2016 produced the strongest ones. Two: the target set has shifted over three decades from mass-casualty urban bombings, to attacks on security installations, to precisely targeted killings designed to produce political and economic effect without a large toll. Operation Parakram, 2001-02

Ten months, half a million soldiers, no war, and roughly eight hundred dead. The most expensive non-event in Indian military history and, precisely for that reason, the most instructive.

What happened. After the attack on Parliament on 13 December 2001, India mobilised its strike corps and much of its holding formations to the western border — an estimated 500,000 troops — under Operation Parakram. The deployment lasted from December 2001 to October 2002. Pakistan mobilised in response. The crisis peaked twice: in January 2002, and again after the Kaluchak massacre in May 2002, when foreign governments issued travel advisories urging their nationals to leave both countries.

Intense international diplomacy followed. On 12 January 2002, President Musharraf made a televised address banning LeT and JeM and pledging that Pakistani territory would not be used for terrorism — a commitment restated in the January 2004 joint statement with Vajpayee, which remains the single most-cited Pakistani undertaking in Indian diplomacy. India stood down in October 2002.

What it achieved

  • Extracted the January 2002 and January 2004 commitments, which India has invoked in every subsequent diplomatic exchange.
  • Contributed to a genuine, if temporary, reduction in infiltration and to the 2003 ceasefire.
  • Demonstrated that India would mobilise — a signal that had not previously been sent.

What it cost

  • Established Roughly 798 soldiers died during the deployment, the large majority in mine-laying accidents and other non-combat causes — more than India lost in the Kargil war.
  • Financial cost estimated at several thousand crore rupees.
  • Large tracts of Punjab and Rajasthan farmland were mined; demining took years and civilian casualties continued afterwards.
  • The strategic failure was mobilisation time. India's strike corps, based deep in the hinterland, took roughly three weeks to reach their launch positions. By then Pakistan had fully mobilised, the international community had intervened, and the moment for punitive action had passed.
  • Because it ended without action, it arguably damaged deterrence: it demonstrated that India would mobilise and then not strike.
The single most important consequence

Operation Parakram is the direct cause of everything in Section 29. The three-week mobilisation window was identified as the critical failure, and the Indian Army's response was the doctrinal revision announced in April 2004 and universally known as “Cold Start” — reorganising offensive power into smaller, pre-positioned integrated battle groups capable of launching shallow, rapid thrusts within days rather than weeks. If you can say “Parakram's failure was time, and Cold Start was the answer to time,” you have connected two things that most candidates know separately and nobody connects.

26/11 Mumbai

Sixty hours in November 2008 that changed Indian internal security architecture more than any event since 1962 changed the Army. Know the sequence, the failures and the reforms — and know why India did not strike.

The attack

Established Ten LeT operatives sailed from Karachi, hijacked the Indian fishing trawler MV Kuber, killed its crew, and landed by inflatable dinghy at Badhwar Park, Colaba, on the evening of 26 November 2008. Splitting into teams, they attacked the Chhatrapati Shivaji Terminus, the Taj Mahal Palace and Oberoi-Trident hotels, Nariman House (a Jewish centre), Leopold Café and Cama Hospital. Operations concluded on 29 November. 166 people were killed, including 28 foreign nationals from over a dozen countries, and more than 300 were injured.

The attackers were in continuous voice-over-IP contact with handlers in Pakistan throughout, receiving real-time target direction based on Indian television coverage — the first mass-casualty urban attack conducted under live remote command, and a fact that changed how every country thinks about live broadcasting from an incident scene.

The people to know

  • ASI Tukaram Omble — unarmed, grabbed the barrel of Ajmal Kasab's rifle and held on while being shot, enabling Kasab's capture alive. Ashoka Chakra, posthumous. That single act gave India a living witness, a confession, a trial and an evidentiary record admissible internationally. Without it, 26/11 would have been an assertion; because of it, it is a proven case.
  • Major Sandeep Unnikrishnan, NSG, killed at the Taj — Ashoka Chakra, posthumous. Havildar Gajender Singh, NSG, killed at Nariman House.
  • Hemant Karkare (ATS Chief), Ashok Kamte and Vijay Salaskar — senior police officers killed near Cama Hospital.
  • Ajmal Kasab — tried, convicted, sentence confirmed by the Supreme Court, and hanged on 21 November 2012.
  • David Coleman Headley (Daood Gilani) — a US citizen who conducted the reconnaissance; convicted in the United States, later an approver in Indian proceedings. Tahawwur Hussain Rana, his associate, was Re-verify extradited from the United States to India in 2025.
  • The NSG operation was designated Operation Black Tornado, with Operation Cyclone at Nariman House.

The failures, stated plainly

  • Maritime. Ten armed men crossed 500 nautical miles and landed in India's financial capital undetected. Coastal surveillance, fishing-vessel registration and inter-agency maritime responsibility were all inadequate.
  • Intelligence. Warnings existed, including specific American information derived from Headley and intercepts indicating a sea-borne attack on Mumbai hotels. They were not collated, assessed or actioned. This was a failure of integration, not of collection — the same diagnosis as Kargil.
  • Response time. The NSG is based at Manesar near Delhi. It required a flight, and the aircraft took hours to arrange. Commandos reached the Taj roughly nine to ten hours after the attack began.
  • Local capability. Mumbai Police were outgunned — many carried .303 rifles or nothing — and had no training or equipment for an armed assault of that character.
  • Media. Live broadcasting of security force movements was relayed to the attackers by their handlers. This is not a hypothetical criticism; it is documented in the intercepts.

“Why did India not strike after 26/11?”

This is asked constantly and deserves a real answer, not a defensive one. “Sir, several reasons, and I think they are honest ones rather than excuses. Military readiness for a strike of that kind was not what it became by 2016 — Operation Parakram had shown the mobilisation problem, and the Cold Start reorganisation was not complete. The political calculation was that a strike would convert overwhelming international sympathy into a bilateral crisis in which India would lose the moral position it had just gained. And there was a genuine assessment that escalation control was weak on both sides. India instead ran a diplomatic and legal campaign — the dossiers, the Kasab trial, the Headley depositions, the international designations — and it produced real results, including the 1267 listing of Jamaat-ud-Dawa within weeks. What is also true is that the absence of a kinetic response was read in Rawalpindi as tolerance, and that reading is part of why the policy changed after 2016. I think both statements are true at once, and the shift after Uri was a correction of the second point rather than a repudiation of the first.”

Uri and the 2016 surgical strikes

The operation itself was not unprecedented. The announcement was. That distinction is the entire significance of September 2016, and getting it right marks you out immediately.

The attack. Before dawn on 18 September 2016, four JeM fidayeen breached the perimeter of a brigade administrative base at Uri, near the LoC in Baramulla district. They set fire to temporary tented accommodation where troops of an incoming unit were resting during a relief-in-place. Nineteen soldiers were killed — the majority by fire — and a further soldier died later, bringing the toll to twenty on most counts. It was the deadliest attack on the Indian Army in that sector in decades.

The response. On the night of 28-29 September 2016, Indian Special Forces crossed the Line of Control at multiple points and struck launch pads in Pakistan-occupied territory, returning before first light. On 29 September, the Director General of Military Operations, Lt Gen Ranbir Singh, announced the operation at a joint press conference with the Ministry of External Affairs, describing “surgical strikes on launch pads along the Line of Control” and stating that India had no plans for further operations. Pakistan denied that any strike had occurred, describing the event as routine cross-LoC firing.

The thing to say that nobody says

“Sir, cross-LoC raids were not new — the Army had conducted them before, including after Kupwara in 2013, and Indian officers have publicly confirmed this. What was new in 2016 was that India announced it. That was a deliberate choice, and it had three effects: it satisfied a domestic demand for visible response, it established publicly that India would act across the LoC, and it forced Pakistan into a corner where it had to deny an event its own soldiers knew had happened — because acknowledging it would have created a domestic demand for retaliation. The most interesting feature of the operation is that India's public statement included a line saying there were no plans for further action. That was escalation management written into the announcement itself.”

What it achieved

  • Established a declared willingness to act across the LoC, changing the adversary's risk calculation for the first time since 1999.
  • Demonstrated that a response could be conducted below the threshold that triggers general war — a practical answer to the stability-instability paradox.
  • Built the political and institutional confidence that made Balakot possible three years later.
  • Domestically, it converted a demand for revenge into a controlled military action, which is a genuine achievement of civil-military management.

What it did not achieve

  • It did not stop the attacks. Pulwama followed in 2019, Pahalgam in 2025. Deterrence by punishment against a deniable adversary is inherently hard to prove and easy to overstate.
  • Launch pads are tents, ammunition and men — they are reconstituted in weeks. The physical damage was, by design, limited.
  • It converted a military option into a publicly expected one, which reduces future flexibility: after 2016, an Indian government that does not respond visibly pays a domestic price.
  • The politicisation of the operation in subsequent Indian domestic debate was itself a cost, in that it made a military instrument a subject of partisan argument.

The wider package. Military action was one element. India also boycotted the SAARC summit scheduled at Islamabad in November 2016 — and, decisively, persuaded Bangladesh, Bhutan, Afghanistan and later others to withdraw as well, which caused the summit's cancellation and has left SAARC effectively frozen since. India also announced a review of the Indus Waters Treaty's utilisation provisions and accelerated projects on the eastern rivers. Interpretation The diplomatic isolation of Pakistan within its own region was arguably a more durable achievement than the raid.

Pulwama and Balakot, 2019

The first use of air power across the international boundary since 1971, between two nuclear-armed states, followed within twenty-four hours by an aerial engagement and a captured pilot. Four days that redefined the conventional space under the nuclear ceiling.

Pulwama

On 14 February 2019, a vehicle carrying explosives was driven into a CRPF convoy on the Srinagar-Jammu national highway at Lethpora, Pulwama. Forty CRPF personnel were killed. JeM claimed responsibility; the bomber, Adil Ahmad Dar, was a local resident of Pulwama district. The NIA's chargesheet subsequently detailed the procurement of explosives, the assembly of the device and the role of Pakistan-based handlers.

What Pulwama revealed about convoy security: a very large convoy of 78 vehicles carrying 2,500 personnel moving on a public highway with civilian traffic not sanitised. Subsequent measures included air transport for large CRPF movements, road-opening and sanitisation protocols, limits on convoy size, and restrictions on civilian traffic during movements. This is the operationally useful part of the episode and very few candidates know it.

Balakot

In the early hours of 26 February 2019, Indian Air Force Mirage 2000 aircraft struck what India described as a JeM training facility at Jabba Top, near Balakot in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa — not in Pakistan-occupied Kashmir but in Pakistan proper, across the international boundary. India characterised the action as a “non-military, pre-emptive strike” against terrorist infrastructure, based on credible intelligence of a further attack being prepared. The weapons used were Disputed widely reported as SPICE-2000 precision-guided bombs.

Disputed Battle damage assessment is genuinely contested and you should not assert a figure. Indian officials spoke of “a very large number” of casualties; a senior minister gave a figure of around 250; open-source satellite imagery analysis by several independent groups found the buildings apparently intact; Pakistan said the bombs fell in a forest. The verifiable facts are that Indian aircraft entered Pakistani airspace, released weapons and returned, and that Pakistan responded militarily the next day — which is itself an acknowledgement that something significant occurred.

27 February

On 27 February 2019 the Pakistan Air Force conducted Operation Swift Retort, crossing the LoC and releasing weapons near Indian military installations in the Rajouri-Nowshera area. In the ensuing engagement, Wing Commander Abhinandan Varthaman, flying a MiG-21 Bison, was shot down over Pakistan-occupied territory and captured. He was returned on 1 March 2019 through the Wagah crossing. He was awarded the Vir Chakra. Disputed India stated that Abhinandan shot down a Pakistani F-16 before being downed; Pakistan denied losing any aircraft and denied that F-16s were used. Independent verification has not settled the question. Do not assert either version as fact. Separately and tragically, an IAF Mi-17 helicopter was lost to friendly fire near Budgam the same morning, killing six airmen and a civilian — a fratricide incident that India investigated and acknowledged, and which is a legitimate and important point about air defence procedures under stress.

Why Balakot mattered, in one paragraph

“Sir, the significance was not the damage, which is genuinely contested. It was the threshold. Between 1971 and 2019 neither air force had crossed the other's international boundary, because both assumed that air power was inherently escalatory in a nuclear dyad. Balakot demonstrated that India was prepared to use it against terrorist targets, and that Pakistan's nuclear posture did not confer immunity from conventional response at that level. India also framed it very carefully — a non-military, pre-emptive strike on a terrorist facility, not an attack on Pakistan — which preserved the legal and diplomatic space. What I would also note honestly is that the strike did not prevent Pahalgam six years later, so as a demonstration of capability it succeeded and as a deterrent it is unproven.”

The legal framing you should know

India's justification rests on the “unwilling or unable” doctrine — the contested but widely invoked proposition that a state may act in self-defence under Article 51 of the UN Charter against non-state actors on the territory of another state where that state is unwilling or unable to suppress them. The same doctrine underpinned American strikes in Pakistan and Syria, Turkish operations in Iraq, and French operations in the Sahel. Interpretation Its status in customary international law is genuinely disputed by scholars — but knowing that India is invoking an existing doctrine with significant state practice behind it, rather than improvising a justification, is exactly the kind of precision that impresses a board.

Pahalgam and Operation Sindoor, 2025

Pahalgam

On 22 April 2025, gunmen attacked tourists at the Baisaran meadow above Pahalgam, a high-altitude site accessible only on foot or pony. Twenty-six people were killed, almost all Indian tourists, together with a local pony handler, Syed Adil Hussain Shah, who was killed attempting to intervene. Reports indicated that victims were questioned about their religion before being shot. The Resistance Front claimed responsibility and subsequently retracted the claim. It was the worst attack on civilians in Jammu and Kashmir in many years.

Interpretation The target selection was unusually revealing. Tourism had been the principal official indicator of normalisation after 2019; an attack on tourists at a marquee destination in peak season, conducted so as to provoke communal reaction across India, was aimed simultaneously at the economy, at the normalisation narrative, and at India's internal cohesion. The fact that Kashmiri civilians across the Valley publicly protested against the attack — shutting shops and holding candlelight marches — was, in security terms, the single most significant response to it.

What is genuinely new about 2025

  • Depth. Targets included locations in Pakistani Punjab — Bahawalpur and Muridke are the headquarters complexes of the two principal groups, not launch pads near the line.
  • Domain. The first India-Pakistan exchange in which armed drones and loitering munitions were used at scale by both sides, and in which layered air defence performance became the central military question.
  • The water instrument. The Indus Waters Treaty, which had survived 1965, 1971 and 1999, was for the first time placed in abeyance. Whether this is sustainable or wise is argued in Section 35.

How to handle a question on Operation Sindoor

The temptation is to recite claimed damage. Resist it absolutely. “Sir, I would separate what is established from what is claimed. Established: India struck nine sites on 7 May, including facilities at Bahawalpur and Muridke; exchanges continued for three days involving drones and missiles; an understanding was reached on 10 May through the DGMO channel; and India has stated a new declaratory policy that terrorism will be treated as an act of war. Claimed and contested: aircraft losses on both sides, damage assessments, and the role of external actors in the ceasefire. I would not assert the contested part. What I think is strategically significant is the third point — India has now publicly lowered its own threshold for response, which increases deterrent pressure but also transfers the timing of the next crisis to the adversary. That is a real trade-off and I do not think it has been fully debated.”

An answer that declines to repeat unverified claims, and explains why, is worth more than any number of remembered figures.

The response ladder and the conventional space

Thirty years in one diagram. If you learn a single graphic from this handbook, learn this one — it converts a list of incidents into an argument, and an argument is what you are being marked on.

The case that the new doctrine works

  • Thirty years of restraint produced an escalating series of attacks culminating in 26/11; the evidence that restraint deters is weak.
  • Imposing visible, attributable cost changes the sponsor's cost-benefit calculation, which is the only calculation that matters to an instrumental actor.
  • Each round has demonstrated that the nuclear threshold is higher than Pakistan's signalling implied — which recovers conventional space India had conceded.
  • It satisfies a real democratic requirement. A government that cannot respond to the killing of its citizens loses legitimacy, and legitimacy is a strategic asset.
  • The targeting has been consistently narrow and the framing consistently legal, which has preserved international support.

The case for caution

  • The attacks have continued. Uri followed the restraint years; Pulwama followed Uri; Pahalgam followed Balakot. Deterrence by punishment has not been demonstrated.
  • The ratchet. Each response must exceed the last to remain credible, and the ceiling is fixed. The usable space shrinks with every round.
  • Timing passes to the adversary. A declared policy of automatic response means Pakistan chooses when India fights.
  • Internationalisation. Every crisis brings third-party involvement, which is precisely what the Simla position exists to prevent.
  • Opportunity cost. Crisis management consumes leadership attention and budget that India's larger competition — economic growth and the China frontier — requires.
  • Physical infrastructure is replaceable. Camps are rebuilt; the human and ideological infrastructure is untouched by a missile.

The conventional space under the ceiling

If general war is unavailable and doing nothing is unacceptable, everything depends on what lies in between. Announced as a doctrinal revision in April 2004 in direct response to the Operation Parakram mobilisation failure, “Cold Start” — never officially so named by the Army, which prefers “Proactive Strategy” — involves reorganising offensive capability into eight or so Integrated Battle Groups: self-contained combined-arms formations held close to the border, capable of launching multiple shallow thrusts within 48 to 96 hours. The logic is precise: shallow enough not to cross Pakistan's territorial threshold; fast enough to act before international intervention; multiple enough to complicate the defence; and limited enough to give Pakistan no target worth a nuclear response.

The problems are equally precise. Pakistan's answer was Nasr, a battlefield nuclear weapon designed precisely to make shallow armoured thrusts unattractive — a threat that is cheap to make whether or not it would ever be executed. Implementation has been partial, with test IBGs trialled from 2019. And India's official position has been to deny that “Cold Start” exists, which complicates its use as a deterrent: a doctrine you deny cannot signal.

Interpretation What India actually used instead tells the real story. 2016: Special Forces ground raids across the LoC. 2019: stand-off air power against a specified non-military target across the international boundary. 2025: multi-domain strike — air, missile and loitering munitions — against multiple named targets, with layered air defence protecting the homeland. The trend is away from ground manoeuvre: India has found its usable space in precision stand-off strike rather than in armoured thrusts, because stand-off strike is faster to execute, easier to calibrate, easier to terminate and far less likely to present a target worth a nuclear response.

Escalation control: the phrase that matters

Every Indian action since 2016 has been accompanied by language designed to cap it: “no plans for further operations” (2016), “non-military, pre-emptive” (2019), “focused, measured and non-escalatory” and “no Pakistani military facilities were targeted” (2025). Interpretation This is deliberate escalation management conducted through public statement — telling the adversary, in advance and in public, that the action is bounded, so that he can absorb it without being forced by his own domestic audience to respond. Noticing that pattern and naming it is the single most sophisticated observation available on this topic, and it takes fifteen seconds to say.

Part VI

The military dimension: the line, the bomb, the drones

The LoC: anatomy of a line

Seven hundred and forty kilometres of mountain, forest and snow, held continuously by both armies for more than half a century. Understanding how it is physically held is what separates a candidate who has read about the LoC from one who could be posted to it.

The obstacle system

  • The Anti-Infiltration Obstacle System (AIOS) — universally called “the fence” — was largely constructed between 2003 and 2004, covering roughly 700 km of the LoC. It is typically a double row of concertina and electrified fencing eight to twelve feet high, with motion sensors, thermal imaging devices, alarms and, in newer stretches, radar and camera integration.
  • It is deliberately set back from the Line of Control, often by hundreds of metres to several kilometres, both to comply with the requirement not to alter the line and to allow construction under cover. The consequence is that there is Indian territory in front of the fence, which soldiers must patrol.
  • It requires annual reconstruction in many sectors — snow, avalanche and landslide destroy long stretches every winter, and repair work in spring is conducted under observation and, historically, under fire.
  • Established The fence, combined with the 2003 ceasefire, reduced infiltration dramatically. It did not end it — routes shifted to the unfenceable gaps (rivers, gorges, high passes), to the Jammu IB, to the sea, and latterly to tunnels and drones.
  • Upgrades: the Comprehensive Integrated Border Management System (CIBMS), deployed in pilot stretches on the Jammu IB and elsewhere, integrating radar, thermal imagers, underground sensors, laser barriers and a command-and-control overlay — a "smart fence" intended to detect rather than merely to obstruct.

What ceasefire violations actually are

The phrase covers several distinct activities and a good answer distinguishes them: covering fire to support an infiltration attempt across a specific gap; sniping and Border Action Team raids intended to inflict casualties and dominate; speculative or retaliatory artillery and mortar exchange, which historically caused the majority of civilian casualties and displacement in villages on both sides; and harassment of construction and repair parties working on the fence, bunkers and roads. Disputed Violation counts differ wildly between Indian and Pakistani figures because each counts the other's fire and not its own; use them only with attribution.

The civilians on the line

An often-overlooked point that plays very well in an interview: villages sit within a few hundred metres of the LoC on both sides. India has built individual and community bunkers in thousands across the border districts of Poonch, Rajouri, Jammu, Samba and Kathua — a programme substantially expanded after 2017 — because shelling displaces farming communities and destroys the local economy. The people of Poonch and Rajouri have been living within artillery range for seventy-eight years. Mentioning them, and the bunker programme, shows you are thinking about the population rather than only about the fence.

The ceasefires of 2003 and 2021

Two of the most under-appreciated events in the relationship. Both were agreed at the military level, both held far better than anyone expected, and both illustrate something important about who can actually deliver an agreement in Pakistan.

2003

On 26 November 2003, following a proposal by Pakistan's Prime Minister Zafarullah Khan Jamali and Indian acceptance, a ceasefire came into effect along the LoC, the AGPL and the international boundary in Jammu. It was agreed without a written treaty — an understanding between the two militaries. Established It held substantially for roughly a decade, during which artillery exchange almost ceased, the fence was built, infiltration fell sharply, the cross-LoC bus and trade began, and violence in J&K declined to its lowest level since 1989. It eroded from about 2013 and had effectively collapsed by 2016-2020, with violation counts in the thousands annually.

2021

On 25 February 2021, the two Directors General of Military Operations issued a joint statement agreeing to strict observance of all agreements and ceasefire along the LoC and all other sectors, with effect from midnight of 24-25 February. Established This held remarkably well — violation figures fell by well over ninety per cent and largely remained low, and the relative quiet on the line persisted through periods of otherwise frozen political relations. It was substantially strained during and after the May 2025 exchanges. Re-verify its current status.

Why the 2021 ceasefire matters analytically

  • It was reached at the military level and reportedly through a quiet back-channel involving the two national security establishments — not through a political summit. That is consistent with everything in Section 9 about where authority actually lies.
  • It held despite zero political relations, downgraded diplomatic missions, no trade, and no dialogue. This is direct evidence that military-to-military channels are the most resilient instrument in the relationship, and it is the strongest available argument for keeping the DGMO hotline and the flag-meeting mechanism alive regardless of the political weather.
  • Interpretation The most-cited explanations for Pakistan's interest are: the cost of a two-front posture given its western border crisis with the TTP; FATF pressure requiring visible good behaviour; the economic crisis; and, for India, the desirability of quiet on one frontier while eastern Ladakh was active after 2020. Note that both parties had reasons — an agreement that serves only one side's interest does not hold for four years.
  • The operational dividend was real: border villages returned to normal farming, bunker use fell, and the Army could reallocate effort from responding to fire towards counter-infiltration and hinterland operations.
A GD point worth having

“The 2021 ceasefire is the best evidence we have that limited agreements with Pakistan are possible and can hold — but notice what kind of agreement it was. No treaty, no summit, no signing ceremony, no political capital invested, agreed between two DGMOs, and specific enough to be verifiable. Every grand political initiative in this relationship since 1999 has failed; the modest, technical, military-level arrangement held for four years. If I were advising on process, I would conclude that with Pakistan we should attempt small verifiable things through channels the army controls, rather than large symbolic things through channels it does not.”

The stability-instability paradox

The concept you must be able to explain

The term is Glenn Snyder's, from the Cold War, and it is the single most useful idea in this subject. Stability at the nuclear level produces instability at lower levels. Because both sides are confident that neither wants a nuclear exchange, each becomes more willing to conduct limited violence below that threshold — precisely because it is confident the other will not escalate to the top.

Applied here: Pakistan's nuclear capability made its sub-conventional campaign safer, not riskier. The evidence is chronological and hard to dismiss — Kargil came a year after the tests; 26/11 came ten years after; the most intense period of proxy warfare in Kashmir coincided exactly with the nuclearisation of the subcontinent. Interpretation Most serious analysts on both sides accept the mechanism, while disagreeing about how much of it is a deliberate Pakistani strategy versus an emergent effect.

India's answer since 2016 has been to contest the paradox — to demonstrate that there is usable conventional space between "do nothing" and "general war," and that Pakistani nuclear signalling does not close it. Balakot and Sindoor are, in this reading, arguments about where the threshold actually is. The risk is obvious: the only way to find the threshold is to approach it.

The No First Use debate

Keep NFU

  • It is stabilising: it removes any incentive for the adversary to strike first out of fear of being pre-empted.
  • It underpins India's international standing as a responsible nuclear power — the 2008 NSG waiver was won partly on it.
  • It permits a smaller, cheaper, less alert arsenal: India does not need launch-on-warning, hair-trigger readiness or pre-delegation, all of which are accident-prone.
  • It is credible precisely because the triad is complete — a survivable second strike is what makes NFU a posture rather than a hope.
  • Abandoning it would concede a Pakistani argument and gain nothing India cannot already do.

Review NFU

  • It concedes the first move and requires India to absorb a nuclear strike before responding.
  • Against an adversary with battlefield weapons and no NFU, a declaratory commitment may reduce deterrent pressure at exactly the level where it is needed.
  • Senior Indian figures have publicly raised the question, including Manohar Parrikar as Defence Minister in 2016 (speaking in a personal capacity) and Rajnath Singh in 2019 (“what happens in future depends on the circumstances”).
  • Interpretation Some scholars argue India's actual posture has drifted towards a counterforce or damage-limitation orientation, or towards an implicit first-use option against a Pakistani tactical weapon used on Indian soil — a position sometimes called "massive retaliation against any use, including tactical."
If asked “should India abandon No First Use?”

“Sir, I would keep it, and I would say why. First, NFU is only meaningful if you can survive a first strike and retaliate — and since Arihant's deterrence patrol in 2018 India has a completed triad, so we can. Second, abandoning it would require a much larger, more alert and more expensive force posture, with all the accident risk that brings, and it would not give us a capability we lack. Third, it is a genuine diplomatic asset that helped secure the 2008 waiver. What I would say is that the doctrine already answers the tactical-weapon problem: India's declared position is massive retaliation against any nuclear use against Indian forces or territory, including a battlefield weapon used on Indian soil. That removes the sanctuary that a tactical weapon is supposed to create, without giving up NFU. I would keep the policy and keep the ambiguity about what constitutes unacceptable damage.”

The four structural risks

  1. Time. Ballistic missile flight times between the two countries are of the order of three to eight minutes. Neither has strategic early warning of the kind the superpowers built. Decision time in a crisis is measured in minutes, by human beings, on incomplete information.
  2. Dual-use ambiguity. Several delivery systems can carry either a conventional or a nuclear warhead, and the launching side cannot signal which. A conventional missile strike may be read as a nuclear one.
  3. Command under pressure. Tactical weapons imply either pre-delegation of authority to field commanders or a slow release process that makes them useless — both of which are dangerous in different ways.

  • Agreement on the Prohibition of Attack against Nuclear Installations and Facilities — signed 31 December 1988, in force January 1991. The fact that will be remembered

“Sir, one thing I find remarkable: since 1992, India and Pakistan have exchanged lists of their nuclear installations on the first of January every single year without a break — through Kargil, through the 2001-02 mobilisation, after 26/11, after Balakot, and after Operation Sindoor. It is the only agreement in this relationship that has never once failed. I think that tells you something important: both establishments are entirely capable of sustaining technical arrangements that serve their own interest, even when the political relationship is at zero. If we are looking for where progress is possible, that is the category to look in.”

Drones, tunnels and hybrid warfare

The fence solved the problem of men walking across a line. It does nothing about a quadcopter at 400 feet. The border has moved into the third dimension, and the counter is still being built.

What changed

  • Established The first drone attack on an Indian military installation took place on 27 June 2021, when two improvised explosive devices were dropped by small UAVs on the Jammu Air Force Station, damaging a building and injuring two personnel. It was a small incident with very large implications.
  • The dominant use, however, is logistics rather than attack: small commercial-derivative drones dropping packages of weapons, ammunition, IED components, satellite phones and — in very large volumes — heroin across the Punjab and Jammu borders. BSF detection and shoot-down figures have risen from a handful in 2019 to several hundred a year by 2023-24. Re-verify the latest annual figures.
  • The economics are brutal for the defender: a payload-carrying quadcopter costs a few lakh rupees; the radar, jammer and interceptor architecture to reliably defeat it costs orders of magnitude more per kilometre of frontage. This cost-exchange ratio is the central problem of counter-drone defence everywhere in the world, not only here.
  • Operation Sindoor in May 2025 saw the first large-scale use of armed drones and loitering munitions by both sides in a India-Pakistan exchange — a qualitative change from delivery to strike.

India's counter-UAS effort

  • Detection: low-level lightweight radars, RF detection of controller links, acoustic and electro-optical sensors.
  • Soft kill: RF and GNSS jamming, spoofing, and protocol takeover. Effective against commercial drones, much less so against pre-programmed or fibre-guided ones.
  • Hard kill: guns, nets, interceptor drones, and directed-energy systems. DRDO's anti-drone systems, including a laser-based Directed Energy Weapon, have been deployed and were reportedly employed in May 2025. Re-verify
  • Regulatory: the Drone Rules 2021, mandatory registration, the digital sky platform, geo-fencing of sensitive airspace, and restrictions on drone imports.
  • Structural: counter-drone has been pushed down to unit level — this is now an infantry battalion's problem at the LoC and a BSF company's problem on the IB, not an air-defence-command problem.
A first-rate point for a lecturette on future warfare

“The LoC fence took two years to build and reduced infiltration by most of its volume. A five-lakh-rupee quadcopter flies over it in ninety seconds. That is the whole of modern warfare's asymmetry problem in one sentence — the defender has to be right everywhere and pays a hundred times more per engagement than the attacker. What it means practically is that we cannot buy our way out of this with a bigger fence. The answers are detection at scale, cheap interception, and going after the launch network on the other side through intelligence rather than trying to catch every drone in the air. That is an intelligence problem wearing a technology costume.”

Part VII

The wider contest: water, China, America, collapse

The Indus Waters Treaty and Sir Creek

The most successful and most durable agreement in the history of the relationship — it survived 1965, 1971 and 1999 — and, since April 2025, the most uncertain. A near-certain interview question.

What the treaty does

The Indus Waters Treaty was signed at Karachi on 19 September 1960 by Jawaharlal Nehru and Mohammad Ayub Khan, with the World Bank as a signatory in a limited capacity and as broker after nine years of negotiation. Its architecture is a partition rather than a sharing formula, which is why it worked:

  • Eastern rivers — Ravi, Beas, Sutlej — allocated to India for unrestricted use, approximately 33 million acre-feet (about 20% of the system's flow).
  • Western rivers — Indus, Jhelum, Chenab — allocated to Pakistan, approximately 135 million acre-feet (about 80%).
  • India retains limited rights on the western rivers: domestic and non-consumptive use, run-of-the-river hydroelectric generation, storage up to about 3.6 MAF, and irrigation of up to about 1.34 million acres — with detailed technical constraints on design in Annexures D and E.
  • A Permanent Indus Commission with a commissioner from each side, required to meet at least once a year and to exchange data.
  • A three-tier dispute mechanism: the Commission → a Neutral Expert appointed by the World Bank for technical “differences” → a Court of Arbitration for legal “disputes.”

The disputes you should know

  • Salal (Chenab) — resolved bilaterally in 1978 with design modifications; the model of how the treaty was meant to work.
  • Baglihar (Chenab) — referred to a Neutral Expert, who ruled in 2007, upholding some Pakistani objections on design and rejecting others. Both sides claimed vindication; the project was built with modifications.
  • Kishanganga (a Jhelum tributary) — a Court of Arbitration at The Hague ruled in 2013 that India could divert water for power generation but must maintain a minimum environmental flow. India built the project.
  • Ratle (Chenab) — Pakistan sought a Court of Arbitration while India sought a Neutral Expert; the World Bank in 2022 controversially allowed both processes to proceed in parallel, which India objected to as legally incoherent. The Neutral Expert ruled in January 2025 that he was competent to decide the questions. Re-verify
  • India's modification notices — issued in January 2023 and again in August-September 2024 under Article XII(3), seeking renegotiation on grounds including climate change, demographic pressure, the need for clean energy, and Pakistan's conduct of the dispute-resolution process.

“In abeyance,” April 2025

Re-verify On 23 April 2025, the day after Pahalgam, India announced that the treaty would be held “in abeyance with immediate effect, until Pakistan credibly and irrevocably abjures its support for cross-border terrorism.” In practice this has meant suspension of data sharing, suspension of Permanent Indus Commission meetings, and the removal of design-notification constraints on Indian projects, together with reservoir flushing and drawdown operations on existing Chenab projects that had previously been constrained.

The case for using the water instrument

  • It is non-kinetic: it imposes real cost without risking escalation to war, which no military option can claim.
  • It is sustained rather than a one-off strike, and therefore creates continuing pressure rather than a single event that can be absorbed.
  • Roughly 80% of Pakistan's irrigated agriculture depends on the Indus system; it is the point of greatest leverage India possesses.
  • India has underused its own permitted entitlements for decades — storage, irrigation and run-of-river generation — partly for fear of controversy. Fully exercising rights India already holds under the treaty is not a breach of it.
  • The argument of reciprocity: a treaty's continuation presupposes the counterparty's good faith in the wider relationship.

The case against

  • India lacks the physical capacity to stop the water. The western rivers carry enormous volumes; India has very limited storage on them and building it would take fifteen to twenty years and vast capital. The instrument is therefore more symbolic than hydraulic in the short term — and a threat that cannot be executed is a weak threat.
  • Precedent. India is a lower riparian to China on the Brahmaputra and the Sutlej. Establishing that an upper riparian may suspend a water treaty for political reasons is a precedent China would be delighted to cite, particularly with the Medog project under construction.
  • Reputational cost. The treaty is cited worldwide as a model of transboundary water cooperation; abandoning it damages India's standing as a rules-abiding power at a time when that standing is an asset.
  • Humanitarian and escalatory risk. Pakistani officials have stated that interference with water flows would be treated as an act of war. Targeting the water supply of 240 million people is an instrument with consequences that are hard to bound.
  • It may not work. The record suggests economic pressure has not changed Pakistan Army behaviour; there is no strong reason to expect water pressure to be different.
The balanced answer

“Sir, I would distinguish three things that get run together. First, fully using India's existing entitlements under the treaty — storage, run-of-river projects, the 1.34 million acres of irrigation — is something we are entitled to do and have under-used for sixty years; I would do that regardless. Second, suspending data sharing and Commission meetings, which is what abeyance has meant in practice, is a pressure signal with limited physical effect. Third, actually stopping the western rivers is not currently possible — we do not have the storage, and building it would take two decades. So I would support the second as leverage and prioritise the first as policy, while being honest that the third is not an available option today. I would also be careful about precedent, because we are the lower riparian on the Brahmaputra, and China is watching what we establish here.”

That answer demonstrates hydrological literacy, strategic awareness and the ability to distinguish a signal from a capability — three things almost no candidate demonstrates on this question.

Sir Creek and the maritime boundary

A 96-kilometre tidal channel in a marsh, unresolved since 1947, whose settlement would unlock thousands of square kilometres of exclusive economic zone. The smallest of the disputes and the most technically soluble.

  • What it is: a tidal estuary in the Rann of Kutch marshlands, dividing Gujarat from Sindh, navigable only at high tide and shifting with the seasons.
  • The dispute: Pakistan relies on a 1914 resolution of the Government of Bombay settling a dispute between the Rao of Kutch and the Sind government, and on a map showing the boundary along the eastern bank — giving Pakistan the whole creek. India argues that the same 1914 resolution's text refers to the creek being divided, and invokes the thalweg principle of international law, under which a boundary in a navigable channel runs along the mid-channel deepest line.
  • Why it matters: the land area at stake is small and largely uninhabitable. The maritime consequence is not — the terminus of the land boundary determines the baseline from which the territorial sea, the EEZ and the continental shelf are measured, and the seabed is assessed to have hydrocarbon potential. The area affected runs to thousands of square kilometres of EEZ.
  • Human cost: fishermen from both countries are routinely arrested for straying across an undefined line in an unmarked sea, and their boats confiscated. Hundreds are held at any given time. The annual 1 January and 1 July exchange of prisoner lists under the 2008 agreement exists largely because of this.
  • Status: a joint survey was conducted in 2007 and technical-level talks have been held repeatedly. Interpretation Sir Creek is very widely described as the most solvable of the India-Pakistan disputes — the technical work is largely done — and it remains unsolved because neither side wishes to make a visible concession on any territorial matter while Kashmir is unresolved.
Why this is a good answer in a GD on “can India and Pakistan resolve anything?”

“Sir Creek is the test case. The technical work is done, the area is largely uninhabited, both navies would benefit from a defined line, and it would end the fishermen problem overnight. It has not been settled — which tells you the obstacle in this relationship is not technical difficulty. It is that neither government can be seen to concede anything territorial while Kashmir is open. Until that changes, I would not expect progress even on the easy problems, and I would concentrate on arrangements that require no concession — the ceasefire, the hotlines, the nuclear lists, the prisoner exchanges. Those work.”

China-Pakistan: the all-weather axis

The single factor that converts a manageable bilateral problem into a structural strategic one. If you take one thing from this section, take the phrase “two-front” and the understanding of what it actually means.

The two-front problem, precisely

Distinguish three things, because they are routinely confused and the differences are enormous:

  1. A two-front war — simultaneous offensive operations by China and Pakistan. Assessed as the least likely and most demanding contingency, but the one against which force structure must be sized.
  2. A collusive threat — one adversary providing intelligence, equipment, diplomatic cover and resupply while the other fights. Interpretation This is the realistic form, and it was visible in 2025.
  3. A two-front posture — the standing requirement to hold and equip forces for two frontiers simultaneously, which is a permanent budgetary and manpower fact regardless of whether either front is active. This is the one that actually costs India money every single day.

India's institutional responses: raising and reorienting formations for the northern border after 2020; the Mountain Strike Corps; the CDS and the theatreisation debate; accelerated border infrastructure under the BRO; and an emphasis on the maritime flank, where the balance favours India, as a source of asymmetric leverage against China.

The formulation to use

“Sir, I would not describe it as two separate problems. Since 2020 the correct framing is one adversary with two arms. What makes it structural rather than situational is that Pakistan's dependence on China is now close to total — about 80% of its arms imports, its principal creditor, its diplomatic shield at the 1267 Committee, and an economic corridor running through territory India claims. That dependence means Pakistani strategic choices are increasingly made with Chinese interests in view. The practical consequence for India is that we can no longer plan a Pakistan contingency in isolation, and that is the single largest driver of our force structure and of our defence budget.”

FATF and financial pressure

The most effective non-military instrument India has ever used against Pakistan, and the one candidates know least about. Learn the mechanism, the dates and the honest limits.

What FATF is

The Financial Action Task Force, established by the G7 in 1989 and based in Paris, is the global standard-setter for anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorist-financing (AML/CFT). It has 40 Recommendations and conducts mutual evaluations of members. Its two lists:

  • The “grey list” — formally, jurisdictions under increased monitoring. The country commits to an action plan with deadlines. The effect is not a sanction but a market signal: enhanced due diligence by international banks, higher transaction costs and risk premia, slower correspondent banking, and difficulty accessing international capital.
  • The “black list” — high-risk jurisdictions subject to a call for counter-measures. Currently Iran, North Korea and Myanmar.

Pakistan's record

  • 2008 · 2012-2015Earlier periods on the grey list, with removal each time after action plans were assessed as completed
  • June 2018Grey-listed again, on a nomination driven by the United States, the United Kingdom, France and Germany, with India's sustained advocacy behind it. A 27-point action plan, later expanded to 34 items
  • 2019-2022Pakistan legislated extensively — amendments to the Anti-Terrorism Act and the Anti-Money Laundering Act, asset freezes, and the prosecution of Hafiz Saeed and Zaki-ur-Rehman Lakhvi in terror-financing cases resulting in lengthy sentences
  • 21 Oct 2022Removed from the grey list after an on-site visit confirmed completion of the action plans. India's public position was that removal was based on technical compliance and that Pakistan should continue to act against UN-designated terrorists and entities
  • 2023-presentRe-verify India has continued to press for restoration of scrutiny, particularly after Pahalgam. Check the current FATF plenary outcome before your interview

Why it worked

  • Interpretation Pakistani officials themselves estimated the cost of grey-listing at tens of billions of dollars in lost investment and higher borrowing costs over the four years.
  • It produced verifiable legislative and prosecutorial action that thirty years of Indian dossiers had not — convictions, asset seizures and the formal banning of organisations.
  • It is multilateral and technical, so it cannot be dismissed as Indian propaganda. It was not India accusing Pakistan; it was thirty-nine countries assessing a compliance checklist.
  • It imposed cost without any risk of escalation — the entire attraction of the instrument.

Its limits

  • FATF assesses legal and procedural compliance, not intent or outcomes. A country can pass by legislating and prosecuting selectively.
  • It is a technical body and India cannot control it; listing and de-listing require consensus and are influenced by geopolitics. China, Turkey and Malaysia were reported as supporting Pakistan in the process.
  • Delisting in 2022 removed the pressure at precisely the moment Pakistan's economic crisis made it most susceptible.
  • Attacks continued during grey-listing — Pahalgam came after delisting, but Pulwama came during a period of listing.
Interview edge

“Sir, I would argue FATF grey-listing between 2018 and 2022 did more measurable damage to the terror-financing ecosystem than any military action we have taken — it produced convictions of Hafiz Saeed and Lakhvi, asset freezes and legislation, which thirty years of dossiers had not. And it cost us nothing and risked nothing. Its weakness is that it measures compliance rather than intent, so a state can legislate its way off the list while leaving the structures intact, which is broadly what India argued in 2022. I would say the lesson is to keep using the instrument, to keep building the evidentiary case that supports it, and not to expect it to work alone.”

Pakistan's internal crisis and Afghanistan

The most under-discussed factor in the entire subject. A great deal of Pakistan's external behaviour over the next decade will be driven by what is happening inside it — and much of it is India's problem whether India wishes it or not.

The economy

  • Re-verify Pakistan has entered more than twenty IMF programmes since 1958 — among the highest of any member. The 2022-23 crisis brought foreign exchange reserves to a few weeks of import cover, inflation above 30%, and a near-default; a US$7 billion Extended Fund Facility was agreed in September 2024.
  • Structural problems: a tax-to-GDP ratio around 9-10%, among the lowest in the world; an agricultural sector and a large retail and property sector effectively outside the tax net; chronic circular debt in the power sector; and debt service consuming the majority of federal revenue.
  • The comparison that matters. At independence, per capita incomes were broadly comparable and Pakistan's was higher for parts of the 1960s. Established India's economy is now roughly ten to eleven times larger in nominal terms. The defence budget gap is of a similar order. This divergence, not any military instrument, is the most consequential development in the relationship since 1971.

The internal security crisis

  • The Tehrik-i-Taliban Pakistan (TTP) was formed in December 2007 from groups in the tribal areas. Its campaign has killed tens of thousands of Pakistani civilians and security personnel.
  • The Army Public School attack at Peshawar on 16 December 2014 killed about 149 people, 132 of them children. It produced the National Action Plan — twenty points including military courts, madrassa regulation, action against all militant groups “without discrimination,” and curbs on hate speech. Interpretation Pakistani analysts widely assess the NAP as having been implemented selectively against groups that attack Pakistan and not against those that operate outward. That distinction is the heart of the “good terrorist / bad terrorist” critique, and it is made most forcefully by Pakistani commentators themselves.
  • Balochistan: a long-running nationalist insurgency, driven by grievances over resource extraction, enforced disappearances and political exclusion. The Baloch Liberation Army and others have attacked CPEC projects and Chinese nationals repeatedly. Re-verify The hijacking of the Jaffar Express in March 2025 was the most spectacular such operation. Pakistan alleges Indian involvement; India denies it.
  • The Pashtun Tahafuz Movement represents a significant non-violent civil-rights challenge, and the state's response to it has been heavily criticised domestically.
  • Sectarian violence against Shia, Ahmadi, Hindu and Christian communities remains a persistent feature.
  • After the Taliban's return to Kabul in 2021, TTP attacks on Pakistan rose sharply, and Pakistan-Afghanistan relations deteriorated into cross-border strikes and mass deportations of Afghan refugees — the precise opposite of the “strategic depth” the policy was designed to achieve.

Civil-military and political instability

Re-verify Imran Khan was removed by a no-confidence vote in April 2022 and subsequently convicted and imprisoned; the February 2024 elections produced a contested result and a coalition government; the army's political role has if anything expanded, with the Special Investment Facilitation Council institutionalising its role in economic decisions and the Chief of Army Staff promoted to Field Marshal in May 2025. Why a candidate should care — and the answer to “should India want Pakistan to collapse?”

This question is asked more often than you would expect, and the wrong answer is instantly disqualifying. “No, sir, and I would say that as a matter of Indian interest rather than sentiment. A collapsing nuclear-armed state of 240 million people on our border is the single worst outcome available to us — loose weapons, uncontrolled refugee flows, an ungoverned space for groups that already target us, and a Chinese intervention we could not prevent. What India needs is not a failed Pakistan but a Pakistan in which the army's institutional interest is no longer served by hostility. That is a slow project and it is mostly an economic one. Meanwhile I think our strongest card is simply the growth differential — our economy is now ten or eleven times theirs, and that gap does more to change their calculations every year than any strike does.”

Afghanistan, the Taliban and the TTP

The theatre in which Pakistan's proxy doctrine was invented, and the one in which it has most visibly failed. Worth knowing because it is the cleanest available demonstration that the instrument turns on its user.

  • The Durand Line — drawn in 1893 between British India and Afghanistan, running 2,670 km and dividing the Pashtun population. No Afghan government, including the Taliban, has ever accepted it as an international border. This is the foundational Pakistani insecurity on its western frontier and it explains far more Pakistani behaviour than is generally recognised.
  • “Strategic depth” — the doctrine, associated with General Aslam Beg and the 1980s, of securing a friendly Afghanistan to provide rearward space and to deny India influence on Pakistan's western flank.
  • Pursued through support to the Afghan mujahideen in the 1980s, to the Taliban from 1994 and its capture of Kabul in 1996, and to the Haqqani network throughout the post-2001 period while simultaneously being a US ally.
  • The outcome. The Taliban returned to Kabul in August 2021 — apparently the achievement of the objective. Within two years, TTP attacks on Pakistan had risen sharply, the Afghan Taliban had refused to act against the TTP or to accept the Durand Line, Pakistan was conducting air strikes inside Afghanistan, and it had begun mass deportation of Afghan refugees. Interpretation Strategic depth produced strategic exposure. It is the best-documented case anywhere of proxy blowback, and it is the strongest single argument that can be made to a Pakistani interlocutor in that country's own interest.

India's position

  • Pre-2021: India invested approximately US$3 billion in Afghan reconstruction — the Afghan-India Friendship Dam (Salma Dam) in Herat, the Zaranj-Delaram highway connecting to the Iranian border, the Afghan Parliament building, the Stor Palace restoration, hospitals, schools and thousands of scholarships. India had significant public goodwill and no military footprint.
  • India developed Chabahar port in Iran precisely to reach Afghanistan and Central Asia bypassing Pakistan, which has consistently refused transit rights for Indian goods.
  • Post-2021: India closed its embassy, then returned a “technical team” to Kabul in June 2022 to oversee humanitarian assistance — wheat, medicines and vaccines. Re-verify Engagement has since deepened, including ministerial-level contact in 2025 and the upgrading of the mission; India has not extended formal recognition, consistent with the position of almost all states.
  • India's interests: denying anti-India groups sanctuary, retaining connectivity to Central Asia, protecting its development stake, and — a factor rarely stated but important — the fact that Taliban-Pakistan friction is, from a cold strategic standpoint, favourable to India.

The point to make

“Afghanistan is the proof of concept for the argument that this instrument does not stay pointed where you aim it. Pakistan spent forty years building a friendly government in Kabul, achieved it in 2021, and within two years was bombing its territory, facing a sharp rise in attacks at home, and deporting three million Afghans. The Durand Line is still not accepted. I would make that argument to a Pakistani audience rather than to an Indian one — it is not an Indian talking point, it is an empirical observation that their own analysts make.”

“Should India talk to Pakistan?”

The strongest available answer refuses the binary. “Sir, I would ask ‘talk about what, and with whom.' Trade, connectivity and the economics of hostility

Two neighbours of a combined 1.6 billion people conduct almost no trade with each other. The economics of the relationship are a study in foregone opportunity — and in why economic interdependence has failed to do the work its advocates predicted.

  • India granted Pakistan Most Favoured Nation status under WTO rules in 1996. Pakistan never reciprocated, maintaining instead a restricted positive list. This asymmetry ran for twenty-three years.
  • Bilateral trade at its peak was around US$2.4 billion a year — trivial for economies of this size, and dwarfed by the estimated informal trade routed through Dubai, Singapore and Afghanistan, commonly estimated at a similar or larger figure.
  • 15 February 2019: after Pulwama, India withdrew MFN status and imposed a 200% customs duty on all Pakistani goods — effectively ending imports.
  • August 2019: after the constitutional changes, Pakistan suspended bilateral trade entirely and downgraded diplomatic relations. A 2021 Pakistani cabinet decision to resume sugar and cotton imports was reversed within a day.
  • May 2025: the Attari-Wagah land route closed; remaining indirect trade routes restricted. Re-verify
  • Transit is the deeper issue. Pakistan has consistently refused overland transit for Indian goods to Afghanistan and Central Asia. This is why India built Chabahar in Iran and pursues the International North-South Transport Corridor. Interpretation Pakistan's refusal of transit costs Pakistan transit revenues and costs India time and money — the clearest available illustration that this relationship is not governed by economic rationality.

What normal trade would be worth

Interpretation World Bank and other studies have estimated the potential of India-Pakistan trade under normal conditions at US$25-37 billion annually, against actual figures near zero. The gains would be asymmetric and would favour Pakistan proportionally far more, since India's economy is roughly ten times larger. That asymmetry is itself part of the explanation: economic integration with a much larger neighbour is read in Rawalpindi not as opportunity but as dependence, and dependence is precisely what an establishment defined by resistance to India cannot accept. This is why the liberal theory that trade produces peace has failed here, and being able to say why is far more impressive than simply asserting that it has failed.

The growth differential as an instrument

“Sir, I think India's most powerful instrument against Pakistan is not military at all and does not involve Pakistan. In 1947 our per capita incomes were comparable. Today India's economy is roughly ten to eleven times larger, and the gap widens every year. That differential does three things no strike can do: it makes our defence spending sustainable and theirs not; it makes us a partner every major power wants and them a problem every major power manages; and it steadily refutes the proposition the Pakistani state was founded on. It is slow, it is unglamorous, and it is the only instrument in this entire subject that compounds. So my answer to ‘what should India do about Pakistan' would start with ‘grow at 7% for twenty years and keep the border secure while we do it.'”

Part VIII

Analysis: narratives, controversies, scenarios, options

Competing narratives

Five coherent accounts of the same seventy-eight years. You will be asked to take a position. Take one — but demonstrate first that you know what the others say, because that is what distinguishes a considered position from a repeated one.

1 · The mainstream Indian account

India accepted Partition, secured a lawful accession from the competent authority, and was invaded. It referred the matter to the UN in good faith and accepted resolutions whose first step Pakistan never took. It has fought four defensive wars, has never initiated one, and has never annexed territory it took. Since 1989 it has faced a state-sponsored terrorist campaign that has killed tens of thousands of its citizens, and has responded with thirty years of restraint before adopting proportionate, targeted and legally framed retaliation.

Strongest evidence: the Instrument of Accession; the sequencing of the UN resolutions; the Junagadh comparison; Kargil's launch three months after Lahore; the convictions in the 26/11 and Pulwama cases; Pakistan's own FATF action plan.

Weakest point: it tends to treat Kashmiri political grievance as entirely manufactured, which the 1953 dismissal and the 1987 election do not support.

2 · The Pakistani official account

Partition's logic required a Muslim-majority contiguous territory to join Pakistan. India secured an accession from a fleeing autocrat, promised a plebiscite at the UN and never held it, and has held the Valley by force ever since. The movement in Kashmir is indigenous, and Pakistan extends only moral, political and diplomatic support. India's 2019 actions were unilateral and unlawful. India's strikes are aggression dressed as counter-terrorism.

Strongest evidence: Nehru's public statements on a reference to the people; the Security Council's continuing seisin of the matter; India's own conduct at Junagadh; documented human-rights concerns in the Valley.

Weakest point: it cannot explain the ISI's destruction of the pro-independence JKLF in favour of a pro-accession group, the Punjabi composition of LeT and JeM, Kargil's regular Northern Light Infantry troops, or why Pakistan itself convicted Hafiz Saeed and Lakhvi if no support existed.

3 · The Kashmiri accounts — plural

There is no single Kashmiri narrative and pretending otherwise is the most common error. At least four exist: the pro-India mainstream of the National Conference and PDP, which argues for autonomy within India and regards 1953 and 1987 as broken promises; the separatist/azadi position, itself split between independence and accession to Pakistan; the Kashmiri Pandit account, of a community ethnically cleansed and abandoned by both the state and the separatist movement that claimed to speak for the Valley; and the Jammu and Ladakh accounts, which have their own grievances about Valley dominance and have generally supported integration.

Its contribution: it insists that the dispute has a population with agency, not merely a territory with a claim.

Its difficulty: the four positions are mutually incompatible, which is exactly why no settlement has ever been designed that satisfies them all.

4 · The structural-realist account

Neither state is uniquely at fault. A partition that left an undefined line, a vast power asymmetry, and a smaller state whose military inherited the levers of government were together sufficient to produce permanent rivalry. The weaker party in such a dyad will always seek asymmetric means; the stronger will always seek to impose the status quo. Kashmir is where this is conducted, not why it occurs.

Its strength: it explains the persistence of the conflict across radically different governments in both countries, and the failure of every conciliatory initiative from either side.

Its weakness: it flattens moral distinctions that matter — sponsoring the killing of civilians is not merely the behaviour of a weaker power — and offers almost no guidance on what to do next.

5 · The ideological account

The dispute is not really about land at all. Pakistan was founded on the proposition that Hindus and Muslims cannot live in one state; a Muslim-majority territory content within a secular India is an existential refutation, and a successful pluralist India is a permanent one. On this reading the conflict is not resolvable by any territorial formula and can only be outlasted.

Its strength: it explains why enormous costs have been accepted for territory of limited economic value, and why economic logic has consistently failed.

Its weakness: Bangladesh in 1971 refuted the two-nation theory decisively without ending the conflict, which suggests ideology is not the whole story; and the reading risks becoming a licence for fatalism, which is not a policy.

How to take a position

A strong formulation: “Sir, I hold the Indian position, and the strongest single argument for it is the sequencing of the UN resolutions — Pakistan was required to withdraw first, never did, and every subsequent obligation including ours was conditional on it. But I do not think our record is spotless. The dismissal of Sheikh Abdullah in 1953 and the 1987 election were our own mistakes and they created the grievance that Pakistan then armed. I would rather argue India's case on the strong ground than pretend there is no weak ground — and I would add that acknowledging our mistakes is also how we fix them, which is why the restoration of the assembly in 2024 matters.”

That answer is patriotic, honest, analytically serious, and very hard to shake in cross-questioning.

Controversies

Six arguments that recur, plus a pointer to those settled elsewhere. Know the shape of each. You do not need a verdict on all of them — you need to know where the evidence is thin, and to say so.

  1. The arguments settled elsewhere in this handbook. Simla as a historic blunder, whether India should have taken all of Kashmir in 1948, 1965 or 1971, Haji Pir at Tashkent, whether India should have struck after 26/11, the merits of the Article 370 abrogation, the repeal of AFSPA, No First Use, and abrogating rather than suspending the Indus Waters Treaty are each argued in full in the relevant section. Know where each is argued; do not re-argue them here.
  2. Was referring Kashmir to the UN a mistake? Interpretation The criticism is that India internationalised a matter it regarded as settled while holding military advantage. The defence is that India was the complainant alleging aggression, that the Charter route was the available one in 1948, and that the military position in January 1948 was far less favourable than hindsight suggests. The fairest verdict: defensible at the time, costly in consequence, and the real error was accepting a ceasefire before Gilgit and Skardu were recovered.
  3. Was the IC-814 release justified? Disputed The government's position was that it had no viable rescue option at Kandahar, on Taliban-controlled territory, with 155 hostages. The consequence was Masood Azhar and JeM — Parliament, Pathankot, Pulwama. The honest formulation: a defensible decision under duress with catastrophic downstream cost, and the real failure was earlier, in allowing the aircraft to leave Amritsar where it had landed and could have been immobilised.
  4. Was Operation Parakram a failure? Mostly yes as executed, but it produced the January 2004 commitment and it produced Cold Start. A mobilisation that does not strike damages deterrence.
  5. Balakot's battle damage. Disputed Genuinely unresolved between official claims and open-source imagery analysis. Do not assert a figure. The strategic significance does not depend on it.
  6. The F-16 and Abhinandan. Disputed Unverified in both directions. Say so.
  7. Is a settlement possible at all? Interpretation The 2004-07 back-channel suggests yes, under specific conditions: a Pakistani leader who controls the army, an Indian government with a majority, both economies wanting quiet, and absolute secrecy. Those conditions have been met once in seventy-eight years. That is not the same as never.

Lesser-known facts

Use two or three of these, not all of them. A single well-placed detail that the board did not expect is worth more than a recitation.

  • Jammu and Kashmir signed a Standstill Agreement with Pakistan, not with India. Pakistan then blockaded the state, in breach of the agreement it had itself signed. Almost nobody knows this, and it is the strongest opening fact in any argument about 1947.
  • The raiders' two-day halt at Baramulla to loot probably cost Pakistan the Kashmir Valley. Srinagar airfield was undefended on 24 October 1947.
  • Stuart tanks were dismantled, carried over the mountains and reassembled to force Zoji La at 11,500 feet in November 1948 — the highest tank action in the world at the time, and a complete tactical surprise.
  • Operation Gibraltar failed because Kashmiri villagers reported the infiltrators to the Indian authorities. The people who were supposed to rise informed instead.
  • 4 December is Indian Navy Day because of Operation Trident, the missile-boat attack on Karachi in 1971.
  • The entire Siachen conflict arises from the phrase “thence north to the glaciers” in a 1949 ceasefire agreement. Four words, four decades, several thousand lives.
  • Kargil was first reported by a shepherd, Tashi Namgyal, looking for a lost yak.
  • India and Pakistan have exchanged lists of their nuclear installations on 1 January every year since 1992 without a single break — through Kargil, Parakram, 26/11, Balakot and Operation Sindoor. It is the most reliable thing in the relationship.
  • The LoC fence is not on the LoC. It is built inside Indian territory, sometimes kilometres inside, and Indian soldiers patrol the ground in front of it.
  • The Kartarpur Corridor has generally remained open through crises that closed everything else, including in 2019 and 2025. Re-verify
  • Pakistan has lost far more people to terrorism than India has — tens of thousands of civilians and security personnel since 2001 on its own government's figures. The instrument turned.
  • Rashtriya Rifles was raised in 1990 specifically so that the rest of the Army would not be permanently absorbed into counter-insurgency — an organisational decision of considerable foresight.
  • The 1963 Sino-Pakistan boundary agreement contains a clause providing for renegotiation after the settlement of the Kashmir dispute — which is an implicit acknowledgement by both parties that the territory's status is unsettled.
  • Turtuk and three other villages in the Shyok valley were captured by India in 1971 and are the only Balti-speaking villages under Indian administration. Some families were divided by the change of control and remain so.

Scenarios

These are analytical constructs for thinking, not predictions. Present them as such — a candidate who forecasts a war sounds unserious; one who reasons through contingencies sounds like an officer.

Six scenarios, from most to least likely
ScenarioWhat it looks likeKey considerations
1 · Armed coexistence with periodic crises
Most likely		 No dialogue, no trade, a broadly holding ceasefire, continued low-level violence in J&K, and a major attack every two to four years followed by a calibrated Indian strike and a rapid return to the baseline. Essentially the present. Sustainable but corrosive. Each cycle starts one rung higher, leadership attention is consumed, and the risk of miscalculation accumulates even though no single crisis is likely to escalate.
2 · A mass-casualty attack and a larger Indian response An attack comparable to or worse than 26/11, triggering a response larger than Sindoor — multiple days, deeper targets, possibly naval and cyber dimensions. The central question is escalation control on both sides under domestic pressure. India's declared “act of war” policy makes a large response near-automatic, which is deterrence when it works and a trap when it does not. Third-party pressure would be intense and fast.
3 · Escalation to limited conventional conflict A short, sharp conflict of days to weeks across the IB or LoC, with air, missile, drone and possibly naval action, terminated by exhaustion and external pressure. Both sides have incentives to keep it short. India's advantages are depth, economy and the maritime flank; Pakistan's are interior lines and the escalation threat. Interpretation Nobody has ever fought a conventional war between nuclear powers to a conclusion, so all confidence here is theoretical.
4 · Internal collapse in Pakistan Compounding economic, political and security crisis producing loss of state writ in significant areas, mass movement of people, and contested control of parts of the territory. Bad for India, not good. Nuclear security, refugee flows, uncontrolled groups and probable Chinese intervention. India's interest is a stable Pakistan with different incentives, not a weak one.
5 · Collusive two-front contingency Simultaneous or sequential pressure from China and Pakistan, or Chinese intelligence, materiel and diplomatic support during an India-Pakistan crisis. Note that the framework already exists on paper — which is why analysts call this a problem of political will rather than of diplomatic design.
If asked directly: “will there be a war with Pakistan?”

“I would not predict one, sir. Neither side wants a general war — India's priority is growth and its principal long-term challenge is on the northern border, and Pakistan cannot afford one economically or militarily. What I would say is that the risk of a crisis has probably risen rather than fallen, because our declared policy now commits us to respond to any major attack, which means the adversary chooses the timing. So the professional question is not whether there will be a war but whether we can absorb the first move, respond decisively, and stop — and that is a question about readiness, air defence, intelligence and above all about disciplined command, which is the part I would be responsible for.”

India's strategic options

Presented neutrally. These are not mutually exclusive, and every actual Indian government has pursued several simultaneously. The purpose of the table is to let you discuss trade-offs rather than advocate a line.

1 · Punitive retaliation as declared policy

The ratchet — each round must exceed the last under a fixed ceiling; the adversary chooses the timing; each crisis internationalises the dispute; and the deterrent effect remains unproven by the record.
2 · Defensive hardening

A defensible synthesis, if you are pushed to choose

“Sir, if I had to sequence it: hold the line and harden the defences, because that is what actually reduces attacks; keep the retaliatory option credible and pre-planned so that it is used coldly rather than under public pressure; put the incremental diplomatic effort into financial and legal instruments, because those have produced results and risk nothing; restore statehood and keep the political process running in Jammu and Kashmir, because the domestic half of the problem is the half we control; and grow the economy, because that is the only thing that compounds. The cost of that package, stated honestly, is that it is slow and undramatic, and there will be attacks during it. I do not think there is a fast answer, and I would be suspicious of anyone who offered me one.”

Part IX

Knowledge toolkit

Lessons and interview technique

Each of these lessons is drawn from the material in this handbook and each can be stated in fifteen seconds. They are what turns the content into judgement.

  1. Ambiguity in a line becomes blood. “Thence north to the glaciers” produced Siachen. An undelineated Sir Creek produces arrested fishermen. The ceasefire line of 1949 produced 1965, 1971, 1999 and everything since. Finish the survey. Write down the coordinates. Sign the map.
  2. A proxy is a weapon that turns. Pakistan built the instrument in Afghanistan in the 1980s, used it in Kashmir from 1989, and has since lost tens of thousands of its own citizens to the same networks and now bombs the government it spent forty years installing. This is not an Indian argument; it is an observation Pakistani analysts make themselves.
  3. External sponsorship needs a domestic grievance to work. Pakistan's support was as available in 1985 as in 1990; what changed was 1987. The corollary is operational, not sentimental: good governance is a counter-terrorism capability.
  4. Nuclear stability buys sub-conventional instability. Kargil came a year after the tests. The bomb did not prevent conflict; it channelled it downwards. Any Indian strategy has to operate in the space the paradox creates.
  5. Mobilisation is not deterrence. Parakram put half a million men on the border for ten months, lost about 798 of them without a battle, and struck nothing. A demonstrated intention that is not executed teaches the adversary the opposite of what was intended.
  6. Intelligence fails at integration, not at collection. Kargil and 26/11 have the same diagnosis: the information existed and was not assembled. Every reform since — MAC, NTRO, DIA, NIA, NATGRID — is an attempt to fix assembly. That is why jointness matters.
  7. Announcing an action changes what it is. Cross-LoC raids were not new in 2016; declaring one was. Public announcement converts a tactical event into a strategic signal — and simultaneously removes the adversary's option of quietly absorbing it.
  8. The technical channel outlasts the political one. The 2021 DGMO ceasefire held for four years with no ambassadors, no trade and no dialogue. The nuclear-installations list has been exchanged every January since 1992 without a break. Build institutions that do not require goodwill.
  9. The target of a terrorist attack is not the people it kills. It is the reaction. Pahalgam sought communal violence across India and did not get it. Measure counter-terrorism by the reaction denied, not only by the attack prevented.
  10. Only one instrument compounds. Strikes are events; camps are rebuilt; grey-listing ends. The growth differential — roughly ten to one and widening — is the only thing in this subject that works while you sleep.

Interview technique on this topic

This subject is a trap laid for the emotional candidate. Almost everything that goes wrong on it goes wrong in the first fifteen seconds of the answer.

The seven rules

  1. Separate what is established from what is claimed — out loud. Say “what is established is…; what is claimed and contested is…”. On Balakot's damage, the F-16, and the May 2025 losses, say plainly that you would not assert a contested figure. This is the single highest-scoring behaviour available on this topic, because every other candidate recites television claims.
  2. Never sound like a news channel. No triumphalism, no ridicule of Pakistan, no casualty-counting. The board contains people who have commanded on that line. Your tone should be the tone of someone who knows what the cost is.
  3. Distinguish the state from the people. “The Pakistan Army's institutional interest” is a precise phrase. “Pakistan wants” is a lazy one. And say clearly that Pakistani civilians have suffered more from these networks than anyone.
  4. Acknowledge Indian mistakes without apology. 1953, 1987, the IC-814 release, the response to the Pandit exodus. A candidate who can name a national mistake and explain what was learned from it is demonstrating exactly the quality being assessed. A candidate who insists India has never erred is demonstrating the opposite.
  5. Never generalise about Indian Muslims or Kashmiris. Beyond being wrong, it is disqualifying. The useful facts run the other way: India has more Muslims than Pakistan and supplied a negligible number of ISIS recruits; Kashmiris protested against the Pahalgam killings; the J&K Police, overwhelmingly Kashmiri, has taken heavy casualties fighting militancy.
  6. Give a date, a place and a consequence. Not “there was a war in 1965” but “Operation Gibraltar in August 1965, infiltrators into the Valley, and it failed because Kashmiri villagers reported them.” Specificity is the whole game.
  7. Take a position and hold it under pressure — but change it if given a better argument. Both are being tested. Refusing to commit reads as weakness; refusing to reconsider reads as rigidity. The formula is: “That is a fair point, sir, and I had not weighted it. It would change my view on X, though I would still hold Y, because…”

Five traps and the way out

  • “Should we just finish Pakistan off?” — a deliberate provocation. Answer with the consequences of a collapsing nuclear state on your border, and with what India actually wants: a Pakistan whose army has different incentives.
  • “Aren't all terrorists from one community?” — Samjhauta 2007, Akshardham's acquittals, the Maoist insurgency, Punjab, the North East. Terrorism in India is not a single-community phenomenon and the facts are easy.
  • “Was Article 370's removal legal?” — do not argue the merits as though the question were open. It is settled by the Supreme Court in December 2023. Then discuss outcomes and the pending restoration of statehood.
  • “How many terrorists did we kill in Balakot?” — “Sir, that figure is contested and I would not assert one. What is established is…”. Then pivot to the threshold argument.
  • “Why can't we just talk to them?” — “Talk about what, and with whom?” Then the distinction between technical channels that have held and political dialogue that has not.

The framework applied: four briefs

Each of these takes a live question through the nine-rung ladder from Section 2. Read one aloud. The shape is identical every time, and once the shape is automatic you can apply it to a question you have never seen.

Brief 1 · Was abrogating Article 370 the right decision?

Define
On 5 August 2019 Parliament approved a resolution and the President issued orders under Article 370(1)(d) and 370(3) rendering the provision inoperative, and the Reorganisation Act split the state into two Union Territories.
Locate
Article 370 was a transitional provision from 1949, already hollowed out by some forty-five Presidential Orders between 1954 and 1994.
Establish
The Supreme Court upheld the action unanimously in December 2023, holding that J&K retained no internal sovereignty on accession and that 370 was temporary; it directed elections and restoration of statehood.
Contest
The process is criticised — using the Governor in place of the dissolved Constituent Assembly, the communications shutdown, the mass detentions, the absence of consultation. The Court did not rule on the validity of the Governor's concurrence.
Analyse
Two theories of the problem: that special status sustained separatism and blocked integration, or that it was the bargain that made accession legitimate and its removal validates the separatist claim.
Test
Since 2019: reduced organised militancy and larger tourist numbers, but Jammu-region violence has risen and targeted killings of minorities and migrants began in 2021. Assembly elections were held in September-October 2024 with high turnout, which is the most significant single indicator.
Position
It is settled law and it will not be reversed. I would judge it by outcomes, and the outcome that matters most is the political process. Restoring statehood on the timeline the Court envisaged is the step that would do most to consolidate it.
Concede
The manner of it — no consultation, no elected assembly, a long communications blackout — imposed a real cost in trust that the substance did not require.
Close
The measure of success is not the absence of militancy. It is whether a Kashmiri believes a vote in Srinagar changes something.

Brief 2 · Operation Sindoor and the “new normal”

Define
India's declared policy since May 2025 that any major act of terror will be treated as an act of war, that responses will not be constrained by nuclear signalling, and that Sindoor is paused rather than concluded.
Locate
The fifth rung of a ladder: investigate (1993-2008), mobilise (2001-02), isolate (2008), cross the LoC (2016), cross the IB (2019, 2025).
Establish
Nine sites struck on 7 May, including facilities at Bahawalpur and Muridke; three days of exchange involving drones, missiles and air defence; an understanding reached through the DGMO channel on 10 May.
Contest
Aircraft losses on both sides, damage assessments and the role of external actors in the ceasefire are all contested. I would not assert any of them.
Analyse
Declaring an automatic response raises the expected cost of an attack, which is deterrence. It also transfers the timing of the next crisis to the adversary and obliges India to escalate above the previous rung each time, under a ceiling that does not move.
Test
The historical record is not encouraging for deterrence by punishment: Uri followed the restraint years, Pulwama followed Uri, Pahalgam followed Balakot.
Position
I support keeping the option credible and pre-planned. I am more cautious about declaring it automatic, because automaticity removes the choice of timing, which is the one thing a stronger power should never give away.
Concede
Against that, a democratic government must be able to respond visibly when its citizens are killed, and ambiguity has its own costs. That is a real argument on the other side.
Close
The military instrument is necessary. It is not sufficient, because a missile cannot destroy money, handlers, ideology or recruitment.

Brief 3 · Should the Indus Waters Treaty remain in abeyance?

Define
The 1960 treaty allocating the three eastern rivers to India and the three western to Pakistan, held in abeyance by India on 23 April 2025.
Locate
It survived 1965, 1971 and 1999 — the single most durable agreement in the relationship.
Establish
Abeyance has meant suspension of data sharing and of the Permanent Indus Commission, and the removal of design and operating constraints on Indian projects on the western rivers.
Contest
Whether unilateral abeyance is available under the treaty, which has no exit clause, is a genuine legal question and is disputed.
Analyse
Three different things get run together: using entitlements India already has; suspending procedural cooperation as a pressure signal; and physically withholding water, which India cannot presently do and could not for fifteen to twenty years.
Test
The precedent problem is real. India is the lower riparian to China on the Brahmaputra, and establishing that an upper riparian may suspend a water treaty for political reasons is a precedent we would regret.
Position
Fully exercise our existing entitlements as permanent policy. Use abeyance of cooperation as leverage. Do not promise a capability we do not have.
Concede
The counter-argument is strong: leverage that is never used is not leverage, and this is the one point of genuine Pakistani dependence.
Close
In water, capability is measured in reservoirs, not in announcements.

Brief 4 · Should India abandon No First Use?

Define
India's declared policy since the 1999 draft doctrine and the CCS statement of 4 January 2003: credible minimum deterrence, no first use, and massive retaliation to inflict unacceptable damage.
Locate
Pakistan has no NFU and has moved to “full spectrum deterrence,” including the Nasr battlefield system tested in 2011.
Establish
India completed its triad with Arihant's first deterrence patrol in 2018, which is what makes an assured second strike — and therefore NFU — credible rather than aspirational.
Contest
Senior Indian figures have publicly questioned NFU, and some scholars argue the actual posture has drifted towards counterforce.
Analyse
NFU permits a smaller, less alert, cheaper arsenal without launch-on-warning or pre-delegation — all of which are accident-prone in a dyad with three-to-eight-minute flight times.
Test
The tactical-weapon problem is already answered within the doctrine: India's declared response to any nuclear use against its forces or territory, including a battlefield weapon on Indian soil, is massive retaliation. That removes the sanctuary a tactical weapon is meant to create.
Position
Keep NFU. Keep deliberate ambiguity about what constitutes unacceptable damage. Invest in survivability rather than in declaratory change.
Concede
Declaratory policy is not a guarantee of behaviour in extremis, and adversaries know it. NFU's value is largely in what it lets us avoid building.
Close
NFU is not restraint we are showing. It is a posture we can afford because we can survive.

Glossary

Use the exact term. Precision of vocabulary is the fastest available signal that you have read rather than watched.

International Boundary (IB)
India's settled international border with Pakistan, running from the Gujarat coast to the Jammu region — about 198 km of it in Jammu. Pakistan calls the Jammu stretch the “Working Boundary,” implying the status of J&K is unsettled; India does not accept the term.
Line of Control (LoC)
About 740 km from Akhnoor to NJ 9842, created by the Simla Agreement of 1972 from the 1949 ceasefire line. It is delineated on maps signed by both sides — which is precisely what the AGPL is not.
Actual Ground Position Line (AGPL)
About 110 km from NJ 9842 to Indira Col, in the Siachen-Saltoro area. Established by occupation in 1984 and after; not mutually authenticated, which is the core of the Siachen dispute.
NJ 9842
The map grid reference at which the 1949 Karachi Agreement's delineation stopped, with the words “thence north to the glaciers.” The four most expensive words in Indian military history.
Instrument of Accession
The legal document by which a princely state acceded to India or Pakistan. Signed for J&K by Maharaja Hari Singh on 26 October 1947 and accepted on 27 October; identical in form to those signed by other rulers.
Standstill Agreement
A short-term arrangement continuing existing administrative links pending accession. J&K offered one to both dominions; Pakistan signed and India did not — and Pakistan then blockaded the state in breach of it.
UNCIP
United Nations Commission for India and Pakistan. Its resolutions of 13 August 1948 and 5 January 1949 set out the three-stage sequence: Pakistani withdrawal, Indian reduction, then a plebiscite.
UNMOGIP
United Nations Military Observer Group in India and Pakistan, deployed from 1949. India's position since Simla is that its mandate lapsed; Pakistan continues to engage with it.
Simla Agreement
2 July 1972. Established bilateralism as the framework, converted the ceasefire line into the LoC, and committed both sides to settle differences by peaceful means without altering the line unilaterally.
Article 370
The transitional provision that governed the constitutional relationship between the Union and J&K. Rendered inoperative on 5 August 2019; upheld by the Supreme Court in December 2023.
Article 35A
Inserted by Presidential Order in 1954; empowered the state legislature to define “permanent residents” and confer special rights in property, employment and scholarships. Ceased to operate in 2019.
AFSPA
Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act. Applies in areas declared “disturbed”; confers powers of search, arrest and use of force, with a requirement of central sanction for prosecution of personnel — the provision that generates most of the criticism.
PoJK / PoK
Pakistan-occupied Jammu and Kashmir — India's term for the territory west of the LoC, comprising so-called “Azad Jammu and Kashmir” and, administratively separated from it, Gilgit-Baltistan.
Shaksgam / Trans-Karakoram Tract
Approximately 5,180 km² ceded by Pakistan to China under the 1963 boundary agreement; India regards the cession as void.
AIOS
Anti-Infiltration Obstacle System — the LoC “fence,” built mainly in 2003-04 over roughly 700 km, and constructed inside Indian territory rather than on the line.
CIBMS
Comprehensive Integrated Border Management System — the “smart fence,” integrating radar, thermal imaging, underground sensors and a command overlay; deployed in pilot stretches.
Fidayeen
An attacker who expects to die but is not using a suicide device — typically storming a fortified position and fighting until killed. Distinct from a suicide bomber. The signature method of JeM and LeT.
Overground worker (OGW)
A person with no weapon and no formal membership who provides shelter, transport, reconnaissance, communications or funds. The logistical foundation of the insurgency and the hardest element to detect.
Hybrid terrorist
An operative with no prior record and no organisational footprint who conducts one targeted killing and returns immediately to ordinary life. The dominant post-2019 pattern in the Valley.
Launch pad
A staging camp close to the LoC on the Pakistani side where infiltration parties assemble before crossing. The target set of the 2016 surgical strikes.
Rashtriya Rifles
A counter-insurgency force raised in 1990, manned by the Army on deputation — created so that the rest of the Army would not be permanently absorbed into internal security.
Ikhwan
Surrendered militants organised as counter-insurgents in the mid-1990s. Militarily effective and a source of serious human-rights allegations — a genuinely two-sided episode.
UAPA
Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act. Amended in 2008, 2012 and 2019; the 2019 amendment permits designating individuals as terrorists.
NIA
National Investigation Agency, created December 2008 — India's federal counter-terrorism investigating agency.
MAC / NATGRID
The Multi Agency Centre under the IB is the intelligence-sharing hub; NATGRID is the database-linking project. The proposed National Counter Terrorism Centre was never created, blocked by states on federalism grounds.
FATF grey list
Formally, “jurisdictions under increased monitoring.” Not a sanction — a market signal that raises due-diligence costs. Pakistan was listed June 2018 to October 2022.
Hawala
An informal value-transfer system operating on trust and offsetting ledgers, moving value without moving money across borders and leaving no formal banking record.
FICN
Fake Indian Currency Notes. Serves a dual purpose — funding operations and attacking the currency itself. Made a scheduled terrorist offence by the 2012 UAPA amendment.
Stability-instability paradox
Glenn Snyder's concept: stability at the nuclear level encourages violence below it, because both sides are confident the other will not escalate to the top. The single most useful idea in this subject.
No First Use (NFU)
India's declared policy since 1999 and formalised in January 2003: nuclear weapons will be used only in retaliation for a nuclear attack, and then massively. Credible because the triad is complete.
Full spectrum deterrence
Pakistan's declared posture since about 2013 — deterrence at strategic, operational and tactical levels, explicitly including battlefield nuclear weapons.
Nasr / Hatf-IX
Pakistan's short-range (about 60-70 km) battlefield nuclear system, first tested in 2011 and presented as the answer to Indian conventional thrusts.
Cold Start / Proactive Strategy
The Indian Army's post-2004 concept of rapid, shallow, multiple thrusts by Integrated Battle Groups within 48-96 hours — designed to solve the three-week mobilisation failure of Operation Parakram.
“Unwilling or unable”
The contested doctrine under which a state claims a right of self-defence against non-state actors on another state's territory where that state is unwilling or unable to suppress them. India's legal framing for Balakot.
Indus Waters Treaty
1960. Eastern rivers (Ravi, Beas, Sutlej) to India, western rivers (Indus, Jhelum, Chenab) to Pakistan, with limited Indian rights on the latter. Held in abeyance by India since April 2025.
Sir Creek
A 96-km tidal estuary in the Rann of Kutch. The dispute is between the eastern-bank reading of a 1914 resolution and the thalweg principle, and the real stake is the maritime boundary and the EEZ.
CPEC
China-Pakistan Economic Corridor, announced 2015, linking Kashgar to Gwadar. India objects in principle because it transits Gilgit-Baltistan.
Durand Line
The 1893 boundary between Pakistan and Afghanistan, about 2,670 km, never accepted by any Afghan government. The root of Pakistan's western insecurity.
Strategic depth
The Pakistani doctrine of securing a friendly Afghanistan as rearward space and to deny India influence. It produced the Taliban — and then the TTP.
TTP
Tehrik-i-Taliban Pakistan, formed December 2007. Attacks the Pakistani state; responsible for the Peshawar school attack of December 2014.
TRF / PAFF
The Resistance Front and People's Anti-Fascist Front — front organisations that emerged after 2019 with deliberately secular names, designed to evade designation and to support a claim of indigeneity.

Revision sheet

The last page you read before you walk in. Nothing here is new; everything here is load-bearing.

Eight numbers, with their caveats

  • 740 km LoC · 198 km IB in Jammu · 110 km AGPL — approximate and commonly cited
  • 166 killed at Mumbai in 2008 · 40 CRPF at Pulwama · 19 soldiers at Uri · 26 civilians at Pahalgam — these four are firm
  • About 798 Indian deaths during Operation Parakram, mostly non-combat — more than Kargil
  • About 93,000 prisoners of war in 1971
  • 33 MAF / 135 MAF — the eastern/western river split under the IWT, roughly 20/80
  • About 80% of Pakistan's arms imports from China in recent periods Re-verify
  • Roughly 10-11× — the ratio of India's economy to Pakistan's, nominal
  • Do not quote Balakot damage figures, May 2025 loss claims, or infiltration totals. Say they are contested and say why.

The arc in eight sentences

  1. Partition left a princely state with a Hindu ruler, a Muslim majority and no decision, and an invasion forced one.
  2. The accession was lawful; the ceasefire line that resulted was not a border, and the ambiguity in it has generated conflict ever since.
  3. Three conventional wars produced no change in that line, and 1971 proved conventional war could not deliver Pakistan's objective.
  4. So the instrument changed — an existing proxy-war capability, built in Afghanistan in the 1980s, was turned towards Kashmir when a domestic grievance opened in 1987.
  5. Nuclear weapons in 1998 did not prevent conflict; they pushed it downwards, and Kargil came a year later.
  6. India absorbed thirty years of attacks with a policy of restraint, internationalisation and institution-building, culminating in 26/11.
  7. From 2016 India began imposing visible, publicly announced and carefully bounded costs — across the LoC, then across the international boundary, then across multiple domains.
  8. Each round starts one rung higher under a ceiling that does not move, which is why the durable instruments are the unglamorous ones: money, law, intelligence, politics in Kashmir, and India's own growth.

Five facts that win arguments

  • J&K signed a Standstill Agreement with Pakistan, not India — and Pakistan then blockaded the state.
  • The UN resolutions required Pakistan to withdraw first. It never did. Every later obligation was conditional on that step.
  • India accepted a plebiscite in Junagadh in 1948 and won it — the sequencing argument, not hypocrisy.
  • Kargil began three months after Lahore, and Pathankot eight days after the Lahore stop of December 2015.
  • Pakistan convicted Hafiz Saeed in its own courts under FATF pressure, after two decades of denying there was a case.

Four answers to have ready, word for word

  • On contested claims: “What is established is…; what is claimed and contested is…; I would not assert the second.”
  • On talks: “Talk about what, and with whom? Keep the technical channels open permanently; hold the political dialogue for an interlocutor who can deliver.”
  • On Pakistan's collapse: “A collapsing nuclear state of 240 million on our border is the worst available outcome. We want a Pakistan with different incentives, not a weak one.”
  • On what to do: “Hold the line, keep the retaliatory option credible and cold, press the financial and legal instruments, restore the political process in Kashmir, and grow. It is slow and there is no fast answer.”

The ladder, for any question you have never seen

Define the thing precisely · Locate it in time · Establish what is agreed · Contest what is not · Analyse the mechanism · Test it against a case · Position yourself · Concede the strongest objection · Close with one sentence.

If you have thirty seconds, use rungs 1, 3, 7 and 9. If you have two minutes, use all nine. If you know nothing about the question, say so and then reason from the nearest thing you do know — which is a better answer than a confident invention, and every board knows it.

Last word before you walk in

You are not being examined on whether you can recite the casualty figures from a war that ended before you were born. You are being examined on whether, given incomplete information and a strong emotional pull in one direction, you can hold a position that is both patriotic and honest — and then revise it when given a better argument.

This subject makes that harder than any other, because it comes pre-loaded with grief. Twenty-six people were killed in a meadow in April. A hundred and sixty-six were killed in Mumbai. Forty were killed on a road in Pulwama. Nothing in this handbook is an argument against feeling that. It is an argument that the feeling is not, by itself, a policy — and that the officers who actually hold that line are required to be precise, proportionate and unemotional at the exact moment when nobody else is.

Know the dates. Tag the claims. Concede the weak ground. Take a position anyway.
