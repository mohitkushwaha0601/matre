Using this handbook

This is a reference document, not a narrative. Read Section 2 (the answer framework), Section 3 (the snapshot) and Section 60 (the revision sheet) first; then go to whatever the board is likely to press you on. The subject is emotionally loaded on both sides, which is exactly why a calm, sourced, structured answer stands out so sharply.

Four labels used throughout

On this topic, more than most, the difference between a fact and an assertion is everything. Four tags are used:

Established Documented and broadly agreed — treaty texts, dates, official statements, force locations acknowledged by both sides.

Interpretation A reasonable reading that serious analysts dispute. Adopt one if you like, but say you are adopting one.

Disputed Figures or claims where evidence is weak, deliberately withheld, or politically manufactured — casualty counts, intentions, intrusion statistics.

Re-verify Fast-moving current affairs. Anything after 2024 in this document must be re-checked against the Ministry of External Affairs and Ministry of Defence websites in the week before your interview.

The single most useful habit

Say “India’s official position is X; China’s stated position is Y; independent analysts differ on Z.” That one sentence structure will carry you through almost every question on this topic. An Interviewing Officer is not checking whether you are patriotic — he assumes that. He is checking whether you can hold a national position and describe the adversary’s position accurately, because you cannot out-think an opponent you refuse to understand.

What this handbook deliberately does

  • Treats the boundary as a problem of inheritance, not of villainy. Both republics inherited a frontier drawn by empires that never surveyed most of it. That is the honest starting point.
  • Separates the boundary dispute from the wider rivalry. They are connected but not identical. Trade, Tibet, Pakistan, the Indian Ocean and the Dalai Lama’s succession would matter even if the border were settled tomorrow.
  • Gives the Chinese case as Beijing makes it, so you can answer it rather than caricature it.
  • Refuses the “who is to blame for 1962” trap as the organising question. “What did each side believe it was doing, and what did each get wrong?” is the better one.
  • Keeps geography in front. Almost every tactical fact on this frontier — who can reinforce faster, who can see further, why a particular ridge matters — reduces to terrain, altitude and road-heads.
  • Presents India’s strategic options neutrally. Section 51 lays out choices and their costs. It does not tell you which to prefer, and neither should you in a GD — argue a position, but show you know its price.

A word on maps. Every schematic in this document is a teaching diagram, not a survey map. Relative positions are approximate and deliberately simplified. Do not reproduce them as authoritative depictions of any boundary. For official depictions, use Survey of India material.

The nine-step answer framework

One structure answers almost every question on this subject. Learn the ladder, not the essays. Whether a board member asks about Galwan, the Medog dam, CPEC or the Quad, he is asking the same nine questions in a different order — and a candidate who climbs the ladder audibly sounds organised even on a topic he has never seen before.

  1. What?

    State the thing itself in one or two sentences — what, where, when, who. No adjectives, no verdict, no history.

    Trap: opening with 1962. The board has not yet confirmed that you know what the subject is. Establish that in fifteen seconds and everything afterwards is believed.

  2. Why?

    Why is this happening, and why now? Separate the trigger from the driver — the incident that set it off from the interest that made it worth setting off.

    Trap: treating the trigger as the cause. Galwan’s trigger was a disputed structure; its driver was a pre-positioned move months in preparation.

  3. Background?

    The minimum history that makes step 2 intelligible. Three dates, not thirty. If a date does not change the listener’s understanding of the present, leave it out.

    Trap: this is where candidates drown. Discipline here is the single clearest marker of a prepared mind.

  4. India’s interest?

    Name the interest specifically: territorial, access, strategic depth, water, trade, technology, reputation, precedent. Say which one, and say whether it is vital, important or merely desirable.

    Trap: the phrase “it is against our national interest.” That is an assertion wearing the costume of an argument.

  5. Security implication?

    What does it change on the ground — force levels, deployment, access, warning time, escalation risk, the two-front calculation, the maritime flank?

    Trap: stopping at “it is a threat to our security.” Say which security, where, and how soon.

  6. Economic implication?

    Cost of holding, cost of building, trade and supply-chain exposure, opportunity cost. Every rupee committed here is a rupee not committed somewhere else.

    Trap: skipping this rung because the topic looks military. On this frontier the economics is the strategy — the 1962 gap was modest and the present gap is wide because of thirty years of growth rates.

  7. Argument for?

    The strongest case for the policy, action or position under discussion — stated as its own advocates would state it, not as a straw man.

    Trap: arguing for the side you personally hold and against a caricature of the other. Assessors notice this immediately.

  8. Argument against?

    The strongest case against, stated with equal force. If you cannot make it convincingly, you do not yet understand the question.

    Trap: omitting this rung to sound decisive. It has the opposite effect — conceding the best opposing point and then answering it is what makes a position sound earned.

  9. What should India do?

    A recommendation with three properties: it is sequenced (first this, then that), it is affordable, and it names its own cost. Then stop talking.

    Trap: a wish list. “India should strengthen infrastructure, boost the economy and improve diplomacy” is nine words that commit to nothing.

Worked example

What
On the night of 15 June 2020, Indian and Chinese troops fought hand-to-hand in the Galwan valley of eastern Ladakh, near Patrolling Point 14, over the removal of a Chinese structure. Twenty Indian soldiers were killed, including Colonel B. Santosh Babu, Commanding Officer of 16 Bihar. China acknowledged four dead. No firearms were used.
Why
Trigger: a disagreement over whether an agreed local disengagement had actually been carried out. Driver: a large, pre-positioned PLA movement into eastern Ladakh at several points simultaneously in April–May 2020 — which indicates planning, not friction — most plausibly to pre-empt India’s improving access along the Darbuk–Shyok–DBO road and to test Indian resolve at a moment of pandemic disruption.
Background
Three dates are enough. 1962 left the LAC in this sector undefined and unclarified. 1993–2013 produced agreements barring the use of firearms and requiring restraint — which is why the fighting was with hands and improvised weapons. 2019–20 saw completion of the DSDBO road, which changed local access towards Daulat Beg Oldi.
India’s interest
Three, in descending order of vitality: preserving the DSDBO axis and the approach to the Karakoram Pass; retaining patrolling access up to India’s claimed LAC; and — the most important and least tangible — denying the precedent that a unilateral change of the status quo can be made to stick.
Security
Ended forty-five years without a fatality on the LAC. Forced the emergency induction of additional divisions into Ladakh and a lasting rebalance of the Army towards the northern border. Demonstrated that the CBM architecture manages accidents but does not deter a decided move. Raised the probability that a future incident involves firearms.
Economic
A large recurring cost in holding formations at high altitude through winter — habitat, fuel, rations, air maintenance. Acceleration of border infrastructure spending. Press Note 3 FDI screening, app bans, procurement restrictions. And the honest complication: bilateral trade subsequently reached record levels, so the economic instruments did not do what they were advertised to do.
For
The case that India handled it well: restraint prevented escalation between two nuclear-armed states; the Kailash Range operation in August created real leverage rather than mere protest; disengagement was achieved in stages without conceding the principle; and the boundary was moved from the margin to the centre of the relationship — “the state of the border determines the state of the relationship.”
Against
The case that it was a failure: assessment did not anticipate a move of that scale despite available indicators; buffer zones removed patrolling access to areas India previously reached; the Depsang blockage took more than four years to address; and the economic measures raised costs at home without reducing dependence.
India should
Complete de-escalation without allowing buffer zones to harden into a new line; press for LAC clarification on the model of the successful 2001 middle-sector map exchange; continue the lateral roads, tunnels and ISR that make surprise expensive; and de-risk selectively in APIs, electronics and critical minerals. The cost, stated honestly: a permanent two-front deployment is expensive, and every rupee spent on mountain habitat is a rupee not spent on a submarine.
Three rules that make the ladder work
  • Never skip rung 8. A position that has survived its own best counter-argument is the only kind worth holding, and saying so out loud is what distinguishes conviction from noise.
  • Rung 9 must cost something. If your recommendation has no trade-off, you have not made one.
  • Announce the structure only if you are asked a big question. “Sir, may I take that in three parts — what it is, why it matters to us, and what I think we should do” is excellent for a broad question and pompous for a narrow one.

Interview edge

If asked “what is the India-China problem?”, do not begin with 1962. Begin with structure: “Sir, I see three layers — a boundary problem inherited from empire, a Tibet problem that turned a quiet frontier into a live one, and a power problem, which is the rivalry of two rising states in one continent. The three sectors: a primer

You cannot discuss this frontier without a mental map. Learn this section physically — trace it with a finger on an atlas — because every incident in the rest of the handbook happens somewhere on it.

Western sector — Ladakh

  • Terrain: cold desert, 12,000-19,000 ft, almost no tree cover, thin population. Mobility is channelled through a handful of valleys and passes; whoever holds the heights sees everything.
  • Core dispute: Aksai Chin, a high plateau between the Kunlun and Karakoram ranges that carries China’s G219 highway linking Xinjiang to Tibet. That road is the reason Aksai Chin is non-negotiable for Beijing.
  • Key features north to south: Karakoram Pass and Daulat Beg Oldi (DBO); the Depsang Plains; Galwan Valley; Kugrang/Chang Chenmo, with Hot Springs and Gogra; Pangong Tso and the eight “Fingers” on its north bank; the Kailash Range and Chushul bowl with Rezang La, Rechin La, Gurung Hill, Magar Hill and Spanggur Gap; and finally Demchok on the Indus.
  • Indian formation: XIV “Fire and Fury” Corps, Leh, with the Indo-Tibetan Border Police manning many forward posts in peacetime.

Middle sector — Himachal Pradesh and Uttarakhand

  • The quietest sector and the only one where the two sides have actually exchanged maps of their perception of the LAC (2001). That fact alone is worth remembering — it shows clarification was possible and then stopped.
  • Friction is episodic and small-scale: Barahoti (a grazing ground in Chamoli district, disputed since 1954-55), Nelang/Nilang, Pulam Sumda, Sang Chok La, Lapthal and Sangchamalla.
  • Shipki La and Lipulekh are historic trade and pilgrimage passes; Lipulekh additionally involves a trijunction dispute with Nepal over Kalapani.

Sikkim sector

  • The alignment here derives from the Anglo-Chinese Convention of 17 March 1890 on Sikkim and Tibet, which is why both sides have often described this sector as the closest to settled. China invoked the same 1890 convention during Doklam — selectively, India argued.
  • Nathu La and Cho La, the 1967 clash sites, and Naku La, where scuffles occurred in 2020 and 2021.
  • South-west of Sikkim lies the Doklam plateau, claimed by both Bhutan and China, overlooking the approach to India’s Siliguri corridor — the “Chicken’s Neck,” roughly 20-22 km at its narrowest, connecting the North-East to the rest of India.

Eastern sector — Arunachal Pradesh

  • Steep, forested, monsoon-drenched, crossed by deep gorges — terrain that favours the defender but punishes logistics on both sides.
  • China claims the whole state as “Zangnan” or “South Tibet,” with Tawang as the emotional and religious core: birthplace of the Sixth Dalai Lama and site of the largest monastery in India.
  • Friction points: Bum La, Yangtse near Tawang (site of a December 2022 clash), Sumdorong Chu, Asaphila, Longju, Dibang, Walong and Kibithu on the Lohit.
  • Indian formations: IV Corps (Tezpur), III Corps (Dimapur), XXXIII Corps (Sukna) under Eastern Command, with XVII Mountain Strike Corps at Panagarh.

The one-line distinction you must get right. An international boundary is delimited (described in a treaty), demarcated (marked on the ground) and mutually accepted. The LAC is none of those things. It is a line describing where each side’s troops are in effective control, and because the two sides have different perceptions of where it runs, patrols from both armies legitimately walk into areas the other considers its own. That single structural fact — differing perception of the same line — produces almost every face-off you will be asked about.

Three terms interviewers use to separate the prepared from the rest
  • Claim Line — where a country says the boundary ought to be.
  • Line of Actual Control — where the forces actually are; the term entered bilateral treaty language formally in 1993, though Zhou Enlai had used it in a letter to Nehru as early as 1959.
  • Patrolling Point (PP) — an internal Indian Army/ITBP designation for a point up to which patrols go to assert presence. Loss of access to a PP is how “status quo” changes get measured in practice. PPs are an Indian administrative device, not a bilaterally agreed set of markers.

Part I

Origins of the question: how two civilisations acquired a border

Two civilisations before the border

For roughly two thousand years India and China were neighbours who almost never fought. The reason is geography: the Himalaya, the Karakoram and the Tibetan plateau made large-scale invasion across the mountains impractical. What crossed instead were monks, merchants, manuscripts and ideas. Understanding this matters, because both governments still invoke that long peace — for opposite purposes.

The Buddhist transmission

  • Established Chinese tradition dates the formal arrival of Buddhism to 65-67 CE, when the Han emperor Ming is said to have received the monks Kashyapa Matanga and Dharmaratna, for whom the White Horse Temple at Luoyang was built.
  • Kumarajiva (c. 344-413), whose father was Indian and mother a Kuchean princess, became the greatest translator of Buddhist texts into Chinese — his renderings shaped East Asian Buddhism permanently.
  • Bodhidharma, traditionally a south Indian prince, is credited in Chinese tradition with founding Chan (Zen) Buddhism at Shaolin.
  • Chinese pilgrims travelled the other way: Faxian (in India c. 405-411, during Chandragupta II’s reign), Xuanzang (629-645, who studied at Nalanda and left the single most detailed account of seventh-century India), and Yijing (671-695, who came by sea via Srivijaya).
  • Indian mathematics and astronomy reached Tang China; the Gautama (Qutan) family of Indian astronomers served the Tang court, and Gautama Siddha compiled the Kaiyuan Zhanjing treatise.

Trade and the rare collisions

  • The Silk Road and its southern branches linked Kashmir, Ladakh and Khotan; a second route ran from Bengal and Assam through Burma into Yunnan. A third was maritime: Chola and Song, and later Zheng He’s treasure fleets, which visited Calicut repeatedly between 1405 and 1433 — Zheng He is believed to have died at or near Calicut in 1433.
  • Established The one celebrated military episode is the mission of the Tang envoy Wang Xuance in 648 CE. Attacked by a usurper in north India, he raised a force with Tibetan and Nepalese help and defeated him. It is a footnote, not a war — and it is worth noting the Tibetan role even then.
  • Ladakh-Yarkand caravan trade across the Karakoram Pass, and the Kalimpong-Lhasa wool and borax trade, remained commercially significant into the twentieth century. Indian trading families were established at Lhasa, Gyantse and Yatung; the Indian rupee circulated widely in Tibet.

How India uses this history

  • Evidence that conflict is aberrant, not natural — “two thousand years of peace, sixty of difficulty.”
  • Evidence of civilisational parity: two ancient states, neither a tributary of the other.
  • Evidence that Tibet functioned historically as a distinct polity mediating between them.

How China uses this history

  • Evidence of a benign Chinese order to which neighbours related peacefully — the implicit model for a China-centred Asia.
  • Evidence that “the border question was created by imperialism,” i.e. by Britain, and is therefore a colonial residue India should not defend.
  • Evidence, in the official telling, of long Chinese involvement in Tibetan affairs from the Yuan period onward.
A distinction worth making in a GD

“Two thousand years of peace” is true but slightly misleading. There was peace largely because there was no shared administered border — only a vast buffer of Tibetan and Central Asian space that neither state governed intensively. Peace was a by-product of distance. When that buffer disappeared between 1950 and 1951, the two states became direct neighbours for the first time in history, and conflict followed within a decade. Interpretation That is the most economical explanation of the entire dispute, and it is a strong line to open with.

Tibet: the pivot of everything

You cannot understand the India-China border without Tibet. India does not share a border with China proper anywhere; it shares a border with Tibet and with Xinjiang. Every claim, every road, every clash discussed later in this handbook sits on the Tibetan plateau or its approaches.

Why the plateau matters strategically

  • Altitude dominance. Tibet averages over 4,500 m. Forces on the plateau look down on the Indian plains; forces on the Indian side must climb to fight. But the plateau side also has flat, open ground behind it, which favours road, rail and airfield construction, while the Indian side is steep, cut by gorges, and hard to build on.
  • Water tower of Asia. The Indus, Sutlej and Brahmaputra (Yarlung Tsangpo) all rise in or near Tibet, as do the Mekong, Salween, Yangtze and Yellow rivers. Whoever controls the plateau controls the headwaters of rivers serving well over a billion people.
  • Strategic depth and basing. The plateau gives China dispersal space for missiles, airbases and sensors that is remote from its own coastal heartland and from US maritime power.
  • Minerals. Significant deposits including lithium, copper, chromite and uranium, increasingly exploited.

The historical status question — the honest version

This is contested and you should present it as contested. The simplified competing narratives:

The Chinese account

  • Tibet has been part of China since the Yuan dynasty (13th century), when Mongol rulers brought both China and Tibet under one imperium.
  • Qing emperors exercised authority through ambans (imperial residents) at Lhasa from 1720s, including in the selection of high incarnations via the Golden Urn procedure introduced in 1793.
  • The 1911-1950 interval was a period of weakness in which imperialists detached Tibet; the 1950-51 action was therefore “peaceful liberation,” restoring sovereignty, not conquest.

The Tibetan and much Western scholarly account

  • The Yuan and Qing relationships were chö-yön (priest-patron) arrangements between a Tibetan religious hierarch and a Mongol or Manchu ruler — personal and religious, not a modern transfer of sovereignty.
  • Between 1913 and 1950 Tibet exercised the practical attributes of independence: its own government, army, currency, postage, passports, and conduct of foreign relations, including the Simla Conference.
  • No foreign state formally recognised Tibetan independence — which is legally significant and is the weakest point in this account.

The twentieth-century sequence

  • 1904Younghusband Expedition. A British Indian military mission fights its way to Lhasa; the 13th Dalai Lama flees to Mongolia. The Lhasa Convention is signed with Tibetan authorities without Chinese participation — an inconvenient precedent for Beijing’s later argument.
  • 1906-07Britain and China sign the Anglo-Chinese Convention (1906), and Britain and Russia the Anglo-Russian Convention (1907), which together recognise Chinese “suzerainty” and bar both powers from dealing with Lhasa directly. The word suzerainty — authority short of sovereignty — becomes the crux of a century of argument.
  • 1910Qing forces under Zhao Erfeng occupy Lhasa. The 13th Dalai Lama flees the other way — to British India, living at Darjeeling. He returns in 1912-13 after the Qing collapse.
  • 1913Tibetan authorities expel the remaining Chinese officials and the 13th Dalai Lama issues a proclamation that Tibetans have since read as a declaration of independence. Its exact legal character is argued about to this day.
  • 1913-14Simla Conference (see next section).
  • 19507-19 October: the PLA crosses the upper Yangtze and takes Chamdo; the Tibetan army, a few thousand strong with obsolete equipment, is overwhelmed. Tibet appeals to the United Nations; the appeal is shelved, with India and Britain both unwilling to press it.
  • 195123 May — the Seventeen Point Agreement is signed in Beijing. Tibetan delegates later stated they signed under duress and without authority to use the Tibetan government seal. It formally incorporated Tibet into the PRC while promising autonomy and non-alteration of the Dalai Lama’s status.
  • 1956-59Revolt in Kham and Amdo against land reform spreads to central Tibet, culminating in the Lhasa uprising of March 1959.
The Patel letter — a document you should be able to name

Established On 7 November 1950, weeks after the PLA entered Tibet, Deputy Prime Minister Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel wrote to Nehru warning that India now faced “a determined, calculating” power on a frontier it had always considered secure, that Chinese ambitions extended to areas in Assam, and that India would have to plan for a two-front problem — China in the north and Pakistan in the west. He died on 15 December 1950. The letter was drafted with substantial input from Foreign Secretary Sir Girija Shankar Bajpai.

Interpretation The letter is frequently used to argue Nehru ignored clear warnings. A fair answer notes three things: that it identified the problem with remarkable precision; that it did not propose a practical alternative policy in 1950, when India had neither the military nor the economic capacity to contest Tibet; and that the counterfactual — what India could realistically have done — is rarely examined by those who cite it. Use it as evidence of foresight, not as a settled verdict on Nehru.

India’s loss of position in Tibet

Independent India inherited from Britain a set of genuinely unusual rights in Tibet: trade agencies at Yatung, Gyantse and Gartok, a military escort at Gyantse, rest houses, and control of the posts, telegraph and telephone lines from the Indian border to Gyantse. Established India surrendered all of these in the 1954 Agreement (next section but one), receiving in return the Panchsheel principles and six designated trading passes — but no mention whatsoever of the boundary.

The counting exercise that clarifies the topic. Before 1950, the frontier states between India and China were Tibet, Nepal, Bhutan and Sikkim — four buffers. By 1951 Tibet was gone. Sikkim became an Indian protectorate and, in 1975, an Indian state. Nepal and Bhutan remain, and both are now arenas of competition. The steady erosion of buffers is the single most important structural change in India’s northern security environment since independence.

British frontier policy in the west

Aksai Chin is the classic case of a boundary invented in an office. Between 1865 and 1899 British officials proposed at least four different alignments for the same stretch of uninhabited plateau, for reasons that had far more to do with Russia than with China. India inherited the most expansive of them; China argues from a different one. Both positions have documentary support, which is precisely why the dispute has never been resolved on legal grounds alone.

The strategic context: the Great Game

British anxiety in the nineteenth century was about Russian expansion into Central Asia, not about China. Between 1865 and 1885 Russia absorbed Tashkent, Samarkand, Khiva, Kokand and Merv. The question for Calcutta was where to place a defensible line north of Kashmir, and whether a weak Chinese presence in Xinjiang was a useful buffer or an irrelevance. Different answers produced different lines.

India’s 1954 decision

Established In July 1954, shortly after the Panchsheel agreement, the Government of India directed that its maps show a firm, defined boundary in all sectors, replacing earlier depictions that marked stretches “boundary undefined.” Interpretation Critics argue this converted an open question into a fixed position that could not later be traded away without appearing to surrender territory. Defenders argue that a state which leaves its frontier vague on its own maps invites encroachment — and that China was already acting unilaterally. Both readings are defensible; say which you prefer and why.

Simla 1914 and the McMahon Line

The eastern sector dispute turns on one conference and one map annexure. Learn the mechanics precisely — this is the single most common place where candidates get caught out, because the details are genuinely complicated and both sides’ slogans obscure them.

What happened at Simla

  • Established The conference ran from October 1913 to July 1914 at Simla and Delhi. Three plenipotentiaries attended: Sir Henry McMahon for British India, Lonchen Shatra Paljor Dorje for Tibet, and Ivan Chen for the Republic of China.
  • The conference’s main business was not the India-Tibet boundary at all. It was the division of Tibet into an autonomous “Outer Tibet” (Lhasa-governed, under nominal Chinese suzerainty) and a “Inner Tibet” where Chinese authority would be greater. That was what China refused.
  • The India-Tibet alignment — the McMahon Line — was settled in bilateral exchanges between McMahon and Lonchen Shatra in March 1914, largely at Delhi, and recorded in notes and on two maps at a scale of eight miles to the inch.
  • The crucial procedural fact: Ivan Chen initialled the draft convention on 27 April 1914 but, on instructions from Beijing, did not sign or ratify the final text of 3 July 1914. Britain and Tibet then signed a bilateral declaration binding the convention as between themselves and denying China its benefits.

India’s legal case

  • Tibet in 1914 exercised treaty-making capacity in fact; it had expelled Chinese officials and conducted its own external relations.
  • China’s refusal to ratify concerned the Inner/Outer Tibet division, not the India-Tibet alignment, and the Chinese representative raised no objection to that alignment at the time.
  • The line follows the highest watershed of the eastern Himalaya — the classic principle for a mountain boundary, and the one China itself invokes elsewhere.
  • India has administered the area continuously; and in the 1960 officials’ talks India produced extensive evidence of administration south of the line, including in Tawang after 1951.
  • China accepted a virtually identical watershed alignment in its 1960 boundary treaty with Burma (now Myanmar) — the eastern continuation of the very same McMahon Line. That is India’s sharpest debating point.

China’s legal case

  • Tibet was a part of China and had no sovereign capacity to conclude boundary treaties; therefore the line is void ab initio.
  • The line was “secretly” negotiated bilaterally while a tripartite conference was in session, and was not published in Aitchison’s Treaties until a 1938 volume was reissued bearing the original 1929 date — a point the historian Alastair Lamb documented and which Beijing uses heavily.
  • It is an “imperialist line” imposed by a colonial power, and a decolonised India should not inherit colonial impositions.
  • Tawang was administered from Lhasa — through the Tawang monastery and the Tsona Dzong — until India took over in 1951, which China presents as proof that the line was never effective.
  • China has never accepted the line in any document and formally protested India’s administration.

Tawang: the hard case

  • Tawang lies south of the McMahon Line as drawn, and therefore in India on India’s reading. But Tibetan authorities continued to administer and collect revenue there after 1914, because Britain did not physically take over.
  • Established India established administration at Tawang in February 1951, when Major R. (Bob) Khathing of the Assam Rifles, an officer from Manipur, led a small column into Tawang and assumed charge, easing out the Tibetan officials.
  • Tawang is the birthplace of the Sixth Dalai Lama, Tsangyang Gyatso (b. 1683), and the site of Tawang Monastery, founded in the 1680s and the largest in India. Its religious weight is precisely why China has hardened on it since the mid-1980s.
  • It is also the most practicable military approach from Tibet into the Brahmaputra valley, via Bum La and the Se La axis. Religion, history and military geography point to the same place.
Interview edge — the Burma point

If a board member presses you with “but the McMahon Line was a colonial imposition,” the cleanest reply is factual rather than indignant: “Sir, China signed a boundary treaty with Burma in 1960 that accepted essentially the same McMahon alignment in its eastern continuation, and settled boundaries with Nepal in 1961, Pakistan in 1963, Mongolia, Afghanistan and later Russia, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan and Vietnam — in most cases on the basis of lines of colonial origin. So the objection appears to be to this particular line rather than to colonial lines as a category.” That is a complete, evidence-based answer delivered without heat.

Aksai Chin: the land nobody watched

“Aksai Chin” means roughly “white stone desert” in Turkic. It is a salt-crusted plateau between 4,300 and 5,500 m, with no permanent population, almost no rainfall and temperatures that fall well below −30 °C. Nehru’s much-quoted remark that “not a blade of grass grows there” was factually near-accurate and politically disastrous — it is still thrown at Indian policy sixty years later.

Why it matters to China

  • It carries the G219 Xinjiang-Tibet Highway, built 1951-57, the only all-weather road link between Xinjiang and western Tibet that avoids the far longer northern and eastern routes. In the 1950s, before the modern rail and road network existed, it was the umbilical cord for Chinese control of western Tibet.
  • It gives depth to Chinese positions covering both Xinjiang (where Beijing has long-running security concerns) and Tibet.
  • It adjoins the Shaksgam/Trans-Karakoram Tract ceded by Pakistan in 1963, creating a continuous Chinese-controlled belt across the top of Ladakh — and physical adjacency to the Karakoram Pass and, further west, to the CPEC alignment through Gilgit-Baltistan.

Why it matters to India

  • It is claimed as part of the Union Territory of Ladakh; Parliament’s unanimous resolution of 14 November 1962 pledged to recover every inch of territory, and no government has repudiated it.
  • Chinese positions on the plateau overlook the approaches to the Depsang Plains, Daulat Beg Oldi and the Sub-Sector North, which in turn face the Karakoram Pass and the approaches towards Siachen and the Shaksgam.
  • Loss of the plateau removes India’s ability to threaten the Xinjiang-Tibet link in any contingency — a strategic asset in reverse.

The “Aksai Chin for Arunachal” swap. Established Zhou Enlai indicated in 1960, and Deng Xiaoping revived in 1980-82, a “package settlement”: China would accept the McMahon alignment in the east if India accepted Chinese control of Aksai Chin in the west — broadly, each side keeps what it holds. India did not accept. Interpretation Whether that was a historic missed opportunity or a trap is genuinely contested. Note two hard facts for balance: first, no Indian government could have ceded claimed territory after the 1962 Parliament resolution and survived; second, from around 1985 China hardened its position and began to insist on “meaningful adjustments” in the east, specifically Tawang — so the swap that was notionally on the table in 1960 was no longer on the table by the time India might have been politically able to consider it.

The phrase to avoid, and what to say instead

Never repeat “not a blade of grass grows there” approvingly. It signals that you evaluate territory by its agricultural yield. Say instead: “The value of Aksai Chin is not in the ground but in what the ground carries — a highway, an axis of communication between two Chinese regions, and observation over the Depsang approaches. Terrain is valued by what it enables, not by what it produces.”

Part II

The road to 1962

1949-54: a new China on the doorstep

Two revolutions ended within two years of each other — India’s independence in 1947 and the Chinese communist victory in 1949. Both new states set out to consolidate frontiers their predecessors had left vague. Along one stretch of the Himalaya, those two consolidation projects pointed at the same ground.

India’s opening position

  • India recognised the People’s Republic of China on 30 December 1949, among the first non-communist states to do so, and established diplomatic relations on 1 April 1950.
  • India campaigned persistently for the PRC to take China’s seat at the United Nations Security Council in place of the Republic of China, and declined to press the Tibetan appeal to the UN in 1950.
  • India played an active mediating role in the Korean War (1950-53), chairing the Neutral Nations Repatriation Commission — which gave Delhi standing in Beijing but irritated Washington.
  • Strategic rationale, in Nehru’s own framing: a newly independent, economically weak India could not afford an arms race or a hostile northern neighbour; it needed decades of peace to industrialise. Interpretation This is the logic behind almost every decision of the period, and it is the fair way to present Nehru’s policy rather than as naïveté.

The consolidation of India’s own frontier

  • 1950: the North East Frontier Agency (NEFA) is constituted, administered by the Governor of Assam as the President’s agent — deliberately kept outside normal state administration.
  • February 1951: Tawang taken over (see previous part).
  • 1954: NEFA’s divisions are renamed in Indian rather than colonial-tribal terms (Kameng, Subansiri, Siang, Lohit, Tirap); the maps are hardened; and the Indo-Tibetan Border Police did not yet exist — frontier duty fell to the Assam Rifles in the east and to the state police and army in Ladakh.
  • 1950: the Indo-Nepal Treaty of Peace and Friendship (31 July 1950) and the Indo-Bhutan Treaty (8 August 1949) lock in India’s position with the two remaining buffer states; Sikkim becomes a protectorate by the treaty of 5 December 1950.

China’s opening position

  • Mao’s stated objective was the restoration of a unified state within what Beijing regarded as its historical extent: Xinjiang (1949), Tibet (1950-51), and pressure on remaining peripheries.
  • China’s immediate strategic problems were Taiwan, Korea, and internal consolidation. The Indian frontier was a third-order concern in the early 1950s — which is part of why the road across Aksai Chin was built quietly rather than announced.
  • Established Chinese maps published in the 1950s showed large areas of NEFA, parts of Ladakh, and sections of the middle sector as Chinese. When India protested in 1954, Zhou Enlai’s reply was that these were old Kuomintang-era maps that the new government had not had time to revise. India accepted the explanation at the time; the maps were not in fact revised.

Panchsheel and “Hindi Chini Bhai Bhai”

The 1954 agreement is the most misunderstood document in the whole subject. It is routinely described as a friendship treaty. It was in fact a trade agreement about Tibet, in which India gave up substantial inherited rights and received a preamble of principles — and in which, crucially, the boundary was never mentioned.

What the 1954 Agreement actually contained

  • Established Full title: the Agreement on Trade and Intercourse between the Tibet Region of China and India, signed at Peking on 29 April 1954, valid for eight years.
  • Its preamble set out the five principles — Panchsheel: mutual respect for territorial integrity and sovereignty; mutual non-aggression; mutual non-interference in internal affairs; equality and mutual benefit; and peaceful coexistence.
  • India surrendered: its military escort at Gyantse; its post, telegraph and telephone installations and rest houses on the Gyantse-Yatung route; and its extraterritorial trade agency rights, which were converted to ordinary trade agencies at Yatung, Gyantse and Gartok. China got reciprocal agencies at New Delhi, Calcutta and Kalimpong.
  • The agreement named six passes for traders and pilgrims: Shipki La, Mana, Niti, Kungri Bingri, Darma and Lipulekh.
  • It described Tibet as the “Tibet Region of China,” which in effect recorded India’s acceptance of Chinese sovereignty there.
  • The boundary is not mentioned anywhere in the text. Interpretation India’s later position was that naming the passes implied recognition of the traditional alignment. China’s position was that nothing was settled. This omission is the most consequential drafting decision in modern Indian diplomacy.

The case for the 1954 agreement

  • India could not have held the Tibetan rights militarily; they were a colonial legacy, indefensible and an obvious irritant.
  • It bought a decade of quiet on the frontier while India built the Second and Third Five Year Plans.
  • Panchsheel gave India enormous standing in the decolonising world — Bandung, the Non-Aligned Movement, and moral authority disproportionate to Indian power.
  • Raising the boundary in 1954 risked a formal Chinese claim being registered when India had no capacity to resist it.

The case against

  • India gave away real, tangible assets for a preamble, without extracting the one thing that mattered — a boundary settlement or at least an acknowledgment.
  • Conceding Tibet’s status removed the last legal basis on which India might have argued for a buffer.
  • It signalled that India would not contest Chinese moves, encouraging later unilateralism.
  • The government did not tell Parliament or the public about Chinese map claims and early incursions, which meant that when the truth emerged in 1959 the political reaction was uncontrollable.

The slogan years

  • June 1954: Zhou Enlai visits India; October 1954: Nehru visits China — the first visit by a non-communist head of government, drawing vast crowds.
  • “Hindi Chini Bhai Bhai” becomes the public register of the relationship. Interpretation The slogan was popular enthusiasm as much as policy, but it constrained policy: once the public was told China was a brother, disclosing border incidents became politically costly, which is exactly what happened between 1954 and 1959.
  • April 1955: the Bandung Conference, where Nehru effectively introduced Zhou Enlai to the Afro-Asian world. Interpretation Several historians argue this was a strategic error in status terms — India built up a rival’s standing while assuming a permanent seniority it could not sustain.
  • 1956: The Dalai Lama and Panchen Lama visit India for the 2,500th Buddha Jayanti. The Dalai Lama raises the possibility of staying; Nehru, after speaking with Zhou, advises him to return, on the assurance that autonomy would be respected.
A nuance that impresses boards

Panchsheel is not dead. Its five principles were reaffirmed in Indian and Chinese statements repeatedly, and they remain embedded in the language of Indian diplomacy. Interpretation The honest Indian position today is that the principles were sound and the application was asymmetric: India observed the first principle — respect for territorial integrity — more consistently than it was observed towards India. That formulation lets you defend Indian diplomatic tradition without defending the 1954 bargain.

The road that changed everything

Nothing illustrates the nature of this frontier better than the fact that a foreign state built a 1,200-kilometre highway across territory India claimed, and India found out from a newspaper.

  • Established China constructed the Xinjiang-Tibet Highway (later G219) between roughly 1951 and 1957, running from Yecheng (Kargilik) in Xinjiang to Gartok and Rutog in western Tibet — crossing approximately 179 km through the Aksai Chin territory India claimed.
  • September 1957: the completion is reported in the Chinese press, with a map. Indian officials take notice.
  • 1958: India sends two reconnaissance patrols into Aksai Chin. One is detained by Chinese forces; the other confirms the road. India protests formally in October 1958.
  • Why nobody noticed earlier: India had no habitual presence, no aerial reconnaissance programme over the area, no road within hundreds of kilometres, and the plateau is approached from the Indian side only across extremely difficult passes. The nearest Indian post was days away on foot.
The lesson that still applies

Unobserved territory is unheld territory. The 1957 discovery is the ancestor of every later Indian investment in surveillance — satellite constellations, high-altitude long-endurance drones, the Border Roads Organisation’s presence-creating road programme, the Vibrant Villages Programme, and the doctrine that patrolling to a designated point is itself an act of sovereignty. If you are asked why patrolling points matter so much in Indian statements about Depsang, this is the historical answer: India learned in 1957 what happens to ground you do not visit.

The Barahoti precedent

Less famous but analytically useful: Barahoti, a small grazing plain in what is now Chamoli district, Uttarakhand, saw Chinese personnel appear as early as 1954-55, within months of the Panchsheel agreement. India protested; talks were held in 1958; nothing was resolved. Interpretation Barahoti shows the pattern that would recur for seventy years — small, deniable, non-lethal presence in a disputed pocket, sustained until it becomes routine. It is the earliest documented instance of what analysts now call salami-slicing on this frontier.

1959: Tibet, the Dalai Lama, first blood

1959 is the hinge year. Before it, the border problem was a diplomatic file. After it, it was a public, emotional, armed confrontation — and the two issues that had been separate, Tibet and the boundary, fused permanently.

The Lhasa uprising and the flight

  • 10 March 1959Crowds surround the Norbulingka palace in Lhasa, fearing the Dalai Lama will be abducted. The uprising spreads; fighting breaks out.
  • 17 March 1959The 14th Dalai Lama leaves Lhasa in disguise with a small party.
  • 31 March 1959He crosses into India at Khenzimane / Chuthangmu in the Tawang sector and is received by Indian officials. India grants asylum.
  • 18 April 1959The Tezpur statement, his first public statement on Indian soil, repudiating the Seventeen Point Agreement as signed under duress. Beijing reacts furiously and accuses India of complicity.
  • 1960The Dalai Lama establishes his headquarters at Dharamsala, Himachal Pradesh, where the Central Tibetan Administration remains today. Roughly 80,000 Tibetans follow into exile in the first years; the refugee population in India is today of the order of 70,000-90,000.

The first shots

  • Established 25 August 1959 — Longju, in the Subansiri area of NEFA, just south of the McMahon alignment. Chinese troops attack an Assam Rifles post. India loses the post. This is the first armed clash between the two states.
  • Established 21 October 1959 — Kongka La, eastern Ladakh. A Central Reserve Police Force patrol under Deputy Central Intelligence Officer Karam Singh is ambushed. Nine Indian policemen are killed and ten captured. This is why 21 October is observed as Police Commemoration Day across India — a fact worth knowing, because very few candidates do.
  • The Kongka La incident, coming after Longju, destroyed public faith in the government’s account of the frontier and forced the tabling of the diplomatic correspondence as White Papers, which is why so much of this record is publicly available.

Was there Indian or foreign involvement in the Tibetan resistance? Disputed It is now well documented from declassified American sources that the CIA ran a programme supporting Tibetan guerrillas from the late 1950s, including training in Colorado and, later, operations from Mustang in Nepal. India’s own role is more complex: after 1962 India raised the Special Frontier Force (Establishment 22 / “Vikas”) on 14 November 1962 under Major General Sujan Singh Uban, recruited substantially from Tibetan exiles. The extent of Indian involvement before 1962 remains unclear in the open record. Present this carefully and do not overclaim in either direction.

Zhou’s offer and the 1960-61 talks

Between the first clashes and the war there were two and a half years of intense correspondence and one serious negotiation. Knowing that this diplomacy happened — and why it failed — is what separates an informed answer from a slogan.

  • 7 Nov 1959Zhou Enlai writes to Nehru proposing that both sides withdraw 20 kilometres from the “so-called McMahon Line” in the east and from the “line of actual control” in the west — the first significant use of that phrase. Nehru rejects it: in the west, withdrawal from a line that already reflected Chinese advances would legitimise them.
  • Apr 1960Zhou Enlai visits Delhi, 19-25 April, for six days of talks with Nehru. No agreement. Zhou’s implicit package — Chinese acceptance of the McMahon alignment in the east against Indian acceptance of Chinese control in Aksai Chin — is not taken up. Zhou goes on to Kathmandu and signs a boundary treaty with Nepal.
  • Jun-Dec 1960Officials of both sides meet in Beijing, Delhi and Rangoon and produce a massive Officials’ Report in 1961, setting out each side’s historical and cartographic evidence. It records disagreement with great precision and is still the best single documentary source on the rival cases.
  • 1960-62China concludes boundary treaties with Burma (1960), Nepal (1961), Mongolia (1962) and later Pakistan (1963) and Afghanistan (1963) — a pattern of settling with weaker neighbours while the Indian dispute escalated.

Why India would not deal

  • Parliament and the press, now fully informed, would not tolerate any concession; Nehru told Zhou frankly that he could not carry the country.
  • India’s legal case in the east was strong and in the west arguable; conceding the west looked like rewarding a fait accompli.
  • Accepting a swap would have validated the principle that territory can be acquired by occupation — with obvious implications for Kashmir.
  • Domestic politics after the Tibet crisis made any softening look like appeasement of a state sheltering behind Panchsheel while taking land.

Why China would not wait

  • The Tibetan revolt made the Aksai Chin road route to western Tibet operationally essential.
  • The Dalai Lama’s presence in India was read in Beijing as a standing Indian instrument against Chinese rule in Tibet.
  • The Sino-Soviet split was deepening; a demonstration of resolve had value in the communist world, and Soviet neutrality in 1959 had angered Beijing.
  • Internally, China was emerging from the catastrophe of the Great Leap Forward; an external success had political utility. Interpretation Analysts differ sharply on how much weight to give this factor.

The Forward Policy

The most criticised decision in modern Indian defence history, and the one you must be able to describe without either defending it or sneering at it.

What it was

  • Established From November 1961, following a directive issued after a high-level meeting, Indian forces were ordered to establish posts as far forward as possible towards the Indian claim line, including behind and between Chinese positions, to prevent further Chinese advance and to establish presence in unoccupied areas.
  • By September 1962, roughly 60 posts had been established in the western sector, many of them small, isolated, supplied by air-drop, and positioned without mutual support.
  • The underlying assumption, stated in intelligence assessments of the time, was that China would not react strongly — that it would avoid a major confrontation, as it had in similar situations before.

The reasoning behind it — presented fairly

  • Passive defence had already cost India ground: the Aksai Chin road, Longju, Kongka La. Doing nothing was itself a policy with costs.
  • In international law and practice, presence supports claim; absence erodes it. Establishing posts was a way to contest the record without firing.
  • Political pressure was intense. Parliament, the opposition and the press demanded action. Interpretation Several historians treat the Forward Policy substantially as a political answer to domestic pressure rather than a military plan.

Why it failed militarily

  • No logistics. Posts were supplied by air-drop with high wastage; many had no road access, inadequate winter clothing, and days of rations.
  • No mutual support. Platoon and section posts, often out of artillery range of each other, could be eliminated one at a time.
  • No reserves. There was no force in depth to restore a lost post or to punish an attack.
  • A mismatch between political intent and military means. The Army leadership’s professional reservations were recorded and overruled; the Chief of the General Staff, Lieutenant General S.P.P. Thorat, had submitted an appreciation in 1959-60 predicting broadly what happened, and it was not acted upon.
  • It handed China a justification. Beijing was able to present the war as a response to Indian “nibbling” — a narrative it uses to this day.
The balanced verdict to offer

“The Forward Policy was not wrong as an aim; it was wrong as an execution.” Asserting presence on disputed ground is normal state behaviour, and India does it today — with roads, habitat, integrated surveillance and formations in depth behind each post. In 1961-62 India did it with none of those. The failure was not of will; it was the classic civil-military failure of ordering a posture the force was not resourced or structured to hold, and of an intelligence assessment that substituted hope for analysis. That is a lesson about the process of national security decision-making, and it is the reason the Chiefs of Staff, the National Security Council structure and the Chief of Defence Staff exist in the forms they do.

Names to know from this period. Jawaharlal Nehru, Prime Minister; V.K. Krishna Menon, Defence Minister, who resigned on 7 November 1962; General P.N. Thapar, Chief of Army Staff, who resigned after the war; Lieutenant General B.M. Kaul, Chief of the General Staff and then commander of the newly raised IV Corps; Lieutenant General S.P.P. Thorat, whose warning was ignored; Lieutenant General Daulet Singh, GOC-in-C Western Command, who formally recorded his reservations; B.N. Mullik, Director of the Intelligence Bureau, whose assessments underpinned the belief that China would not escalate.

Part III

The war and its verdict

The 1962 war: how it was fought

The Sino-Indian War lasted thirty-two days, was fought in two distinct phases separated by a three-week lull, and ended with a unilateral Chinese ceasefire and withdrawal in the east. It was not a single collapse; it was a sequence of local defeats produced by the same set of systemic causes.

The immediate trigger: Dhola and the Namka Chu

  • Established On 4 June 1962 an Assam Rifles detachment established the Dhola post in the Namka Chu valley, in the Tawang sector. The post lay south of the Thag La ridge but, on the map annexed to the 1914 agreement, apparently north of the drawn line — a cartographic ambiguity of a few kilometres in extremely difficult terrain.
  • Chinese troops surrounded the area from 8 September 1962. India ordered them evicted under Operation Leghorn — an order issued in Delhi on the assumption that a brigade could be concentrated and supplied on the Namka Chu. It could not: supplies had to cross the 4,500 m Hathung La on porters’ backs and by limited air-drop.
  • 7 Infantry Brigade under Brigadier John Dalvi was strung out along the river in penny packets, without winter clothing, with a handful of mortars and, in some units, fewer than fifty rounds per rifle. Dalvi protested in writing. IV Corps was raised on 4 October 1962 under Lieutenant General B.M. Kaul specifically to execute the eviction.

Phase One: 20-25 October 1962

  • 20 October: China attacks simultaneously in the eastern and western sectors — a coordinated theatre-level offensive, not a border incident. In the Namka Chu, 7 Infantry Brigade is destroyed in hours; Brigadier Dalvi is captured. In Ladakh, the isolated Forward Policy posts in the Galwan, Chip Chap and Pangong areas are overrun one by one.
  • Sirijap, Pangong Tso: a company of 1/8 Gorkha Rifles under Major Dhan Singh Thapa holds against repeated assaults; he is captured and later awarded the Param Vir Chakra — one of the few PVC recipients to survive and to receive it after being presumed dead.
  • Bum La, 23 October: Subedar Joginder Singh of 1 Sikh holds his platoon position through three waves, is wounded, fixes bayonets and counter-charges; captured, he dies in captivity. Param Vir Chakra, posthumous.
  • 23-24 October: Tawang is abandoned without a fight and occupied by Chinese forces. Indian troops fall back to the Se La and Bomdila positions.
  • 25 October: operations pause. China issues a three-point proposal for negotiations; India rejects talks under occupation.

The lull: 25 October - 13 November

  • India reorganises. 4 Infantry Division is reconstituted under Major General A.S. Pathania. Reinforcements are flown to Assam. Krishna Menon resigns as Defence Minister on 7 November; Nehru takes the portfolio.
  • 14 November — Parliament would shortly pass its resolution; on the same day an Indian counter-attack is launched at Walong, timed for Nehru’s birthday. It fails against superior numbers on the heights.
  • Diplomatically, Nehru writes to President Kennedy on 19 November requesting urgent air support. The Cuban Missile Crisis (16-28 October) had absorbed superpower attention for most of Phase One — a piece of timing that is not coincidental in most analysts’ view.

Phase Two: 14-21 November 1962

  • Eastern sector: Chinese forces bypass the strong Se La position through the flanks and cut the road behind it. 62 Infantry Brigade is ordered to withdraw, the withdrawal becomes a rout, and Brigadier Hoshiar Singh is killed. Bomdila falls on 18 November. Chinese troops reach the edge of the Brahmaputra plain near Chaku and Foothills; there is panic and partial evacuation in Tezpur.
  • Walong axis: after the failed counter-attack, Walong is lost on 16 November; Kibithu falls.
  • Western sector: the main effort falls on the Chushul bowl, defended by 114 Infantry Brigade, to threaten the airfield. This is where the Indian Army’s finest performance of the war occurs (next section).
  • 21 November: China declares a unilateral ceasefire effective from midnight and announces withdrawal, in the eastern sector, to 20 km behind the line of actual control of 7 November 1959. It keeps Aksai Chin. Indian prisoners are repatriated in 1963.

What China wanted. Interpretation The mainstream analytical view is that Beijing’s objectives were political rather than territorial in the east: to destroy the Forward Policy, to demonstrate that India could not contest Aksai Chin, to end Indian pretensions to Asian leadership, and to establish a permanent psychological ascendancy. The withdrawal in the east is the key evidence — a state seeking Arunachal would not have given it back. The retention of Aksai Chin is equally telling: that was the objective that was actually territorial.

Rezang La, Walong, Se La: the small unit story

The strategic verdict on 1962 is a defeat. The regimental verdict is very different, and for a defence aspirant it is the more important half. Know two or three of these actions properly. Do not recite all of them.

Rezang La, 18 November 1962

  • Established Charlie Company, 13 Kumaon — roughly 120 men, overwhelmingly Ahirs from the Rewari-Mahendragarh belt — held a position at about 5,000 m (16,000 ft) guarding the southern approach to Chushul airfield.
  • The position lay in a bowl behind a crest line, which meant Indian artillery could not range onto the approaches. The company fought without artillery support, in temperatures far below freezing, from first light.
  • Of about 120 men, 114 were killed. The company commander, Major Shaitan Singh, moved between platoon positions under fire, was wounded, and was found in the spring, still with his weapon. Param Vir Chakra, posthumous. Five Vir Chakras and four Sena Medals were awarded to the company.
  • The bodies were recovered only in February 1963 when the snow melted, many still at their posts with ammunition expended. A memorial stands at Rezang La and another at Rewari; 18 November is observed as Rezang La Day.
  • Operational significance: the delay imposed at Rezang La is widely credited with preventing the Chinese from taking Chushul airfield before the ceasefire. Interpretation How decisive it was strategically is debatable; how decisive it is to the Indian Army’s self-understanding is not.

Walong, 14-16 November 1962

  • The only sector where India attempted a deliberate counter-offensive. 11 Infantry Brigade, with 6 Kumaon, 4 Sikh, 3/3 Gorkha Rifles and 4 Dogra, attacked up the Lohit valley towards the Chinese-held heights.
  • The attack failed against a numerically superior, better-positioned enemy, but the defence of the approaches was stubborn. Walong Day is observed on 16 November.
  • The Walong axis remains operationally important; the Kibithu-Walong road and the Sisseri river bridge are among the infrastructure priorities in that sector today.

Nuranang / Jaswantgarh, 17 November 1962

  • 4 Garhwal Rifles held the Nuranang position on the Se La-Tawang road and inflicted heavy casualties before withdrawal. Rifleman Jaswant Singh Rawat, killed here, was awarded the Maha Vir Chakra; the post is named Jaswantgarh and is maintained as a shrine on the Tawang road.

How to use this in an interview

If asked about 1962, do not let the answer be only about failure. The formulation that works: “Sir, 1962 was a failure of the system, not of the soldier. Where units were given a defensible position, ammunition and a clear task — Rezang La, Nuranang, the Chushul bowl — they fought as well as any army in the world. Where they were strung along a river in company posts with fifty rounds a man and no artillery, no army would have held. That distinction is the whole lesson of the war.” It is accurate, it is generous to the institution, and it shows you understand that battles are won or lost before they begin.

Why India lost — eight reasons

A favourite question, because it separates candidates who have memorised battles from those who can analyse a system. Eight causes, in rough order of weight.

  1. Political direction without military feasibility. The Forward Policy and Operation Leghorn were ordered without asking whether the force could execute them. There was no institutional mechanism compelling the question to be asked, and officers who asked it were sidelined.
  2. Intelligence failure of assessment, not of collection. The Intelligence Bureau’s judgement that China would not respond to the Forward Policy with force was the load-bearing assumption of Indian policy. It was wrong, and it was never seriously contested inside the government. Raw information about Chinese build-up existed; it was interpreted through the wrong frame.
  3. Logistics. The Indian side had almost no roads to the frontier; the Chinese side had a decade of road-building behind it and interior lines across the plateau. Troops arrived from the plains and fought within days at 4,000 m — many without acclimatisation, windproof clothing, or snow boots. This single factor explains more of the outcome than any other.
  4. Equipment and scales. Many units carried .303 Lee-Enfield bolt-action rifles against semi-automatic weapons; artillery was scarce and often could not be got forward; radio sets were inadequate; there was no high-altitude clothing scale worth the name.
  5. Command and organisation. A corps headquarters (IV Corps) was created and committed within sixteen days of raising. Commanders were appointed for political reliability in some cases rather than operational fitness; divisional and brigade boundaries cut across natural terrain compartments; the Army Headquarters intervened directly in tactical decisions.
  6. A decade of under-investment. Defence spending was roughly 2 per cent of GDP or less through the 1950s; the emphasis was on development. The Army was structured, equipped and deployed for a Pakistan contingency in the plains, not a Himalayan campaign.
  7. Non-use of offensive air power. See the next section — a decision with defenders and critics.
  8. Strategic isolation. The Soviet Union, India’s principal arms supplier, was neutral at the critical moment because of the Cuban crisis and its own dispute with Beijing; American and British assistance came late and in small quantities; the non-aligned world largely declined to condemn China.

What China got right, stated plainly. Concentration of force at the decisive point; excellent tactical reconnaissance and use of ground, particularly flanking movements through terrain Indian commanders believed impassable; disciplined infiltration; clear and limited political objectives; and — the one that is least discussed — a rigorously enforced stopping point. Knowing when to stop is a strategic skill, and in 1962 one side had it.

The air power controversy

India used its air force extensively for transport, supply-dropping and casualty evacuation in 1962, and did not use it for offensive strikes. Sixty years later this is still argued about, and it is a genuinely good interview question because both sides have a case.

The case that offensive air should have been used

  • Chinese supply lines ran across the open Tibetan plateau on a small number of roads — an ideal interdiction target with no cover.
  • The IAF operated Hunters, Canberras, Vampires and Gnats and had recently inducted MiG-21s; it was, in the judgement of several Western analyses since, superior to the PLA Air Force’s capability over Tibet at that time.
  • Chinese airfields on the plateau suffered severe payload and range penalties from altitude; Indian airfields in the plains did not.
  • Air Chief Marshal P.C. Lal and others later expressed the view that the IAF could have materially changed the outcome, particularly against logistics.

The case for restraint as it was made at the time

  • Fear of Chinese retaliation against Indian cities — Calcutta, Tezpur, Guwahati — which had effectively no air defence in 1962.
  • Advice, including from foreign governments, that escalation to air war risked widening the conflict at a moment when India was militarily unprepared for it.
  • Concern about damaging the prospects of a ceasefire and international sympathy.
  • Uncertainty about Chinese air strength on the plateau — intelligence on PLAAF deployments was poor.
The mature answer

Interpretation Say: “With hindsight the balance of expert opinion is that offensive air power would have helped, especially against logistics on the plateau. But the decision was taken with 1962’s information, not ours — and the real failure was not the choice itself, it was that India had no framework for taking such a decision. There was no joint planning body, no air staff representation in the key meetings, and no contingency study. India reached the same conclusion I would reach now, but for the wrong process.” Then connect it forward: this is one of the intellectual ancestors of joint planning, the Integrated Defence Staff (2001), the Strategic Forces Command, and the Chief of Defence Staff and Department of Military Affairs (2019-20).

Consequences and reconstruction

The war’s most durable effects were institutional. Nearly every element of India’s current northern border security architecture dates from the three years after 1962.

Immediate political consequences

  • 14 November 1962: Parliament unanimously resolves that India will drive out the aggressor from every inch of Indian soil. This resolution still constrains every Indian government’s negotiating space.
  • Krishna Menon resigned; General Thapar resigned and was replaced by General J.N. Chaudhuri; Lieutenant General Kaul resigned. Nehru’s prestige never recovered; he suffered a stroke in January 1964 and died in May 1964.
  • The Colombo Proposals (December 1962, six non-aligned states) suggested mutual withdrawals as a basis for talks. India accepted them in principle; China accepted them only in part; nothing came of it.
  • India’s non-alignment was re-read: military assistance from the United States and Britain arrived during the war, and India signed for Soviet MiG-21 production — the beginning of a long-term defence relationship with Moscow.

The comparison worth drawing

Compare 1962 and 2020 in one breath and you will make a strong impression: “Sir, in 1962 India had no road to the frontier, no acclimatised reserves, no surveillance, and a political directive the army could not execute. In 2020 India had the Darbuk-Shyok-DBO road, formations that could be moved and sustained through a Ladakh winter, satellite and UAV coverage, and a military leadership whose assessment was sought before the response was framed. Part IV

Freeze, thaw and the framework that held for thirty years

1967: Nathu La and Cho La

The least-known and most consequential Indian success on this frontier. If you can discuss 1967 intelligently you will stand out immediately, because most candidates have never heard of it.

The context

  • Sikkim was then an Indian protectorate. Nathu La (4,310 m) and Cho La are passes on the Sikkim-Tibet watershed, part of the one sector where an 1890 convention nominally defined the alignment.
  • During the 1965 India-Pakistan war China had issued an ultimatum demanding India dismantle “military structures” on the Sikkim-Tibet border — an episode that established the pattern of Chinese pressure coinciding with Indian crises elsewhere.
  • By 1967 the local situation was tense: loudspeaker propaganda, jostling, and repeated Chinese objection to Indian wire-laying along what India held to be the watershed alignment.
  • Major General Sagat Singh, commanding 17 Mountain Division, had refused earlier suggestions to vacate Nathu La, judging that withdrawal from the watershed would expose the Chumbi valley approaches and the Sikkim interior.

Nathu La, 11-14 September 1967

  • On 11 September, while 70 Field Company Engineers with 2 Grenadiers were laying wire along the crest, a confrontation escalated and Chinese medium machine guns opened fire from prepared positions on the shoulders. Indian troops in the open suffered heavily in the first minutes; Lieutenant Colonel Rai Singh, commanding 2 Grenadiers, was wounded.
  • India responded with artillery — and this was the decisive difference from 1962. Indian gunners on the ridges of the Sebu La and Camel’s Back had observation over the Chinese positions in the Chumbi valley; Chinese bunkers were systematically destroyed over three days.
  • Firing ceased on 14 September; a local flag meeting and exchange of dead followed on 15-16 September.

Cho La, 1 October 1967

  • A separate clash at Cho La, a few kilometres north, involving 7/11 Gorkha Rifles and 10 JAK Rifles. Chinese troops attempting to push across the pass were repulsed in close-quarter fighting; Indian forces held and in places improved their position.
  • Rifleman Devi Prasad Limbu and others were decorated; the action is remembered in the Gorkha regiments particularly.

Why 1967 matters more than its size
  • It broke the psychological legacy of 1962. Indian formations held ground, used artillery decisively and did not withdraw. Both armies drew the conclusion that a repeat of 1962 was not available.
  • It validated the post-1962 reconstruction — acclimatised mountain divisions, forward-deployed artillery with observation, better communications.
  • It established the template for every subsequent standoff: local, intense, terminated quickly, then managed diplomatically. Nathu La-Cho La, Sumdorong Chu, Doklam and even 2020 follow the same arc.
  • It is the answer to “has India ever stood up to China?” — a question that is sometimes put to candidates precisely to see whether they know anything beyond 1962.

1975 Tulung La and the long quiet

Between 1967 and 2020 the frontier produced exactly one fatal firefight. That fact is the strongest evidence that the management architecture, whatever its failures, worked.

  • Established 20 October 1975 — Tulung La, Arunachal Pradesh. An Assam Rifles patrol was ambushed; four men were killed. India protested that the patrol was well within Indian territory. This was the last fatal exchange on the LAC until 15 June 2020 — a gap of nearly forty-five years.
  • 1975: Sikkim becomes the 22nd state of India following a referendum. China refused to recognise the merger for nearly three decades, formally accepting it only around 2003-05 in the context of the Vajpayee visit.
  • 1976: Ambassadors are exchanged again after a fourteen-year gap, following the 1962 downgrade.
  • February 1979: Foreign Minister Atal Bihari Vajpayee visits China — the first senior Indian visit since 1962. It is cut short when China launches its punitive war against Vietnam while he is in the country, an episode that badly damaged the visit’s outcome and is often cited as a cautionary tale about timing in diplomacy.
  • 1980-82: Deng Xiaoping revives the “package proposal” in interviews and through diplomatic channels. India does not take it up.
  • 1981-87: Eight rounds of official-level boundary talks. They establish habits of dialogue but settle nothing. From around 1985, in the sixth round, China explicitly seeks “meaningful concessions” in the eastern sector — signalling that Tawang is now part of its ask.

The 1959 versus 1962 line problem — a technical point worth knowing. China has at times referred to the LAC of 7 November 1959; India has consistently refused to accept a “1959 line,” arguing that the LAC is not a historical line to be fixed by reference to a particular date but reflects actual control, and that China’s own positions have moved since. In September 2020 the Ministry of External Affairs formally restated that India has never accepted the so-called unilaterally defined 1959 LAC. If a board member mentions “the 1959 line,” this is the correct response.

1986-87: Sumdorong Chu

The closest the two countries came to war between 1962 and the present, and the crisis that produced the whole modern architecture of border management. It is also the best available case study in successful Indian crisis handling on this frontier.

What happened

  • Summer 1986A Chinese detachment establishes a semi-permanent presence, including a helipad and structures, at Wangdung in the Sumdorong Chu valley, in the Tawang sector just south of the McMahon alignment as India reads it. India had maintained a seasonal observation post nearby; it had been withdrawn for the winter and the ground reoccupied in the interim.
  • Oct 1986India responds with Operation Falcon — a rapid heli-lift of troops, under Eastern Army Commander Lieutenant General Narahari, to occupy the dominating heights, including Hathung La and the Lungro La ridge, overlooking the Chinese position. India ends the crisis holding the tactically superior ground.
  • 20 Feb 1987Arunachal Pradesh becomes a full state of the Indian Union. China protests strongly; the two issues fuse.
  • 1987Exercise Chequerboard, conducted under Army Chief General K. Sundarji, demonstrates India’s ability to move and sustain large formations in the eastern sector at speed using air transport. Estimates of the scale vary; the point of it was signalling.
  • May 1987Rhetoric peaks; Western media speculate about war. Foreign Minister N.D. Tiwari transits Beijing and passes a de-escalatory message; flag meetings and the eighth round of talks defuse the situation.
  • 1993-95Both sides eventually agree to a mutual pull-back in the Sumdorong Chu area, completed around 1995 — the first negotiated disengagement on the LAC, and the direct precedent for 2020-24.
The lessons of Sumdorong Chu
  • Vacated ground gets occupied. Seasonal withdrawal from an observation post created the opportunity. This is precisely why India now invests in year-round habitat and all-weather roads.
  • Take the heights, then talk. Operation Falcon changed the local balance before negotiations began, which is why the eventual settlement was mutual rather than a one-sided Indian pull-back. The same logic is visible in the Kailash Range operation of August 2020.
  • Escalate militarily, de-escalate politically, simultaneously. India reinforced and signalled willingness to talk at the same time. That is a sophisticated posture and it is the one India has attempted in every subsequent crisis.
  • Crises produce architecture. Sumdorong Chu convinced both sides they needed rules. The 1988 visit and the 1993 and 1996 agreements flow directly from it.

1988: the Rajiv Gandhi breakthrough

One visit changed the structure of the relationship for thirty years. The formula it produced — build everything else while the boundary waits — is exactly the formula that collapsed in 2020.

  • Established 19-23 December 1988: Prime Minister Rajiv Gandhi visits China — the first Indian prime ministerial visit since Nehru in 1954. He meets Deng Xiaoping, who greets him with the remark that the two countries should “forget the unpleasant past” and “look forward.”
  • The outcome was a deliberate de-linking: the boundary question would be pursued in a dedicated channel while trade, culture, science and other cooperation proceeded independently of it.
  • A Joint Working Group (JWG) on the boundary question was established at the level of Foreign Secretary and Vice-Foreign Minister, with a subsidiary Expert Group of diplomatic and military officials. The JWG met regularly for years and was the vehicle that produced the 1993 and 1996 agreements.
  • India reaffirmed that Tibet is an autonomous region of China and that Tibetans in India would not be permitted anti-China political activity — a formulation restated in subsequent joint statements.

What the 1988 formula achieved

  • Nearly three decades without a fatal incident on a 3,488 km disputed frontier — a genuinely remarkable record by any international comparison.
  • Trade grew from negligible to tens of billions of dollars.
  • Regular summitry, defence exchanges, joint exercises (Hand-in-Hand from 2007) and cooperation in BRICS, the SCO, the WTO and climate negotiations.
  • It allowed India to concentrate on economic reform after 1991 without a live northern front.

What it cost

  • Interpretation De-linking removed India’s leverage: China could expand economic access to India while conceding nothing on the boundary, because the two were formally separate.
  • The asymmetry of growth after 1990 meant that every year of delay improved China’s relative position. Time was not a neutral factor.
  • Infrastructure asymmetry widened enormously in the same period, for reasons discussed in Part VI.
  • The trade relationship became structurally lopsided and created dependencies that constrain Indian options today.
The sentence that frames the whole post-1988 period

“From 1988 to 2020 India and China ran an experiment: can two states with an unsettled, militarised border build a large economic relationship and simply postpone the border? For thirty-two years the answer looked like yes. In June 2020 the answer became no.” Follow it with the corollary that India’s current policy rests on an explicit reversal — the formulation, stated repeatedly by the External Affairs Minister, that peace and tranquillity in the border areas is the basis of the relationship, and the state of the border will determine the state of the relationship. That is the single most quotable statement of current Indian policy, and it is a direct repudiation of the 1988 formula.

The critique of the architecture, stated fairly. Interpretation The agreements are procedural, not substantive. They say what to do when you meet the other side’s patrol; they do not say where the line is. They have no verification mechanism, no penalty for breach, and no dispute-settlement body. They worked for as long as both sides wanted them to work — which is the honest description of most confidence-building regimes. The Indian government’s post-2020 position is that Chinese actions violated all of them, particularly the 1993 commitment to keep forces to a minimum and the 1996 commitment not to use or threaten force. The Chinese position is that the agreements remain valid and that the 2020 events arose from Indian infrastructure construction in disputed areas.

Why the LAC was never clarified

A short but important section. The single technical step most likely to reduce violence on this frontier — exchanging maps showing where each side thinks the LAC runs — was begun, and abandoned. Understanding why is the difference between describing the problem and explaining it.

What was actually done

  • The 1993 and 1996 agreements both contained commitments to clarify and confirm the alignment of the LAC where the two sides had different perceptions.
  • Established In 2000-2001 the two sides exchanged maps of the middle sector — the least contested — and the exercise was completed without major difficulty.
  • In 2002 an attempt to exchange maps of the western sector was made. According to Indian accounts the Chinese side declined to hand over its map after seeing the Indian depiction, and the process stopped. It has not resumed since.

The competing explanations

The Indian reading

  • Ambiguity is an asset for the stronger party. An undefined line permits incremental advance that can always be described as patrolling within one’s own perception.
  • Clarification would have frozen positions at a time when China intended to improve them.
  • A clarified LAC would make any future encroachment unambiguously identifiable as a violation — which is precisely the constraint China preferred to avoid.

The Chinese reading

  • Clarifying the LAC would create a de facto boundary and reduce pressure to reach a final settlement — freezing India’s advantage in the east.
  • Exchanging claim-line maps invites each side to maximise its claim, hardening rather than reducing the dispute.
  • Better to move directly to a package settlement under the 2005 principles than to entrench a provisional line.
A point that will land in a group discussion

“The LAC is not ambiguous by accident. It is ambiguous because ambiguity has been useful to at least one party at every moment when clarification was possible.” Follow it with the practical consequence: every subsequent crisis — Depsang, Doklam, Galwan — has been fought over the interpretation of a line that both governments agreed, in writing, to clarify, and then did not. If you are asked what single measure would most reduce risk on this frontier, LAC clarification is the answer with the best analytical support; note honestly that it requires a political decision in Beijing that has not been forthcoming.

Part V

The new confrontation, 2013-2026

Depsang 2013, Chumar 2014, Burtse 2015

Before Doklam and Galwan there was a decade of escalating, non-lethal standoffs. They are worth knowing individually because each one demonstrates a different technique, and because the pattern they form is the best evidence that 2020 was not a sudden departure.

Depsang, April-May 2013 — the “tent standoff”

  • Established On 15 April 2013 a PLA platoon pitched tents in the Raki Nala area of the Depsang Plains, on ground India regarded as some 19 km inside its perception of the LAC. India established a facing post roughly 300 m away.
  • The standoff lasted about three weeks and was resolved by 5 May 2013, days before the Chinese Premier’s visit to India and before the Indian External Affairs Minister’s visit to Beijing.
  • The resolution reportedly involved India dismantling some structures at Chumar, in the southern part of eastern Ladakh — a location that recurs.
  • Why Depsang matters: the plateau is flat, tank-navigable terrain close to the Daulat Beg Oldi airfield and the Sub-Sector North, and it sits near the approaches to the Karakoram Pass and the Shaksgam. It is the one place on the Ladakh frontier where armour can be used at scale.

Chumar, September 2014

  • A large face-off in the Chumar area — several hundred troops on each side — occurring while President Xi Jinping was visiting India, including during his meeting with Prime Minister Modi in Ahmedabad and Delhi.
  • Interpretation The timing has been read in two ways: as a deliberate demonstration of leverage calibrated to summitry, or as evidence of loose coupling between the PLA’s local commands and the political leadership. The first reading is now the more widely held; the same pattern appeared in 1965 and 2013.
  • Chumar is unusual because it is one of the few places where Indian positions dominate and Indian access is better than Chinese access — which is why it has repeatedly been the bargaining chip in local settlements.

Other incidents worth naming

  • Burtse, September 2015: a dispute over an observation hut in the Depsang-DBO area; resolved locally.
  • Demchok, 2016-2019: recurring frictions over grazing, the Charding-Ninglung Nallah and Chinese objection to Indian development works.
  • Pangong Tso, August 2017: a filmed scuffle involving stone-throwing on the north bank, during the Doklam standoff — the first widely circulated video of physical confrontation on the LAC.
  • Naku La, Sikkim, May 2020 and January 2021: brawls resulting in injuries on both sides.
  • Yangtse, Tawang sector, 9 December 2022: a clash in which Indian and Chinese troops were injured; the Defence Minister made a statement in Parliament. No fatalities were reported. It is the most significant eastern-sector incident since 1987.

The statistics problem. Disputed You will see figures quoted for “transgressions” per year — 300, 400, 600. These come from internal Indian assessments that have been reported in the press, not from published official data, and they count events under a definition that has changed over time. A transgression under Indian doctrine means a patrol crossing India’s perception of the LAC, which the other side does not regard as a crossing at all. Never quote a transgression statistic as an established fact. If pressed, say: “Reported figures run into the hundreds annually, but they reflect differing perceptions of the line rather than a count of deliberate intrusions, and the Government does not publish them systematically.” That answer is both accurate and more impressive than a number.

Doklam 2017

Seventy-three days, three countries, no shots, and a fundamental shift in how India handles this frontier. It is also the one episode in which India intervened militarily on behalf of a third country’s territorial claim — and you should be able to explain why that was lawful and why it was necessary.

The geography, which is the whole story

  • The Doklam plateau (Chinese: Donglang) lies at the trijunction of India (Sikkim), Bhutan and China, at the southern tip of the Chumbi valley — a wedge of Chinese territory that points south between Sikkim and Bhutan.
  • India holds that the trijunction is at Batang La; China holds that it is at Mount Gipmochi (Gyemochen), several kilometres further south, on its reading of the 1890 convention. Bhutan claims the plateau as Bhutanese territory.
  • South of the plateau lies the Jampheri (Zompelri) ridge. From it, observation extends towards the Siliguri corridor — the strip roughly 20-22 km wide at its narrowest that connects the North-East to the rest of India, carrying the rail, road, power and pipeline links to eight states and to India’s position in Bhutan and in the eastern Himalaya.

The sequence

  • 16 June 2017A PLA construction party begins extending a road southward on the Doklam plateau towards the Jampheri ridge.
  • 18 June 2017Indian troops from the Doka La post cross into the plateau and physically block the construction party — not by force but by standing in the way. Bulldozers stop.
  • 29 June 2017Bhutan issues a démarche to China stating that the construction is a direct violation of the 1988 and 1998 agreements between Bhutan and China to maintain the status quo pending a boundary settlement. This is the legal foundation of India’s position.
  • Jul-Aug 2017Intense Chinese rhetoric, including repeated references to 1962 and a lengthy Foreign Ministry document asserting Chinese sovereignty. India maintains a deliberately low-key public posture, avoiding escalatory statements.
  • 28 Aug 2017Mutual disengagement announced. Chinese road-building towards the ridge stops; both sides pull back their confronting personnel. Days later Prime Minister Modi attends the BRICS summit at Xiamen.
  • 2018Wuhan informal summit (27-28 April) between Modi and Xi, followed by Mamallapuram in October 2019 — an attempt to stabilise the relationship at leader level with “strategic guidance” to the two militaries.

What India achieved

  • Road construction towards the Jampheri ridge stopped. The immediate objective was met.
  • India demonstrated it would act militarily for Bhutan’s territorial position, reinforcing a security relationship China has been actively courting away.
  • It was managed without a shot, without public escalation, and without the relationship collapsing.
  • The legal ground was solid: India acted at Bhutan’s instance on a Bhutanese claim, invoking Bhutan-China agreements rather than asserting an Indian claim.

What China achieved, and the caveats

  • Established Satellite imagery reported by multiple independent analysts since 2018 shows substantial Chinese construction in the wider Doklam area — roads, helipads, and permanent positions — away from the specific face-off site.
  • Chinese settlements have been documented in areas of northern and western Bhutan that Bhutan claims, including Pangda in the Amo Chu valley.
  • Bhutan and China have since advanced their own boundary process: a Three-Step Roadmap MoU in October 2021 and subsequent rounds including work on a Joint Technical Team for delimitation.
  • Interpretation The tactical standoff was an Indian success; the strategic position on the plateau has arguably moved against India since. Say both.
How to answer “was Doklam a victory?”

Do not give a yes or a no. Say: “Tactically yes — the road stopped, the objective was denied, and it was done without firing. Strategically it is more mixed, because Chinese construction elsewhere on the plateau has continued and Bhutan’s own boundary process with China has advanced. The clearest lesson is that on this frontier a win is rarely permanent; presence has to be sustained, not just asserted once.” That answer shows you can distinguish levels of war, which is exactly what the assessment is looking for.

2020: Galwan and the eastern Ladakh crisis

The most serious India-China confrontation since 1962, and the event that ended the post-1988 framework. Get the sequence right, get the numbers right, and be careful with the numbers you cannot verify.

The build-up

  • April-early May 2020: large-scale PLA movement into eastern Ladakh, with formations reportedly moved from exercise areas deep in Tibet and Xinjiang to forward positions. Multiple friction points activate simultaneously — which is what distinguishes 2020 from every previous incident.
  • 5-6 May 2020: a violent confrontation on the north bank of Pangong Tso, with injuries on both sides.
  • 9 May 2020: a separate brawl at Naku La in Sikkim.
  • By late May, Indian patrols find themselves blocked at several traditional patrolling points in Galwan, Hot Springs, Gogra, the Kugrang valley, the Pangong north bank and Depsang.
  • 6 June 2020: the first Corps Commander-level talks at Moldo produce an agreement in principle on mutual disengagement, including from the Galwan Valley.

15 June 2020 — the Galwan clash

  • Established An Indian party led by Colonel B. Santosh Babu, Commanding Officer of 16 Bihar, went to verify that a Chinese position at Patrolling Point 14 had been removed as agreed on 6 June. It had not.
  • The confrontation escalated into hand-to-hand fighting on a narrow ridge above the Galwan river, in darkness, at around 4,300 m, lasting several hours. No firearms were used, in compliance with the 1996 and 2005 protocols. Improvised weapons were used, including iron rods and clubs reported to have been wrapped in barbed wire or studded with nails.
  • Many casualties on both sides resulted from falls into the gorge and from the freezing water of the river.
  • Established Twenty Indian soldiers were killed, including Colonel Santosh Babu, who was posthumously awarded the Maha Vir Chakra. Naib Subedar Nuduram Soren, Havildar K. Palani and Sepoy Gurtej Singh were among those awarded the Vir Chakra. Seventy-six were injured.
  • Disputed China did not acknowledge any casualties for eight months. In February 2021 it announced four deaths and one severely injured officer, Qi Fabao, who was later given a prominent public role. Independent researchers, an Australian newspaper investigation and Russian state media have published significantly higher figures; none is independently verified and you should not quote any of them as fact. The correct formulation is: “China has acknowledged four; several independent assessments suggest more; no figure has been independently verified.”
  • Ten Indian personnel taken during the fighting were returned on 18 June 2020.
The detail that explains the whole event

Why did soldiers of two nuclear-armed states fight with clubs? Because the 1996 CBM agreement prohibits firing within two kilometres of the LAC, and the 2005 protocol and 2013 BDCA require self-restraint in face-to-face situations. Both armies had internalised these rules for decades. Galwan is therefore, paradoxically, evidence that the agreements were being observed in one respect even as they failed in every other. Established After Galwan, India confirmed that field commanders retain full freedom of action to use all resources at their disposal in extraordinary situations — a significant change in rules of engagement, publicly signalled.

Why did China do it? The competing explanations — present two or three, not one.

  • Infrastructure trigger. The completion of the Darbuk-Shyok-Daulat Beg Oldi road in 2019-20, and the Shyok bridge, gave India year-round access to Sub-Sector North for the first time. Beijing may have acted to pre-empt a changing local balance. Interpretation Widely held; the timing fits.
  • Reaction to August 2019. The reorganisation of Jammu and Kashmir and the creation of the Union Territory of Ladakh, with Indian ministerial statements about Aksai Chin, may have been read in Beijing as a change to the territorial status quo.
  • Opportunism in a pandemic. The world was absorbed by COVID-19 and the timing coincided with Chinese assertiveness in the South China Sea, on Taiwan and in Hong Kong. Part of a pattern rather than a bilateral episode.
  • Strategic signalling. A warning against India’s deepening alignment with the United States, the Quad and Indo-Pacific frameworks.
  • Local initiative. A minority view that the scale of movement was greater than intended and that escalation at Galwan was not centrally directed. Interpretation Not widely held, given the simultaneity across multiple sectors.

India’s response

  • Military: rapid forward deployment of additional divisions; the re-roling of I Corps from a Pakistan-facing strike role to the northern borders; winter stocking and habitat for tens of thousands of troops in Ladakh; forward deployment of tanks, artillery and air defence; sustained air-lift by C-17 and C-130J.
  • Economic: Press Note 3 of April 2020 requiring government approval for FDI from countries sharing a land border with India; the banning of 59 Chinese mobile applications on 29 June 2020, including TikTok, with further tranches taking the total past 250; exclusion of Chinese vendors in practice from 5G trials; scrutiny of Chinese participation in public procurement.
  • Diplomatic: the External Affairs Minister and Chinese Foreign Minister met in Moscow on 10 September 2020 and agreed a five-point consensus: observe existing agreements, disengage quickly, avoid escalation, continue dialogue through the SRs and WMCC, and work on new CBMs. The Defence Ministers had met there days earlier.
  • Political: the formulation that the relationship cannot be normal while the border is not — repeated consistently since.

The Kailash Range operation

The least discussed and most instructive Indian action of the 2020 crisis. It is the clearest modern example of creating leverage rather than merely reacting to a fait accompli.

  • Established On the night of 29-30 August 2020, Indian troops — including elements of the Special Frontier Force — pre-emptively occupied a series of unheld dominating heights on the Kailash Range, along the south bank of Pangong Tso and the Chushul bowl: Rechin La, Rezang La, Gurung Hill, Magar Hill, Mukhpari, Helmet Top and the features overlooking the Spanggur Gap.
  • The heights gave Indian forces observation over the Chinese garrison at Moldo and over the Spanggur Gap, the main approach for armour into the Chushul bowl.
  • Company Leader Nyima Tenzin of the Special Frontier Force was killed by a landmine during the operation; his funeral in Leh, attended by senior officials, was the first public acknowledgement of the SFF’s Tibetan composition in decades.
  • Established In the days that followed, warning shots were fired in the air near Mukhpari — the first shots on the LAC in about forty-five years. Both sides accused the other of firing first; both confirmed that shots were fired.
  • The heights were vacated in February 2021 as part of the mutual Pangong disengagement.

The case that it was the right move

  • It converted India from a reactive to a bargaining position; there was now something on the table to trade.
  • It produced the first substantive disengagement, at Pangong, in February 2021.
  • It demonstrated an operational capability — night movement and occupation of unheld heights at extreme altitude — that changes planning assumptions on the other side.
  • It restored deterrent credibility after Galwan without firing on anyone.

The case that the trade was poor

  • India gave up dominating ground in exchange for Chinese withdrawal from an area where it had advanced only recently.
  • The resulting buffer zones restrict Indian patrolling in areas India had patrolled before 2020, while the Chinese rear infrastructure has grown.
  • Interpretation This is a live debate among Indian analysts. The government’s position is that disengagement was sequential and that no territory was conceded. Critics point to loss of patrolling access. Present both.
The principle to extract

Deterrence on a disputed frontier is not about matching the adversary where he has moved; it is about making his position uncomfortable somewhere he has not planned for. Sumdorong Chu in 1986 and Kailash in 2020 are the same idea executed thirty-four years apart. If you want a single example of Indian strategic thinking on this frontier to offer a board, this is the one — and the fact that you know it happened at all will distinguish you.

The formulation to use about the present state of play

Do not say the crisis is over, and do not say nothing has changed. Say: “Sir, disengagement at the friction points has been completed in stages and patrolling arrangements were agreed for Depsang and Demchok in October 2024. Interpretation The most defensible summary is that 2020 produced a new and more heavily militarised normal, not a return to the old one.

Part VI

The military dimension

Military geography of the frontier

Every argument about this border eventually reduces to terrain. If you can explain the geography, you can explain the strategy — and you will not need to memorise very much else.

The structural asymmetry

China’s advantages

  • Flat rear. The Tibetan plateau is high but level, which makes road, rail, pipeline and airfield construction comparatively cheap and fast.
  • Interior lines. Lateral movement between sectors along the plateau is straightforward; forces can be shifted from the eastern to the western sector without descending.
  • Altitude advantage on approach. Forces come down to the border from garrisons already at altitude.
  • Rail. Qinghai-Tibet (2006), Lhasa-Shigatse (2014) and Lhasa-Nyingchi (2021) lines allow heavy-lift movement no Indian sector can match.
  • Civil-military fusion. Dual-use villages, airports and roads built under civilian budgets.

India’s advantages

  • Acclimatisation and experience. Indian formations live at altitude continuously, including on the Siachen Glacier since 1984 — the most demanding sustained high-altitude deployment any army maintains.
  • Defensive terrain. The Indian side is steep and compartmented. Attacking down into it is easier than attacking up out of it; holding it is easier still.
  • Air power economics. Indian airbases in the plains — Chandigarh, Ambala, Bareilly, Hasimara, Tezpur, Chabua, Jorhat — launch with full fuel and weapon loads. Chinese airfields on the plateau suffer significant payload and range penalties from thin air and high field elevation.
  • Shorter lines in the east. The Brahmaputra valley is close behind the eastern sector.
  • Local knowledge and population. The Indian side is inhabited; the Tibetan side is sparsely populated and, in Beijing’s own assessment, not uniformly reliable.

Choke points and vulnerabilities to know

  • Siliguri corridor (“Chicken’s Neck”) — roughly 20-22 km at its narrowest, carrying every land link to eight North-Eastern states. The single most cited Indian vulnerability. Doklam is about this.
  • Chumbi valley — a Chinese salient pointing south between Sikkim and Bhutan, but also a trap: narrow, overlooked from both flanks, and difficult to reinforce under fire. India’s holding of Nathu La and Cho La since 1967 is what makes it a trap.
  • Sub-Sector North and the Depsang Plains — the only armour country in Ladakh, adjoining the Karakoram Pass and approaches to Siachen and the Shaksgam.
  • Zoji La and the Ladakh road links — until the Zoji La tunnel is completed, winter access to Ladakh by road is seasonal, with the Manali-Leh and Nimmu-Padam-Darcha axes as alternatives.
  • Se La and the Tawang axis — the 1962 axis; the Sela Tunnel now gives all-weather access.
  • The Assam-Arunachal road network — a small number of bridges over the Brahmaputra and its tributaries carry everything.
The nuance that separates a good answer from a textbook answer

The “Chicken’s Neck” is India’s most discussed vulnerability, and it is real. But note two qualifications, because a good board member will test whether you can: first, a corridor is only cut if forces can be sustained across it, and an advance to the Siliguri corridor from the Chumbi valley means moving a force down a narrow, overlooked valley across Bhutanese territory in the face of Indian air power and reserves; second, India has been building redundancy — new road alignments, the Sevoke-Rangpo rail link, inland waterways on the Brahmaputra, and transit arrangements with Bangladesh that reduce dependence on the corridor. Say the vulnerability is real and being addressed. That is more accurate than either alarm or dismissal.

The infrastructure race

This is where the contest is actually being conducted. Not battles — bulldozers. If you follow only one strand of this subject in the news, follow this one.

China’s build-out

  • Rail: Qinghai-Tibet (Golmud-Lhasa, 2006), Lhasa-Shigatse (2014), Lhasa-Nyingchi (June 2021, bringing rail within roughly 20 km of the Arunachal frontier in places). The Sichuan-Tibet railway is under construction and is among the most difficult engineering projects ever attempted.
  • Roads: the G219 across Aksai Chin and the G216, with extensive lateral and feeder networks; new roads to forward positions opposite Ladakh and Arunachal; the reported G695 alignment running closer to the LAC in the western sector.
  • Air: airports and airbases including Lhasa Gonggar, Shigatse, Ngari Gunsa, Nyingchi, Hotan, Kashgar and Golmud, with hardened shelters, extended runways and new heliports reported by open-source analysts since 2020. Tingri and other new facilities have been reported.
  • Xiaokang (“well-off”) border villages: Established China has built several hundred model villages along its Himalayan frontier under a programme announced around 2017, including in areas disputed with Bhutan and close to the LAC. Interpretation Analysts widely read these as dual-use — population as presence, and presence as claim.
  • Land Border Law: enacted October 2021, in force from 1 January 2022, formalising state responsibility for border defence, development and population settlement. India formally noted its concern that a unilateral domestic law could affect bilateral arrangements.

The PLA and Western Theater Command

Know your adversary’s organisation. This is straightforward factual material and very few candidates have it, which makes it disproportionately valuable.

The reforms of 2015-16

  • Xi Jinping’s military reform replaced seven Military Regions with five Theater Commands — Eastern, Southern, Western, Northern and Central — designed for joint operations under a single theatre commander.
  • New services and arms were created: the PLA Rocket Force (from the Second Artillery), the Strategic Support Force for space, cyber and electronic warfare — since 2024 reorganised into the Aerospace Force, Cyberspace Force and Information Support Force reporting to the Central Military Commission — and a Joint Logistics Support Force.
  • Group Armies were reorganised around combined-arms brigades, making the brigade rather than the division the basic manoeuvre unit — a shift towards modular, rapidly deployable formations.
  • The CMC under Xi exercises tighter control; the doctrinal formulation is to fight and win “informatised” and now “intelligentised” local wars.

Western Theater Command

  • The largest theatre by area, responsible for the entire Indian frontier plus Xinjiang, Tibet and internal security in both. Headquarters at Chengdu.
  • Subordinate structures relevant to India include the Xinjiang Military District (and within it the South Xinjiang Military District, the counterpart to India’s XIV Corps) and the Tibet Military District, together with Group Armies that can reinforce into the theatre.
  • Established Western Theater Command has seen an unusually high turnover of commanders in recent years — a fact frequently noted by analysts, though the reasons are not publicly known.
  • Force composition includes combined-arms brigades with light and medium wheeled and tracked vehicles suited to plateau conditions, substantial rocket artillery, air-defence and electronic-warfare units, army aviation, and border defence regiments; supported by PLA Air Force units operating from plateau bases and by Rocket Force assets.
  • Reserve dependence: the theatre’s peacetime strength is modest relative to its area; Chinese planning depends heavily on rapid reinforcement by rail and air from outside the theatre. Interpretation This is the principal Chinese operational vulnerability and it is why Indian analysts watch Chinese rail construction so closely.

Doctrine and how it shows up on the LAC

  • Active defence — strategically defensive, operationally and tactically offensive; the doctrine permits pre-emption where a threat is judged imminent.
  • Systems destruction warfare — attacking the adversary’s command, communications, sensors and logistics as a system rather than seeking attrition.
  • The “three warfares” — public opinion warfare, psychological warfare and legal warfare, formally endorsed in Chinese political-work regulations from 2003. These are not metaphors; they are assigned tasks with dedicated units.
  • Short, sharp war — a preference for limited objectives achieved quickly and consolidated politically, which describes 1962, 1967 from Beijing’s perspective, 1979 against Vietnam, and the 2020 moves.
  • Border defence troops and militia — including herder militia and the xiaokang village populations, used to establish presence without committing regular formations.
The comparison a board may invite

If asked to compare the two armies, resist raw numbers. Say: “The PLA is larger, better funded and better equipped in most categories, and it has reorganised for joint operations ahead of us. What India has on this particular frontier is terrain that favours the defender, formations that are permanently acclimatised and continuously operationally employed, air bases that do not pay an altitude penalty, and a force that has been fighting at high altitude continuously since 1984. Capability on a frontier is not an army-wide comparison; it is a comparison of what each side can bring to bear at a particular place in a particular season.” That is the professionally correct way to answer, and it avoids both jingoism and defeatism.

Indian capabilities and reorientation

The most important change since 2020 is not any single piece of equipment. It is that the Indian Army has begun reorienting from a Pakistan-primary posture to a China-primary one — a shift of institutional gravity that takes a decade and is currently in progress.

Formations facing the northern border

  • Northern Command (Udhampur): XIV Corps at Leh — “Fire and Fury” — responsible for Ladakh including Siachen; XV and XVI Corps face Pakistan.
  • Central Command (Lucknow): responsible for the middle sector, with the Uttar Bharat Area and associated formations.
  • Eastern Command (Kolkata): XXXIII Corps (Sukna, Sikkim and the Siliguri corridor), IV Corps (Tezpur, Tawang and Kameng), III Corps (Dimapur, eastern Arunachal).
  • XVII Mountain Strike Corps (Panagarh), raised from 2013, India’s first offensive formation dedicated to the mountains. Its structure and resourcing have been debated and revised; one of its divisions has been re-roled.
  • Established I Corps, historically a Pakistan-facing strike corps, was re-oriented to the northern borders in 2021 — the single clearest institutional signal of the change in priority.
  • Indo-Tibetan Border Police mans the border outposts in peacetime. Established A decision to place ITBP border operations under closer coordination with the Army has been a long-running policy debate; sanction for additional ITBP battalions and border outposts was approved in 2023-24.

  • Acclimatisation is staged and non-negotiable. The Indian Army uses a graduated schedule of several days at each of three stages before troops operate at extreme altitude. Troops flown directly from the plains into battle — as happened in 1962 — suffer casualties from altitude before the enemy fires a shot.
  • Oxygen at 5,000 m is roughly half that at sea level. Loads that are routine in the plains become exhausting; a section attack takes several times as long.
  • Cold injury. Frostbite, chilblains and trench foot are the constant enemy. Wind chill at Ladakh and Siachen altitudes can drive effective temperatures far below −40 °C.
  • Non-battle casualties dominate. On the Siachen Glacier, historically, the great majority of Indian and Pakistani deaths have come from weather, avalanche, crevasses and altitude illness rather than from enemy action.

How altitude changes the fighting

  • Helicopters lose lift. Rotary-wing payload falls sharply with density altitude; a helicopter that lifts a useful load at sea level may lift a fraction of it at 5,000 m. This is why the Cheetah/Chetak fleet, and now the Prachand and Dhruv, matter so much, and why the Chinook is transformative.
  • Aircraft lose payload. Field elevation and thin air reduce take-off weight for both transport and combat aircraft — the asymmetry that favours Indian plains bases over Chinese plateau bases.
  • Ballistics change. Thin air means less drag and longer ranges; firing tables must be corrected for altitude, temperature and extreme angles of sight. Mortar and artillery fire in deep valleys involves very high angles and dead ground everywhere.
  • Logistics multiply. Fuel for heating is as important as ammunition. Kerosene, rations, water (everything freezes), batteries (which lose capacity in cold) and medical oxygen all have to be moved forward and stocked before the passes close.
  • Winter stocking is the annual strategic event. Each year before the passes close, India stocks eastern Ladakh for the winter — fuel, rations, ammunition, medical stores and construction material for tens of thousands of troops. Established After 2020 this expanded enormously, with new smart camps and prefabricated habitat with integrated heating, water and electricity.
  • Equipment must be specified for the environment. Extreme cold weather clothing systems, multi-fuel heaters, insulated habitat, special lubricants, and vehicles with cold-start capability.
The institution to name

The High Altitude Warfare School (HAWS) at Gulmarg, established in 1948 (as a ski school, later expanded), is among the most respected mountain-warfare training establishments in the world and trains personnel from many foreign armies. Alongside it, the Siachen Battle School and the corps battle schools conduct acclimatisation and pre-induction training. Naming HAWS in an interview signals that you know how the Army actually generates this capability rather than merely that it possesses it.

Siachen, in one paragraph, because it will come up. Established India launched Operation Meghdoot on 13 April 1984 to pre-empt a Pakistani move onto the Saltoro Ridge, securing the passes of Sia La, Bilafond La and Gyong La. India has held the dominating Saltoro Ridge since. The glacier is relevant to the China question because it sits between Pakistan-administered territory and the Chinese-held Shaksgam Tract, and because Indian positions there overlook the approaches that would link the two. It is also the world’s highest battlefield and the reason the Indian Army has unmatched institutional experience of extreme-altitude sustainment.

Air power, ISR, drones and space

The 1957 failure to notice a highway is the origin of this whole area of effort. Surveillance is now the frontier’s primary activity in peacetime.

Surveillance and reconnaissance

  • Space: the Cartosat series for imaging, RISAT synthetic aperture radar satellites for all-weather and night imaging, EMISAT for electronic intelligence, GSAT-7A for Air Force communications and GSAT-7 “Rukmini” for the Navy, and the NavIC regional navigation constellation. The Defence Space Agency was created in 2019. Re-verify the current constellation and any new surveillance satellite approvals.
  • Airborne: Netra AEW&C on Embraer platforms and the Phalcon A-50EI AWACS; the AWACS-India programme for additional platforms; P-8I maritime patrol aircraft, which were notably used over eastern Ladakh in 2020 for overland surveillance.
  • Unmanned: Heron and upgraded Heron Mark II medium-altitude long-endurance UAVs, Searcher tactical UAVs, Harop loitering munitions, the leased and then contracted MQ-9B (a deal for 31 aircraft was signed in October 2024), and a rapidly growing inventory of small Indian-made quadcopters, logistics drones and loitering munitions procured under emergency powers.
  • Ground: battlefield surveillance radars, the Swathi weapon-locating radar, long-range observation and thermal imaging systems, and unattended ground sensors.

The drone dimension

  • China fields a large and diverse UAV inventory and exports heavily, including to Pakistan. Swarm capability and high-altitude long-endurance types are areas of Chinese investment.
  • India’s response has three strands: acquisition of high-end platforms, a domestic drone industry accelerated by the Drone Rules 2021 and a production-linked incentive scheme, and counter-UAS systems, including directed-energy and jamming solutions.
  • Interpretation High-altitude drone operations are genuinely hard — thin air reduces lift and endurance, cold degrades batteries, and terrain masks line-of-sight control. Drone capability at 4,000 m is not the same problem as drone capability at sea level, and claims about swarm employment in the Himalaya should be treated sceptically by both sides.
A sentence worth having ready

“The most important weapon on the Line of Actual Control is a camera.” Nothing is fired in peacetime; everything is watched. The contest is over who can observe, record, patrol and document presence — and the side that can see continuously, in all weather, day and night, can contest a claim without contesting it by force. That is why satellites, radar imaging, long-endurance drones and even the Vibrant Villages Programme belong in the same answer.

The nuclear and missile dimension

Handle this section with precision and restraint. Nuclear questions are where candidates most often say something careless.

The basic facts

  • China tested first, at Lop Nur on 16 October 1964 — two years after the war. It is a recognised nuclear-weapon state under the NPT and a permanent member of the UN Security Council.
  • India conducted a “peaceful nuclear explosion” at Pokhran on 18 May 1974 and a series of tests on 11 and 13 May 1998, declaring itself a nuclear-weapon state. It is not a party to the NPT.
  • Disputed Arsenal sizes are estimates, not facts. Independent research institutions have estimated Chinese warheads in the several hundreds and rising, with US Department of Defense projections of significant further growth by 2030; Indian estimates are typically around 170-180. Always say “estimated” and give a range.

Doctrines compared

India

  • Credible minimum deterrence.
  • No first use, with the qualification introduced in the 2003 Cabinet Committee on Security statement that India retains the option of nuclear retaliation against a major attack with biological or chemical weapons.
  • Massive retaliation designed to inflict unacceptable damage, in response to a first strike.
  • Civilian political control through the Nuclear Command Authority (Political Council chaired by the Prime Minister, Executive Council chaired by the NSA), with the Strategic Forces Command (2003) as the executing agency.
  • No use against non-nuclear-weapon states.

China

  • No first use, declared since 1964 and consistently restated.
  • Historically a small, recessed force; since roughly 2020 a rapid expansion in warheads, silos and delivery systems documented by open-source analysis of silo-field construction.
  • A full triad: land-based (DF series), sea-based (JL-series SLBMs on Jin-class submarines) and an air leg.
  • Conventional-nuclear entanglement in systems such as the DF-26, which is dual-capable — a recognised escalation risk.

Delivery systems relevant to this frontier

  • India: the Agni family — Agni-I through Agni-V, with Agni-V (range over 5,000 km, first tested 2012, canisterised versions since 2018) bringing all of China within reach; Mission Divyastra, the first flight test of Agni-V with MIRV technology, was announced on 11 March 2024. Agni-P is a newer-generation intermediate system. The sea leg rests on the Arihant-class SSBNs with K-15 and K-4 missiles — the survivable second-strike component that a no-first-use doctrine requires.
  • China: DF-21 and DF-26 intermediate-range systems relevant to the Indian theatre; DF-31 and DF-41 intercontinental systems; extensive conventional short- and medium-range rocket forces deployed in the western theatre.
The answer on nuclear escalation in a border crisis

If asked whether a border clash could go nuclear, the disciplined answer is: “Very unlikely, and both sides’ behaviour shows why. Both declare no first use, both have kept LAC incidents deliberately non-lethal in weapons terms, and both have strong incentives to contain any incident below the threshold of general war. The real risk is not deliberate escalation but miscalculation — an incident that gets out of local control before the political level can intervene. That is precisely what the hotlines, the border personnel meetings, and the corps commander channel exist to prevent, and it is why India has continued to talk through those channels even at the worst moments.” That answer is measured, informed and demonstrates that you understand escalation management rather than escalation drama.

Part VII

The wider contest

China-Pakistan and the collusive threat

India is the only major power that faces two nuclear-armed neighbours who are strategic partners of each other and adversaries of it. This is the defining structural feature of Indian defence planning, and you should be able to state it without sounding alarmed.

The origins and the territorial link

  • The relationship took shape after 1962, in the shared interest of two states that had each fought India. Established The Sino-Pakistan Boundary Agreement of 2 March 1963 saw Pakistan transfer the Shaksgam / Trans-Karakoram Tract (~5,180 km²) to China. India rejects the agreement as invalid on the ground that the territory is part of Jammu and Kashmir. The agreement itself contains a provision for renegotiation once the Kashmir dispute is settled — a clause India cites as evidence that even the parties knew the title was defective.
  • The Karakoram Highway, completed in the late 1970s, runs through Gilgit-Baltistan — territory India claims — linking Xinjiang to Pakistan.

The military dimension

  • China is by a very wide margin Pakistan’s largest arms supplier. Systems include the JF-17 Thunder (co-produced), J-10C fighters, HQ-9 and other air-defence systems, Type 054A/P frigates, Hangor-class submarines on order, VT-4 tanks, SH-15 artillery and a wide range of missiles and UAVs.
  • Cooperation extends to space and satellite services, and there is a long-standing body of analysis on Chinese assistance to Pakistan’s nuclear and missile programmes historically. Disputed The details of that assistance are contested; do not state specifics as fact.
  • Disputed Following Operation Sindoor in May 2025, senior Indian officers stated publicly that Pakistan had employed Chinese-supplied systems and that China had provided support including situational awareness. Chinese officials denied direct participation. Present this as Indian official statements and Chinese denial, not as established fact, and note that independent verification is not available in open sources.
  • The doctrinal consequence: Indian planning has moved from a “two-front” concept to what General Bipin Rawat described as a “two-and-a-half front” problem — China, Pakistan, and internal security. Whether the two fronts would actually be simultaneous is contested; the planning assumption is that they might be.

CPEC and the economic dimension

  • The China-Pakistan Economic Corridor, announced in 2015 as the flagship of the Belt and Road Initiative, with headline figures around US$ 46-62 billion, links Kashgar in Xinjiang to Gwadar on the Arabian Sea through road, rail, pipeline and energy projects.
  • India’s objection is one of sovereignty: CPEC passes through Gilgit-Baltistan, which India claims. This is the stated reason India has refused to join the Belt and Road Initiative and boycotted both BRI forums — the only major country to do so publicly and on a stated principle.
  • The strategic concern: Gwadar gives China a presence on the Arabian Sea near the approaches to the Strait of Hormuz, and CPEC in principle offers an overland route reducing Chinese dependence on the Malacca Strait — the so-called “Malacca dilemma.” Interpretation Analysts differ on how much dependence a pipeline and road across the Karakoram can realistically relieve; the volumes involved are very small relative to seaborne energy flows. Note the limitation — it shows judgement.
  • CPEC has faced serious difficulties: security incidents involving Chinese nationals in Balochistan and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Pakistani debt distress, delayed projects and disputes over circular debt in the power sector.
How to discuss the two-front problem without sounding fatalistic

Say: “A genuinely simultaneous two-front war is the planning worst case, not the likely case. Both adversaries have their own constraints, and full collusion would carry costs for China that it has so far avoided paying. The practical Indian response has not been to plan for two full wars, but to build capabilities that work on either front — mountain-capable formations, strategic lift, integrated air defence, and stand-off weapons — while using diplomacy and the maritime domain to raise the cost of collusion. Capability that is flexible is worth more than capability that is duplicated.”

Water, rivers and the Medog dam

The rivers are the one dimension of this relationship where the asymmetry is absolute and India has almost no leverage. It is also the issue most likely to grow in importance over your career.

The geography of dependence

  • The Brahmaputra rises in Tibet as the Yarlung Tsangpo, flows east across the plateau for some 1,600 km, then makes the Great Bend around Namcha Barwa and drops steeply into Arunachal Pradesh, where it is called the Siang, becoming the Brahmaputra in Assam.
  • The Indus and the Sutlej also rise in Tibet, making China an upper riparian for rivers on which both India and Pakistan depend.
  • Established India is a lower riparian with respect to China, and an upper riparian with respect to Bangladesh and Pakistan. India’s own arguments in each direction are therefore in tension — a point an honest candidate should acknowledge.

The Medog / Motuo project

  • Established China approved a mega hydropower project on the lower Yarlung Tsangpo at the Great Bend, near Medog (Motuo) in December 2024, with construction commencing in 2025. Reported planned capacity is of the order of 60 GW — which would make it by a wide margin the largest hydropower project in the world, roughly three times the Three Gorges Dam.
  • The site exploits a drop of around 2,000 m over a short distance at the Great Bend. The design reported in Chinese sources involves diversion tunnels through the Namcha Barwa massif rather than a single conventional reservoir.
  • India’s concerns: alteration of flow timing and sediment load in the Siang and Brahmaputra; the possibility of sudden releases; ecological effects on the Assam floodplain; and the precedent of unilateral action on a shared river without a treaty framework. Interpretation Most hydrologists note that the Brahmaputra draws a large share of its flow from monsoon rainfall below the Chinese section, which limits — but does not eliminate — the physical leverage. State this; it is the mark of an informed answer rather than an alarmed one.
  • India’s response: the proposed Siang Upper Multipurpose Project in Arunachal Pradesh, reported at around 11,000 MW, partly justified as creating a buffer against upstream flow manipulation. It faces significant local opposition on displacement and ecological grounds — a genuine dilemma worth acknowledging.

The data-sharing arrangements

  • India and China have operated Memoranda of Understanding on hydrological data sharing for the Brahmaputra (from 2002, renewed periodically) and the Sutlej (from 2005), under which China provides flood-season data for specified stations.
  • Established China suspended the supply of Brahmaputra data during 2017, the year of the Doklam standoff, while continuing to supply it to Bangladesh — which India took as a demonstration that the arrangement is political rather than technical.
  • There is no water-sharing treaty between India and China, and no basin-wide institution. An Expert Level Mechanism on trans-border rivers meets periodically. Re-verify the current status of the MoUs, which require periodic renewal.
The balanced position on “water wars”

Avoid the phrase. Say instead: “The risk is less that China would weaponise water in the sense of deliberately flooding Assam — the engineering and diplomatic costs of that would be very high — and more that India would be exposed to unilateral decisions about timing, sediment and flow on a river it depends on, with no treaty, no institution and no right of consultation. The core Indian interest is therefore procedural: a basin framework and reliable data, not a specific volume of water.” That reframes the issue from confrontation to institutions, which is both more accurate and a better GD contribution.

Economic interdependence

The strangest feature of this rivalry: while the two armies faced each other in Ladakh, bilateral trade reached record levels. Any serious answer has to explain that contradiction rather than ignore it.

The shape of the relationship

  • China is among India’s largest trading partners in goods, and in several recent years the largest. Re-verify the current ranking and figures; they move year to year and are reported differently by Indian and Chinese sources.
  • Established The trade is heavily imbalanced. India’s goods trade deficit with China has run in the range of roughly US$ 85-100 billion and above in recent years, the largest deficit India has with any country. Quote a range and a year, never a bare number.
  • What India imports: electronics and telecom equipment, electrical machinery, industrial machinery, active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) and key starting materials, organic chemicals, fertilisers, solar cells and modules, rare earth magnets, auto components, plastics.
  • What India exports: iron ore, refined petroleum products, marine products, cotton, organic chemicals, and some specialty products. The pattern is close to the classic raw-material-for-manufactures exchange.
  • The dependency that worries planners most: APIs. A very large share — commonly cited at around 70 per cent — of India’s bulk drug imports come from China, which is a national-security issue for a country that supplies a large fraction of the world’s generic medicines. The Production Linked Incentive schemes for bulk drugs and medical devices (2020) were a direct response.

Measures taken since 2020

  • Press Note 3 (April 2020): investment from any country sharing a land border with India requires prior government approval. In practice it applies principally to Chinese investment and has significantly slowed it.
  • App bans: 59 applications banned on 29 June 2020, with further tranches taking the total past 250.
  • Telecom: the National Security Directive on the Telecommunication Sector (2021) and the trusted-sources regime have effectively excluded Chinese vendors from 5G networks.
  • Procurement: restrictions on bidders from land-border countries in public procurement; scrutiny of Chinese participation in infrastructure projects.
  • Positive-side measures: Production Linked Incentive schemes across fourteen sectors; the Semiconductor Mission; the National Critical Mineral Mission and overseas acquisition efforts through KABIL; supply-chain initiatives with Japan and Australia and through the Indo-Pacific Economic Framework.

The uncomfortable fact to state honestly. Despite all of the above, imports from China have risen, not fallen, since 2020. Two reasons: India’s own manufacturing expansion under the PLI schemes itself requires Chinese capital goods, components and intermediates; and some trade has been re-routed through third countries rather than eliminated. Interpretation The honest conclusion is that decoupling is not currently available to India; de-risking in specific critical sectors is. Saying this shows economic literacy and will impress far more than a claim that India has reduced dependence.

The rare earths example, if you want one concrete illustration

China dominates the mining and, far more decisively, the processing of rare earth elements and the manufacture of permanent magnets. In April 2025 China imposed export controls on several heavy rare earths and magnet products; the effects were felt quickly across automotive and electronics supply chains globally, including in India. This is the model of modern economic leverage: not tariffs or embargoes on finished goods, but control of a narrow chokepoint deep in the supply chain that is expensive and slow for anyone else to replicate. India’s response — the National Critical Mineral Mission, overseas acquisition, recycling and processing incentives — is correctly aimed, but building processing capacity takes years, not months. Re-verify the current state of these controls.

Indo-Pacific, Quad and the maritime flank

India’s single greatest asymmetric advantage over China is at sea, and it is worth understanding why that is a geographical fact rather than a claim about relative naval strength.

The logic of the maritime flank

  • China imports a very large share of its energy and a great deal of its trade through the Indian Ocean and the Strait of Malacca. Hu Jintao’s term for this vulnerability — the “Malacca dilemma” — is Chinese, not Indian.
  • India sits astride those routes with peninsular geography, the Andaman and Nicobar Islands commanding the western approaches to Malacca, and the Andaman and Nicobar Command — India’s only tri-service theatre command, established in 2001.
  • Interpretation The concept sometimes described as “horizontal escalation” — responding to pressure on a land frontier where one is disadvantaged by creating pressure at sea where one is advantaged — is much discussed by Indian analysts. It is not declared policy, and you should present it as an analytical concept rather than as India’s doctrine.

China in the Indian Ocean

  • The PLA Navy has operated anti-piracy task forces in the Gulf of Aden continuously since 2008, providing operational familiarity with the region.
  • China’s first overseas military base opened at Djibouti in 2017.
  • Port investments and access arrangements across the region — Gwadar, Hambantota, Kyaukphyu, and terminals elsewhere — are described by some analysts as a “String of Pearls.” Interpretation The term originated in an American consultancy report, not in Chinese doctrine, and analysts differ on whether commercial ports translate into military basing. Use it with that caveat.
  • Chinese research and survey vessels operating in the Indian Ocean, and port calls in Sri Lanka and the Maldives, have been a recurrent point of friction.

The Quad and India’s partnerships

  • The Quadrilateral Security Dialogue — India, the United States, Japan and Australia — began in 2007, lapsed, was revived at official level in 2017 and elevated to leaders’ summits from 2021. Its stated agenda is broad: vaccines, critical and emerging technology, climate, infrastructure, maritime domain awareness, and the Indo-Pacific Partnership for Maritime Domain Awareness (2022).
  • Established The Quad is not a military alliance and India has consistently said so. Malabar is a naval exercise that has included all four since 2020 but is formally separate from the Quad.
  • India’s foundational defence agreements with the United States — LEMOA (2016), COMCASA (2018), BECA (2020) — improve logistics, secure communications and geospatial cooperation. BECA in particular improves the accuracy of Indian targeting and navigation data.
  • India maintains parallel and sometimes contradictory memberships: BRICS, the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation and the Russia-India-China format alongside the Quad and the I2U2 and IMEC initiatives. Interpretation This is deliberate — the policy described as multi-alignment or strategic autonomy, which holds that India should participate in every forum that serves its interests without accepting the obligations of a bloc.
The question that catches candidates: “Is the Quad an Asian NATO?”

The answer is no, and you should be able to say why in three points: there is no mutual defence clause, which is what makes NATO an alliance; there is no integrated military command structure; and India has explicitly and repeatedly ruled out alliance membership, consistent with a policy of strategic autonomy that long predates the Quad. Then add the nuance that makes it a good answer: “That said, the Quad’s value to India is real but indirect — it raises the cost of coercion by signalling that India is not isolated, and it delivers practical goods in technology, supply chains and maritime domain awareness. It is a coalition of capability, not a guarantee of rescue, and Indian policy has been careful not to let it become one.”

Grey-zone and information warfare

Most of what happens between India and China happens below the threshold of armed conflict. Understanding this category is what lets you explain why the frontier is tense without a war.

What “grey zone” means

Coercive activity that is deliberately kept above routine competition but below the threshold that would justify an armed response. It is designed to achieve cumulative gains while denying the target a moment at which retaliation is clearly warranted.

The techniques used on this frontier

  • Salami slicing. Small, individually insignificant advances — a track, a hut, a herder camp, a patrol pattern — each too minor to fight over, cumulatively decisive. The Barahoti pattern of 1954 is the earliest example; Depsang and Doklam are the most recent.
  • Lawfare. Constructing a domestic legal basis for a claim. The Land Border Law in force from January 2022 is the clearest example; so is the invocation of the 1890 convention at Doklam.
  • Cartographic assertion. The “standard map” editions published annually, including the 2023 edition that showed Arunachal Pradesh and Aksai Chin as Chinese and drew formal Indian protest.
  • Toponymic assertion — renaming. Established China has issued successive batches of “standardised” Chinese names for places in Arunachal Pradesh — six in 2017, fifteen in 2021, eleven in 2023, around thirty in 2024, and further batches since. India’s response has been consistent: the Ministry of External Affairs states that assigning invented names does not alter the reality that Arunachal Pradesh is an integral and inalienable part of India. Being able to quote that formulation is worth a great deal.
  • Visa signalling. Issuing stapled visas to residents of Arunachal Pradesh and, at times, Jammu and Kashmir, rather than stamped visas — a way of denying that these are Indian citizens from Indian territory. India has responded by refusing travel on such documents.
  • Protest diplomacy. Routine objection to visits to Arunachal Pradesh by the Indian Prime Minister, President or foreign dignitaries, and to the Dalai Lama’s visits to Tawang.
  • Village settlement. The xiaokang programme — population as a claim-establishing instrument.
  • Dual-use civilian assets. Fishing and herder militia, civilian airports with military specifications, “research” vessels.

The information and cyber dimension

  • The three warfares — public opinion, psychological and legal — are formally part of PLA political work. In practice on the LAC this has included loudspeaker broadcasts, video releases timed for effect, selective disclosure of casualty information, and amplification of divisive narratives on social media.
  • Disputed Cyber intrusions attributed to China-linked groups by private threat-intelligence firms have included reported targeting of Indian power-sector infrastructure around 2020-21 and of Indian vaccine manufacturers during the pandemic. Attribution in cyberspace is inherently uncertain and these are private-sector attributions, not official ones. Say so.
  • India’s institutional response: the Defence Cyber Agency (2019), CERT-In, the National Critical Information Infrastructure Protection Centre, the National Cyber Security Coordinator, and the data-protection and telecom security frameworks.
The strategic problem grey-zone activity poses, stated crisply

“Deterrence works against an attack. It does not work against a process.” A state can deter an invasion by threatening unacceptable costs. It cannot deter a herder camp, a renamed village, a new track or a fishing fleet the same way, because responding proportionately looks trivial and responding disproportionately looks reckless. The answers that do work are unglamorous and cumulative: persistent presence, continuous surveillance, rapid documentation and publicity, matching infrastructure, and populated border villages. That is precisely the logic of the Vibrant Villages Programme and of India’s insistence on patrolling rights. If you can articulate that, you have understood the modern frontier better than most commentary on it.

Tibet, the Dalai Lama and succession

A subject that requires care. India’s position is settled and narrow, and you should be able to state it exactly, because loose talk here is diplomatically consequential.

India’s stated position

  • India recognises the Tibet Autonomous Region as part of the People’s Republic of China — restated formally in the 2003 declaration during Vajpayee’s visit.
  • India hosts the 14th Dalai Lama, who has lived in India since 1959, as an honoured religious leader and guest, and permits him full freedom of religious activity.
  • India does not permit Tibetans in India to conduct anti-China political activity on Indian soil — a commitment restated periodically.
  • The Central Tibetan Administration at Dharamsala is not recognised by India as a government. Around 70,000-90,000 Tibetans live in India, with settlements in Karnataka, Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Arunachal Pradesh and elsewhere; the Tibetan Rehabilitation Policy 2014 governs their status.

The succession question

  • The 14th Dalai Lama, born 6 July 1935, devolved his political authority in 2011 to a democratically elected Sikyong, ending a four-century link between the office and Tibetan temporal government. This is an important and under-appreciated fact: he deliberately removed the political dimension from the institution.
  • Established China maintains, under State Religious Affairs Bureau Order No. 5 (2007), that reincarnations of living Buddhas require state approval, and invokes the Qing-era Golden Urn procedure. In 1995 Beijing rejected the Dalai Lama’s recognition of the 11th Panchen Lama, Gedhun Choekyi Nyima, who disappeared from public view, and installed its own candidate.
  • The Dalai Lama has stated that the institution’s continuation will be decided by Tibetans, and in July 2025 he affirmed that the Gaden Phodrang Trust alone has the authority to recognise a successor. Re-verify the latest statements.
  • The likely scenario analysts expect: two claimants — one recognised by the Tibetan religious establishment, possibly identified outside China, and one designated by Beijing. India would then face a genuinely difficult choice with no good options.
  • The United States has legislated on Tibet — the Tibetan Policy and Support Act 2020 and the Promoting a Resolution to the Tibet-China Dispute Act (“Resolve Tibet Act”) of July 2024 — stating that the succession is a matter for Tibetans. India has not legislated comparably.
How to handle a question on “the Tibet card”

This is a trap for the careless. Do not say India should “use” Tibet, and do not speculate about Indian support for Tibetan independence — that is contrary to stated Indian policy and saying otherwise in an interview shows poor judgement, whatever your private view. The measured answer: “Sir, India’s position is settled: the Tibet Autonomous Region is part of China, His Holiness is an honoured religious guest, and India does not permit political activity against China from Indian soil. Where India does have a legitimate interest is that the succession to a religious office followed by millions of Indian citizens in the Himalayan belt — in Ladakh, Himachal, Sikkim and Arunachal — should be decided by the religious community concerned, not administratively. That is a position about religious freedom, which India can hold consistently with its own constitutional values.” That is accurate, defensible, and shows you can be principled without being reckless.

Part VIII

Analysis: competing readings, controversies and options

Competing perspectives

Four coherent accounts of the same seventy years. You will almost certainly be asked to take a position. Take one — but demonstrate first that you know what the others say.

1 · The mainstream Indian account

India inherited a boundary with a sound historical and geographical basis, sought friendship in good faith in the 1950s, was rewarded with the occupation of Aksai Chin and an unprovoked attack in 1962, and has since sought a negotiated settlement while China has repeatedly changed facts on the ground. The 2020 actions violated every agreement signed since 1993. India’s response has been restrained, proportionate and lawful.

Its strongest evidence: the Simla record; China’s acceptance of the same alignment with Burma; the 1993-2013 agreements that China’s 2020 build-up contravened; the sheer number of unilateral Chinese actions.

Its weakest point: it treats the western-sector claim as settled when the British record itself is genuinely divided.

2 · The Chinese official account

The boundary was never delimited. India inherited and then extended colonial claims, refused reasonable package settlements offered in 1960 and 1980, sheltered a separatist leader, provoked the 1962 conflict through the Forward Policy, and has since built infrastructure in disputed areas while aligning with external powers hostile to China. China’s actions are defensive responses.

Its strongest evidence: the absence of a ratified boundary treaty; the Macartney-MacDonald record; the fact that the Forward Policy was, in fact, a policy of advancing posts.

Its weakest point: it cannot explain why the same colonial-origin logic was acceptable in settlements with Burma, Nepal, Pakistan and others, and it does not address the scale and simultaneity of the 2020 movements.

3 · The revisionist Western account

Associated above all with Neville Maxwell’s India’s China War (1970) and, in a different register, with Alastair Lamb’s work on the frontier documents. It holds that India’s claims were maximalist, that the Forward Policy was provocative, and that China reacted to Indian actions rather than initiating aggression.

Its contribution: it forced attention onto the documentary record and onto Indian decision-making, and Lamb’s archival work on the Aitchison volumes is a serious piece of scholarship.

Its weaknesses, which Indian scholars including Steven Hoffmann, John Garver and Srinath Raghavan have documented: it relies heavily on a particular reading of a partial record, discounts Chinese agency and Chinese preparation, and does not explain the timing, scale or two-phase structure of the offensive, which indicate long planning rather than reaction.

4 · The structural-realist account

Neither state is the villain. Two large states, newly consolidated, with overlapping peripheries and incompatible views of their own centrality, were going to compete regardless of individual decisions. The 1950s buffer collapse made contact inevitable; the asymmetry in growth after 1980 made a re-test of the relationship inevitable; the border is where that competition is physically expressed because it is the only place the two states touch.

Its strength: it explains the persistence of the problem across very different governments in both countries.

Its weakness: it can excuse specific choices that were genuinely avoidable, and it offers little guidance on what to do.

How to take a position without being naive or shrill

A strong formulation: “Sir, I hold the Indian position, and I think the strongest single argument for it is that China accepted the same alignment with Burma in 1960 — which suggests the objection is not really to colonial lines. But I do not think the Indian record is spotless: the Forward Policy was militarily indefensible, and the western-sector claim rests on a British record that was genuinely divided. I would rather argue India’s case on the strong ground than pretend there is no weak ground.” That answer is patriotic, honest and analytically serious, and it is very hard to shake in cross-questioning.

Controversies

Ten arguments that recur. Know the shape of each. You do not need a verdict on all of them — you need to know where the evidence is thin.

  1. Did India miss a settlement in 1960? Interpretation The swap was probably genuinely available, and it is also probably true that no Indian government could have accepted it after the 1959 clashes and the Parliament resolution. The strongest version of the criticism is not “Nehru should have accepted” but “India should not have hardened its maps in 1954 before it had decided what it was willing to trade.”
  2. Was Nehru responsible for 1962? He was responsible for the framework of policy, for the Forward Policy’s authorisation, for the appointment structure that placed Kaul where he was, and for the failure to resource defence. He was not responsible for a Chinese decision taken in Beijing. The fair verdict distributes responsibility across the political leadership, the intelligence assessment, and a military leadership that did not press its professional objections to the point of resignation.
  3. Should the Henderson Brooks-Bhagat Report be released? Interpretation Sixty years on, the operational-sensitivity argument is weak; the institutional-learning argument is strong; the counter-argument is that partial release without context has already done damage. A reasonable position: declassify with an official historical volume alongside it.
  4. Did India give up a UN Security Council seat to China? Disputed Correspondence from the 1950s shows Nehru declining to pursue an American suggestion that India take the China seat, and advocating the PRC’s claim. But there is no evidence that a permanent seat was formally offered to India in a way that India could have accepted — the seat belonged to “China” under the Charter, and changing that required Charter amendment and Security Council consent including a Soviet veto. State it as contested and explain the legal difficulty; do not repeat the simple version.
  5. Was the February 2021 Pangong disengagement a good bargain? Live debate. The government says disengagement was sequential and no territory was lost; critics say India traded dominating heights for the reversal of a recent Chinese advance and accepted buffer zones on ground it used to patrol.
  6. Galwan casualty figures. Disputed Twenty Indian deaths are established. Chinese deaths are not. Do not repeat unverified numbers.
  7. Was Doklam a strategic success? Tactical yes, strategic mixed — see Section 28.
  8. Did Indian infrastructure “provoke” 2020? China has implied it; India rejects the framing on the ground that building roads on one’s own territory is not provocation. Interpretation Analytically, the DSDBO road plainly changed the local balance and plainly features in the timing — but noting a cause is not conceding a justification. Make that distinction explicitly; it is a good one.
  9. Should India have used air power in 1962, and should it now declare it would? See Section 19. The current position is deliberately ambiguous, and ambiguity is itself a deterrent choice.
  10. Is economic engagement with China compatible with the current security posture? The strongest argument for continued trade is that India’s own manufacturing growth currently depends on Chinese intermediates; the strongest argument against is that the deficit funds the adversary’s capability. Most serious analysts land on selective de-risking rather than decoupling. This is a first-rate GD topic precisely because both sides are strong.

Lesser-known facts

Use two or three of these, not fifteen. A single well-placed detail that the board did not expect is worth more than a recitation.

  • 21 October is Police Commemoration Day across India because of the Kongka La ambush of 1959, in which nine CRPF men were killed in Ladakh. Most people who observe the day do not know it commemorates a China-border action.
  • The Indian rupee was legal tender in Tibet and Indian trade agencies ran the postal and telegraph services from the Indian border to Gyantse until 1954.
  • India ran a military escort at Gyantse, deep inside Tibet, inherited from the 1904 Lhasa Convention — a fact that surprises almost everyone.
  • Major Bob Khathing, the officer who established Indian administration at Tawang in 1951, was a Tangkhul Naga from Manipur, a Second World War veteran of the Victoria Cross-winning V Force operations, and later an Indian ambassador. A museum at Tawang is named after him.
  • The Special Frontier Force was raised on 14 November 1962, during the war, recruited largely from Tibetan exiles; it later fought in the 1971 war in the Chittagong Hill Tracts under Operation Eagle.
  • India and China have a joint military exercise, Hand-in-Hand, conducted from 2007; it has been suspended since 2019.
  • Nathu La reopened for border trade in 2006 after forty-four years, following China’s de facto acceptance of Sikkim.
  • The Kailash Mansarovar Yatra has been organised by India’s Ministry of External Affairs through the Lipulekh and Nathu La routes; its suspension and resumption are a reliable barometer of the state of the relationship.
  • The Sixth Dalai Lama was born at Tawang — which is why the town’s status is a religious question in Tibet, not only a territorial one.
  • The 1967 Nathu La clash involved Indian artillery firing into the Chumbi valley for three days — the most sustained exchange of fire between the two armies after 1962, and almost unknown to the public on either side.
  • There has been no exchange of fire causing fatalities on the LAC since 1975 — Galwan in 2020 was fought entirely without firearms.
  • The 2001 middle-sector map exchange actually succeeded, which proves LAC clarification is technically feasible. The process stopped for political reasons, not technical ones.
  • China has settled land boundaries with twelve of its fourteen land neighbours. The two outstanding are India and Bhutan.
  • Daulat Beg Oldi is named after a Central Asian ruler said to have died there on the caravan route; at roughly 16,600 ft it is among the highest airstrips in the world, and it lay unused from 1965 until it was reactivated by an An-32 landing on 31 May 2008.
  • The Sela Tunnel of 2024 opens the exact axis that collapsed in November 1962 — the single tidiest example of infrastructure answering a historical failure.

Conflict scenarios

These are analytical constructs for thinking, not predictions. Present them as such — a candidate who forecasts a war sounds unserious; one who reasons through contingencies sounds like an officer.

Six scenarios, from most to least likely
ScenarioWhat it looks likeKey considerations
1 · Continued armed coexistence
Most likely		 Heavy deployments on both sides, periodic localised face-offs, continued talks, no settlement, no war. Essentially the present. Sustainable but expensive. Ties down formations, absorbs budget, and normalises a high-risk posture in which a local incident could escalate.
2 · Localised clash A Galwan-type incident at a friction point, contained within days by existing mechanisms. The most probable form of violence. The question is whether rules of engagement changed after 2020 make firearms use more likely, and whether both political leaderships can still de-escalate quickly under domestic pressure.
3 · Limited sector conflict A short, sharp action in one sector to seize a specific objective — a ridge, a valley, a patrolling area — followed by a ceasefire and consolidation. The 1962 template. Terrain and season constrain the options. Winter closes many approaches; the campaigning window is narrow. Indian air power and the defender’s advantage raise the cost significantly relative to 1962.
4 · Collusive two-front contingency Simultaneous or sequential pressure from China and Pakistan. The principal planning driver. Considerations: whether China has an interest in a conflict that would consolidate Indian alignment with the United States; the difference between Pakistani action with Chinese support and joint operations, which are very different things.
5 · Maritime or horizontal escalation Pressure applied in the Indian Ocean rather than on the land frontier, by either side. Favourable geography for India, but escalation across domains is hard to control and would internationalise a bilateral dispute — which may or may not serve Indian interests depending on the circumstances.
6 · Negotiated settlement
Least likely in the near term		 A package under the 2005 principles: broad acceptance of the status quo in each sector with adjustments, LAC clarification, and eventual delimitation. Requires political capital in both capitals that neither currently has reason to spend. Note, though, that every element of the framework already exists on paper — which is why analysts describe this as a problem of political will rather than of diplomatic design.
If asked directly: “will there be a war with China?”

“I would not predict one, sir. Both sides have strong reasons to avoid it — China’s principal strategic focus is the Western Pacific and Taiwan, and a war with India would consolidate exactly the coalition it wants to prevent; India’s priority is economic growth, which a war would set back by a decade. What I would say is that the risk of a localised incident is real and has probably risen, because both armies are now deployed in strength where they used to be thin. The job, as I understand it, is to make sure that if something happens locally, we hold, and that it stops there.” That is an honest, measured answer with the right professional emphasis at the end.

Presented neutrally. These are not mutually exclusive, and every actual Indian government has pursued several simultaneously. The purpose of the table is to let you discuss trade-offs rather than advocate a line.

The honest framing for a group discussion

India’s actual policy is a combination: deter by denial on the frontier, balance internally through economic and industrial capacity, balance externally without alliance, engage diplomatically to manage risk, de-risk selectively in the economy, and invest in the maritime domain where the geography favours it. The interesting argument is not which option, but the allocation between them — every rupee spent on mountain infrastructure is not spent on a submarine, and every year spent stabilising is a year in which the growth asymmetry either narrows or widens. If you can say that in a GD, you will have raised the level of the discussion.

Part IX

Knowledge toolkit

Strategic lessons

Twelve lessons this subject teaches, phrased so that each transfers to the responsibilities of a junior leader. These are what the topic is actually for, from an assessment point of view.

  1. Unobserved ground is unheld ground. A highway was built across claimed territory and nobody noticed for six years. Presence, patrolling and surveillance are not bureaucracy; they are how sovereignty is exercised in practice. At your level: the ground you do not visit is the ground you do not control.
  2. Never order what you cannot resource. The Forward Policy failed not because the aim was wrong but because the means did not exist. At your level: do not give a task without the men, time, equipment and information to do it.
  3. An assumption unexamined is an assumption that will fail. “China will not react strongly” was the load-bearing belief of 1962, and nobody was institutionally required to test it. At your level: actively seek the assessment that contradicts your plan.
  4. Logistics decide mountain warfare. Roads, acclimatisation, winter stocking, fuel and habitat are not support functions here; they are the operation. At your level: the administrative plan is the operational plan.
  5. Terrain is valued by what it enables. “Not a blade of grass” cost India dearly as a way of thinking. Ground is worth what it lets you see, move through or deny. At your level: appreciate ground before you appreciate the enemy.
  6. Take the high ground before you need it. Sumdorong Chu 1986 and Kailash 2020 are the same idea. Leverage has to be created before negotiation, not during it.
  7. Buffers matter more than borders. The loss of Tibet in 1951 turned a quiet frontier into a live one. Anticipate the second-order consequences of a neighbour’s consolidation.
  8. Agreements manage risk; they do not create safety. The 1993-2013 architecture kept the peace for thirty years and did not prevent 2020. Value CBMs for what they do — reduce accidents — and do not mistake them for a settlement.
  9. Deterrence works against attacks, not against processes. Grey-zone advance is answered by persistence, documentation and matching infrastructure, not by threats.
  10. Economic strength is the foundation of strategic strength. The India-China gap of 1962 was modest; today it is wide, and it widened during a period of peace. Nothing on the frontier can compensate indefinitely for a gap in national capacity.
  11. The soldier is rarely the point of failure. Rezang La, Nuranang, Nathu La, Galwan — the fighting quality has been consistent across sixty years. What varies is what the system puts behind the soldier.
  12. Know your adversary’s case better than he states it. You cannot anticipate an opponent whose reasoning you refuse to reconstruct. This is a professional obligation, not a concession.

Interview technique on this topic

Short, practical, and specific to this subject — which carries particular traps because it is emotive and because the current affairs move fast.

Six things to do

  • Open with structure, not dates. Use the ladder from Section 2 — what, why, background, our interest, and what we should do. For the subject as a whole, the three-part opening is: boundary problem, Tibet problem, power problem. Then go to detail.
  • Attribute every contested number. “India’s official figure is…”, “China acknowledged…”, “independent estimates range…”. This single habit will do more for you than any amount of extra reading.
  • Say when something is recent and you want to re-check. It reads as integrity, not ignorance. “Sir, that changed in the last few months and I would want to confirm the current position” is an excellent answer.
  • Use one unexpected detail, well placed. Police Commemoration Day; the Burma treaty of 1960; the 2001 middle-sector map exchange; Major Bob Khathing at Tawang. One, not five.
  • Connect history to present capability. 1962’s missing guns and the M777 under a Chinook; 1962’s Se La collapse and the Sela Tunnel of 2024; 1957’s missed highway and today’s satellites.
  • Finish with the professional point. Whatever the question, the assessors are interested in what it implies for an officer. End there.

Six things to avoid

  • Don’t quote unverified Chinese casualty figures from 1962, 1967 or Galwan as fact.
  • Don’t say India should “use the Tibet card.” It is contrary to stated policy and it sounds unserious.
  • Don’t predict a war, and don’t say one is impossible.
  • Don’t abuse Nehru — or defend him uncritically. A calm apportionment of responsibility reads far better than either.
  • Don’t claim parity you cannot support. China’s defence budget and industrial base are substantially larger. Acknowledge it and explain why the local balance on this frontier is a different calculation.
  • Don’t repeat the “UN seat offered to India” story as settled fact, or the Jaswant Singh Rawat seventy-two-hour legend as history. Both are traps for the under-prepared.
The three sentences to have ready in your own words
  • What is the core problem? “Two large states share a frontier that no treaty has ever defined, across a buffer that disappeared in 1951, at a moment when both are rising.”
  • What changed in 2020? “The assumption that the border could be postponed while everything else grew. India’s stated position now is that the state of the border determines the state of the relationship.”
  • What should India do? “Deter locally, build nationally, talk continuously, and never again let ground go unwatched.”

The framework applied: ten briefs

Every major issue in this handbook, run through the nine rungs of Section 2. Read one brief aloud each day for ten days and the structure becomes automatic — which is the point, because in the interview you will not have time to think about structure and content at once.

1 · The boundary dispute as a whole

What
A frontier of 3,488 km on India’s figure, in four sectors, with no ratified boundary treaty anywhere along it and no mutually agreed Line of Actual Control. Two principal claims: Aksai Chin (~38,000 km²) held by China and claimed by India; Arunachal Pradesh (~83,743 km²) claimed by China as “Zangnan”.
Why
Two empires never delimited a frontier they never needed to administer. Two republics then consolidated towards the same ground within a few years of each other, after the buffer between them disappeared.
Background
Johnson (1865) versus Macartney–MacDonald (1899) in the west; the McMahon Line agreed with Tibet at Simla in 1914 but never ratified by China; Tibet’s incorporation into the PRC in 1950–51.
India’s interest
Vital: a defensible frontier and the principle that territory is not altered by force. Important: a clarified LAC, which would remove most incidents. Desirable: a final settlement, which is not currently on offer.
Security
Two active fronts with a collusive partnership between them; permanent deployment at altitudes that degrade men and equipment; and a standing risk that a local patrol encounter escalates because nobody agrees where the line is.
Economic
The cost of holding, the cost of building, and the opportunity cost of both. Set against a bilateral trade relationship running a deficit in the range of US$ 85–100 billion and above.
For
For a settlement on a status-quo basis: it removes the dispute, frees a great deal of money and manpower, ends the risk of accidental war, and the framework already exists in the 2005 Political Parameters.
Against
It would require accepting less than the full claim in at least one sector; the 1962 Parliament resolution constrains any government that tries; and there is no evidence of Chinese readiness, which makes the concession one-sided.
India should
Deter by denial on the ground, pursue LAC clarification as the achievable intermediate goal, keep the SR mechanism running, and build the national capacity that is the only durable answer to a power gap. The cost: indefinite expenditure with no political victory to show for it.

2 · Doklam, 2017

What
A 73-day standoff from 16 June to 28 August 2017 on the Doklam plateau at the India–Bhutan–China trijunction, after Indian troops physically stopped Chinese road construction on territory claimed by Bhutan. It ended with mutual disengagement and no shots fired.
Why
Trigger: the southward extension of a road towards the Jampheri ridge. Driver: a ridge overlooking the approaches to the Siliguri corridor, and a test of whether India would act for a third country.
Background
The Chumbi valley is a Chinese wedge between Sikkim and Bhutan. The Siliguri corridor is roughly 20–22 km wide at its narrowest. India and Bhutan differ with China on where the trijunction lies — Batang La (India and Bhutan) or Gipmochi (China).
India’s interest
Preventing observation of and access towards the corridor that connects the North-East to the rest of India; and preserving the credibility of its security relationship with Bhutan, which is worth more than the plateau itself.
Security
A road to Jampheri would have shortened warning time on the most sensitive stretch of Indian territory. The intervention also established that India would contest construction physically rather than by protest note.
Economic
Small directly; significant indirectly. It accelerated Indian road, rail and tunnel investment in the Siliguri and Sikkim sectors and hardened the case for diversifying connectivity to the North-East.
For
It worked: the road stopped, Bhutan’s confidence was maintained, escalation was avoided, and India demonstrated that a firm response need not produce a war.
Against
Chinese infrastructure and deployment in the wider Doklam area subsequently expanded considerably; the tactical success did not secure a strategic one; and some analysts argue the episode hardened Chinese planning towards the 2020 move.
India should
Continue treating trijunction alteration as a red line, complete the Siliguri-area connectivity redundancies, deepen the Bhutan relationship without crowding it, and monitor the plateau continuously. The cost: a standing commitment to a small country’s territorial position, which limits India’s own flexibility.

3 · Buffer zones and the unfinished de-escalation

What
Areas at several friction points in eastern Ladakh from which both sides withdrew and in which neither patrols, created between 2021 and 2022 as part of sequential disengagement. Patrolling arrangements for Depsang and Demchok were announced on 21 October 2024. Re-verify
Why
Disengagement required separating troops who were within metres of each other. A no-patrol zone was the only mechanism both sides would accept quickly enough to reduce the risk of another Galwan.
Background
Disengagement, de-escalation and de-induction are three distinct stages. The first is substantially done; the second is not; the third has not begun.
India’s interest
Restoring patrolling access to the status quo ante of April 2020 — and, more fundamentally, preventing a temporary risk-reduction device from quietly becoming a permanent line.
Security
Fewer contacts means fewer incidents, which is a real gain. But ground not patrolled is ground not observed, and the 1957 highway is the standing reminder of what that leads to.
Economic
De-escalation, when it comes, would release very large recurring costs in winter stocking, habitat and air maintenance for formations held forward since 2020.
For
They stopped the fighting, made disengagement possible without either side appearing to retreat under pressure, and are explicitly described by India as temporary.
Against
They are asymmetric in effect where India previously patrolled further; “temporary” arrangements on this frontier have a history of becoming permanent; and Parliament has not been given a full account of the areas involved.
India should
Press for time-bound restoration of patrolling rights, tie further normalisation steps to de-escalation, maintain surveillance of the zones even where patrolling is suspended, and keep the language precise in public. The cost: insisting on patrolling rights raises the risk of the very incidents the zones were created to prevent.

4 · CPEC and the Belt and Road Initiative

What
The China–Pakistan Economic Corridor, the flagship BRI project, linking Kashgar in Xinjiang to Gwadar on the Arabian Sea — passing through Gilgit-Baltistan, territory India claims. India has declined to join the BRI since 2017.
Why
For China, an alternative to the Malacca route and a deepened partnership with Pakistan. For India, a foreign infrastructure corridor through territory it regards as its own, which converts a bilateral dispute into a third-party interest.
Background
The 1963 Sino-Pakistan boundary agreement, under which Pakistan transferred the Shaksgam tract (~5,180 km²) to China, established the precedent India objects to.
India’s interest
Preserving the legal position on Jammu and Kashmir; preventing Chinese presence and personnel on a flank of Ladakh; and avoiding endorsement of a framework built on a transfer India never recognised.
Security
Chinese economic presence in Gilgit-Baltistan creates a protection interest on a sensitive flank; Gwadar’s potential dual use affects the Arabian Sea; and the corridor thickens the China–Pakistan relationship India must plan against.
Economic
India forgoes connectivity benefits and regional infrastructure finance. Its counter-offers — Chabahar, the International North–South Transport Corridor, the India–Middle East–Europe Corridor — are smaller, slower and less capitalised. Re-verify
For
For the boycott: participation would amount to acquiescence in the sovereignty position; sovereignty concerns are principled, consistently stated, and have been echoed by others concerned about debt sustainability and transparency.
Against
The boycott has not slowed CPEC, has left India outside a connectivity architecture its neighbours have joined, and arguably spends principle where it buys no leverage.
India should
Maintain the sovereignty objection, which is the one position it cannot trade; compete rather than merely object, by financing and finishing regional projects it has already announced; and work on standards — transparency, debt sustainability, environmental terms — where its argument has wide support. The cost: competing on connectivity requires capital and delivery speed India has historically struggled to supply.

5 · The Medog dam on the Yarlung Tsangpo

What
A very large hydropower project approved by China in December 2024 at the Great Bend of the Yarlung Tsangpo near Medog, close to where the river enters Arunachal Pradesh as the Siang and becomes the Brahmaputra. Capacity figures reported around 60 GW would make it the largest such project in the world. Re-verify
Why
The Great Bend drops some two kilometres in a short distance — the greatest concentrated hydropower potential on earth. China’s stated purpose is clean energy and the development of a remote region.
Background
There is no water-sharing treaty between India and China. Arrangements are limited to hydrological data-sharing MoUs on the Brahmaputra and Sutlej, and data was suspended in 2017 during the Doklam standoff — which is the fact that defines Indian anxiety.
India’s interest
Assured flow, sediment and flood-warning data for the Brahmaputra basin; protection of downstream agriculture, fisheries and the ecology of Assam; and avoidance of a precedent in which upstream infrastructure becomes a bargaining instrument.
Security
The realistic concern is not a released flood — the engineering and the distances make that a poor weapon — but data denial, flow timing and leverage. A secondary concern is seismicity: the site lies in a highly active zone.
Economic
Sediment retention would affect the fertility that Assam’s agriculture depends on. India’s proposed Siang Upper multipurpose project in Arunachal, at around 11,000 MW, is partly a storage counter — and faces significant local opposition.
For
For a calm Indian response: most Brahmaputra flow is generated by rainfall and tributaries below the Chinese section, so the hydrological effect is smaller than headlines suggest; alarmism weakens India’s credibility on the genuine issues.
Against
For treating it as urgent: dry-season flow, sediment and warning data matter most precisely when they are scarcest, and a partner who has already suspended data once has demonstrated willingness to use it.
India should
Press for an institutionalised, year-round data regime rather than renewable MoUs; build its own storage and flood-warning capacity in the Siang basin with genuine local consent; internationalise the norm of upstream transparency rather than the dispute; and reject the language of “water wars”, which raises alarm without producing a policy. The cost: domestic storage projects impose real displacement and ecological costs on Indian citizens.

6 · Economic dependence on China

What
India’s largest goods trade deficit, in the range of US$ 85–100 billion and above in recent years, concentrated in electronics, machinery, chemicals, active pharmaceutical ingredients, solar equipment and critical minerals — with Indian exports dominated by raw and intermediate materials. Re-verify
Why
Three decades of divergent manufacturing trajectories. China built scale, supply-chain depth and process capability in precisely the sectors India’s own growth now consumes.
Background
Trade was negligible until the 1990s and grew fastest under the post-1988 formula of separating economics from the boundary. That separation ended politically in 2020 — but not commercially.
India’s interest
Avoiding coercive leverage over medicines, electronics, telecom and energy transition inputs — while not strangling the manufacturing growth that currently depends on those same inputs.
Security
Dependence in pharmaceuticals and electronics is a wartime vulnerability; telecom and power-sector equipment raise supply-chain integrity questions; and export earnings contribute to the capability India deploys against.
Economic
Measures since 2020 — Press Note 3 FDI screening, app bans, procurement restrictions, PLI schemes — have changed the composition of the relationship more than its size. Imports have risen, not fallen. Say this; it is the honest point and it is the one most candidates avoid.
For
For continued engagement: Chinese intermediates are what make Indian assembly competitive today; abrupt restriction raises costs, feeds inflation and slows the very industrial growth that would eventually reduce dependence.
Against
For aggressive restriction: a dependence that can be switched off is a standing instrument of coercion, as the April 2025 rare-earth export controls illustrated; and voluntary vulnerability to an adversary is a strategic choice, not an economic accident.
India should
De-risk rather than decouple: prioritise the handful of genuinely critical dependencies — APIs, rare earths and magnets, advanced electronics components — build alternative sources with partners, and accept that broad-spectrum restriction is neither achievable nor desirable. The cost: de-risking is inflationary in the short run and demands sustained industrial policy across several governments.

7 · The Quad and India’s partnerships

What
A consultative grouping of India, the United States, Japan and Australia, revived in 2017 and elevated to leaders’ level in 2021. It has no treaty, no secretariat and no mutual defence obligation, and works on maritime domain awareness, supply chains, technology, health and infrastructure.
Why
Shared concern about coercion in the Indo-Pacific, and a shared preference for shaping the regional order without forming an alliance that would provoke the outcome it seeks to prevent.
Background
Originating in the 2004 tsunami relief coordination; lapsed after 2008; revived after 2017. The Malabar naval exercise, now involving all four, is the visible military strand. India has signed LEMOA, COMCASA and BECA with the United States — logistics, secure communications and geospatial intelligence.
India’s interest
Access to technology, intelligence, capital and diplomatic weight; a maritime domain where geography favours it; and, crucially, signalling that India is not isolated — without accepting obligations that would constrain it elsewhere.
Security
Real gains in maritime domain awareness and interoperability. But no Quad partner is obliged to assist India on a land frontier in the Himalaya, which is where India is actually pressed. Be precise about this; it is the heart of the matter.
Economic
Supply-chain resilience, semiconductor and critical-minerals cooperation, and infrastructure finance alternatives — mostly promised, partially delivered.
For
It delivers capability and standing at low cost and without alliance commitments, and it is the only grouping in which India sits as a full equal with three advanced economies concerned about the same problem.
Against
It delivers more signalling than security; it is vulnerable to political change in three capitals; it may harden Chinese behaviour towards India specifically; and it does nothing on the frontier that matters most.
India should
Keep the Quad functional and non-military in framing while deepening the bilateral defence relationships that actually deliver capability; continue to decline alliance membership; and remember that partnerships supplement national capacity and never substitute for it. The cost: strategic autonomy means no one is obliged to come, and India must be able to hold alone.

8 · Border villages: xiaokang against Vibrant Villages

What
China has built hundreds of xiaokang or “well-off” model villages along its Himalayan frontier, including in disputed areas. India approved the Vibrant Villages Programme in February 2023, with an outlay of about ₹4,800 crore to develop and repopulate border villages.
Why
Population is the cheapest and most durable form of presence. A settled civilian community creates administration, roads, communications and a permanent claim to occupation that a patrol cannot.
Background
China’s Land Border Law, in force from 1 January 2022, formalises the protection and settlement of border areas. Indian border districts, meanwhile, had suffered decades of out-migration towards the plains.
India’s interest
Reversing depopulation; retaining the traditional grazing and pastoral communities whose seasonal movement is itself evidence of use; and ensuring that the first Indians a Chinese patrol encounters are citizens, not an empty valley.
Security
Border residents are the original surveillance network — they notice a new track, a new structure, an unfamiliar party. Grazing rights lost quietly in the 2020 buffer arrangements are a live example of what emptiness costs.
Economic
Roads, power, connectivity, tourism and livelihood support in districts that have never had them — some of the best-justified development spending in India, because the security and welfare cases point the same way.
For
Cheap relative to military presence, welfare-positive, and strategically sound. It answers a grey-zone technique with a grey-zone response rather than with a military escalation.
Against
Repopulation cannot be ordered — people leave for schools, hospitals and jobs, and will only return if those exist. Implementation has been uneven, and there is a risk of building infrastructure for villages that remain empty.
India should
Fund livelihoods and services rather than facades; secure grazing and pastoral access explicitly in any border arrangement; integrate border residents formally into surveillance and civil-defence structures; and measure the programme by residents retained, not by money released. The cost: results are slow and unphotogenic, and will not show within a single political cycle.

9 · The succession to the 14th Dalai Lama

What
A contested question of who selects the next Dalai Lama. China asserts that reincarnation requires state approval under its Order No. 5 of 2007 and invokes the Qing-era Golden Urn. The Dalai Lama has stated that the Gaden Phodrang Trust alone has authority in the matter. Re-verify
Why
Legitimacy. Control of the institution would give Beijing religious authority over Tibetan Buddhists — including in the Indian Himalaya — and would close the last independent symbol of the Tibetan question.
Background
The Dalai Lama has lived in India since 31 March 1959. He devolved political authority to an elected Tibetan leadership in 2011. The precedent of the Panchen Lama, whose Chinese-recognised and Tibetan-recognised candidates diverged in 1995, is the template both sides expect.
India’s interest
Protecting the religious freedom of Indian citizens who follow this tradition — in Ladakh, Himachal, Sikkim, Arunachal — while avoiding a position that converts a spiritual question into a territorial one. India recognises the TAR as part of China and does not permit anti-China political activity by Tibetan refugees.
Security
A Beijing-appointed Dalai Lama would give China religious influence in Indian border districts whose populations share the tradition — the quietest and most durable form of access imaginable.
Economic
Marginal directly. Significant for the Himalayan monastic economy, pilgrimage and the tourism that several border districts depend on.
For
For India taking a clear public position: religious freedom is a principle India holds domestically, the issue has wide international support, and silence cedes the ground.
Against
For continued restraint: a formal Indian position would be read as reopening Tibet, would hand Beijing the framing it wants, and would place Indian citizens who follow the tradition at the centre of an inter-state quarrel.
India should
Hold its stated line precisely — India does not take positions on reincarnation but upholds the religious freedom of its own citizens; protect the institutions and communities in its Himalayan districts; and coordinate quietly with others who have legislated on the question. The cost: restraint will be criticised at home as weakness, and the criticism will be loud.

10 · India’s border infrastructure push

What
A sustained programme of roads, tunnels, bridges, airfields and rail towards the northern frontier — the DSDBO road (255 km), the Atal Tunnel (9.02 km, 3 October 2020), the Sela Tunnel (9 March 2024), the Nimmu–Padam–Darcha axis, Zoji La, and the reactivation of advanced landing grounds including Daulat Beg Oldi at about 16,600 ft.
Why
Because logistics decides mountain warfare, and because for four decades after 1962 India deliberately did not build roads to the frontier, on the theory that they would assist an invader. That theory was abandoned after the 1999 Kargil review and finally buried in 2020.
Background
The Border Roads Organisation was raised on 7 May 1960. In November 1962 the Se La–Bomdila axis collapsed for want of a road; the Sela Tunnel of 2024 opens that exact axis in all weather. It is the tidiest example in this handbook of infrastructure answering a historical failure.
India’s interest
Rapid induction and switching of forces between sectors; year-round sustainment; reduced dependence on air maintenance; and the civilian development that keeps border populations in place.
Security
It converts India’s theoretical advantage — shorter distances from its own population and industrial centres — into an actual one, and reduces the number of days an adversary could count on before reinforcement arrives.
Economic
Very large capital expenditure with genuine dual use: the same tunnel serves a brigade and a district. Set against this, high maintenance costs in a region of landslides, snow and seismicity.
For
Defensive, unambiguous, useful in peace and war, and it is the one line of effort on which every school of Indian strategic thought agrees.
Against
Roads cut both ways in a reverse; construction near the LAC has itself been a trigger for confrontation, as the DSDBO road illustrates; and the ecological cost in a fragile, earthquake-prone belt is real and is borne locally.
India should
Complete the lateral connectivity that allows switching between sectors, prioritise tunnels over passes for all-weather reliability, build the maintenance and restoration capacity to match, and pair construction with genuine local consent. The cost: this competes directly with naval and air force modernisation for the same budget — which is the central resource-allocation argument in Indian defence, and the one worth being able to discuss.

Glossary

Line of Actual Control (LAC)
The line separating areas of effective military control, not a boundary. Its alignment is itself disputed in many places. Used in bilateral treaty language from 1993.
Claim line
Where a state asserts the boundary ought to be, as distinct from where its troops actually are.
Patrolling Point (PP)
An Indian designation for a point up to which patrols are sent to assert presence. Not a bilaterally agreed marker.
Disengagement / De-escalation / De-induction
Separating troops at friction points / reducing forces built up in depth areas / returning formations to peacetime locations. Three distinct stages, used precisely by the Indian government.
Buffer zone
An area from which both sides withdraw and in which neither patrols, created as part of a disengagement.
Johnson Line / Ardagh-Johnson Line
The 1865 and 1897 British alignments placing Aksai Chin inside Kashmir along the Kunlun range.
Macartney-MacDonald Line
The 1899 British compromise offered to China, running along the Karakoram and leaving most of Aksai Chin outside India.
McMahon Line
The India-Tibet alignment agreed at Simla in March 1914, running roughly 890 km along the eastern Himalayan watershed from Bhutan to the Burma trijunction.
Simla Convention, 1914
The tripartite convention on Tibet’s status that China initialled but did not ratify; Britain and Tibet then bound themselves bilaterally.
Suzerainty
Authority over another state’s external relations without full sovereignty over its internal affairs — the formula used for China’s position in Tibet in the 1906-07 conventions.
Chö-yön
The “priest-patron” relationship between a Tibetan religious hierarch and a Mongol or Manchu ruler, central to the dispute over Tibet’s historical status.
Seventeen Point Agreement
The agreement of 23 May 1951 incorporating Tibet into the PRC; repudiated by the Dalai Lama in 1959 as signed under duress.
Panchsheel
The five principles of peaceful coexistence set out in the preamble to the 29 April 1954 agreement.
Forward Policy
India’s 1961-62 policy of establishing posts as far forward as possible towards its claim line, including behind Chinese positions.
Aksai Chin
The roughly 38,000 km² plateau in the western sector held by China and claimed by India; carries the G219 highway.
Shaksgam / Trans-Karakoram Tract
About 5,180 km² transferred by Pakistan to China under the 1963 boundary agreement; India rejects the transfer.
Zangnan / South Tibet
China’s terminology for Arunachal Pradesh, which it claims in its entirety (~83,743 km²).
The Fingers
Eight spurs running into the north bank of Pangong Tso, numbered 1 to 8 from west to east; the reference frame for the 2020 standoff there.
Bottleneck / Y-Junction
The point in the Depsang Plains where Chinese positions blocked Indian patrols from reaching Patrolling Points 10 to 13.
Chumbi valley
The Chinese salient between Sikkim and Bhutan, pointing towards the Siliguri corridor.
Siliguri corridor / Chicken’s Neck
The strip roughly 20-22 km wide at its narrowest connecting India’s North-East to the rest of the country.
Doklam / Donglang
The plateau at the India-Bhutan-China trijunction, claimed by Bhutan and China, site of the 2017 standoff.
Border Personnel Meeting (BPM)
Designated venues for local commander-level meetings: Chushul, Daulat Beg Oldi, Nathu La, Bum La, Kibithu.
WMCC
Working Mechanism for Consultation and Coordination on India-China Border Affairs, established 2012 at official level.
Special Representatives (SR)
The political-level boundary dialogue established in 2003; India is represented by the National Security Adviser.
BDCA
Border Defence Cooperation Agreement, 23 October 2013 — no tailing of patrols, maximum self-restraint, additional meeting points and hotlines.
Salami slicing
Incremental territorial advance in steps each too small to justify a military response, cumulatively significant.
Three warfares
Public opinion, psychological and legal warfare — formally part of PLA political work since 2003.
Xiaokang villages
“Well-off” model border villages built by China along its Himalayan frontier; widely assessed as dual-use presence-creating settlements.
Vibrant Villages Programme
India’s 2023 programme to develop and repopulate border villages along the northern frontier.
CPEC
China-Pakistan Economic Corridor, linking Kashgar to Gwadar through Gilgit-Baltistan; the basis of India’s objection to the Belt and Road Initiative.
Malacca dilemma
China’s vulnerability arising from the passage of most of its energy imports through the Strait of Malacca; a Chinese formulation.
HAPO / HACE
High Altitude Pulmonary Oedema and High Altitude Cerebral Oedema — the two life-threatening altitude illnesses that govern acclimatisation policy.
Density altitude
The effective altitude for aerodynamic purposes; the reason helicopters and aircraft lose payload in thin, warm air, and a central constraint on Himalayan operations.
Grey zone
Coercive activity deliberately kept below the threshold that would justify an armed response.
Strategic autonomy / multi-alignment
India’s policy of participating in multiple, sometimes competing, groupings without accepting alliance obligations.

The arc in eight sentences

  1. Two civilisations traded and exchanged ideas for two thousand years without a shared administered border, because Tibet and Central Asia lay between them.
  2. Britain drew several inconsistent lines in the west between 1865 and 1899 and agreed the McMahon Line with Tibet in 1914; neither was ever settled with China by ratified treaty.
  3. Tibet’s incorporation into the PRC in 1950-51 removed the buffer and made India and China direct neighbours for the first time.
  4. The 1954 Panchsheel agreement traded India’s inherited rights in Tibet for a set of principles and said nothing about the boundary.
  5. The discovery of the Aksai Chin highway in 1957, the Tibetan uprising and the Dalai Lama’s arrival in 1959, and the Forward Policy of 1961 produced the war of October-November 1962, which India lost through a failure of system rather than of soldiers.
  6. Nathu La and Cho La in 1967 restored the local balance; Sumdorong Chu in 1986-87 showed the value of taking the heights first; the 1988 visit created a formula of building everything else while postponing the border, which held for thirty-two years.
  7. The agreements of 1993, 1996, 2005 and 2013 kept the peace without settling anything, and the LAC was never clarified after the middle-sector exchange of 2001.
  8. The 2020 build-up in eastern Ladakh and the Galwan clash ended the postponement formula; disengagement was completed in stages by October 2024, but de-escalation has not been, and India’s position is now that the state of the border determines the state of the relationship.

Five facts that win arguments

  • China accepted essentially the same McMahon alignment in its 1960 boundary treaty with Burma — which undercuts the “imperialist line” objection.
  • China has settled land boundaries with twelve of its fourteen land neighbours; the two outstanding are India and Bhutan.
  • The 2001 middle-sector map exchange succeeded — LAC clarification is technically feasible and stopped for political reasons.
  • 21 October is Police Commemoration Day because of Kongka La, 1959.
  • Galwan was fought without firearms because of the 1996 agreement — the CBM regime was being observed in that one respect even as it failed in every other.

Four answers to have ready, word for word in your own phrasing

  • What is the core problem? A boundary problem inherited from empire, a Tibet problem that removed the buffer, and a power problem between two rising states. The border is where the third is physically conducted.
  • Why did India lose in 1962? Political direction outran military means; the intelligence assessment was never tested; and there were no roads. The soldier was not the failure.
  • What changed after 2020? The assumption that the border could be postponed. Disengagement is done; de-escalation is not; the relationship is stabilised, not settled.
  • What should India do? Deter locally, build nationally, talk continuously, and never let ground go unwatched.

The ladder, for any question you have not seen before

What? → Why? → Background? → India’s interest? → Security implication? → Economic implication? → Argument for? → Argument against? → What should India do?

Thirty seconds: rungs 1, 4, 9. Two minutes: 1, 2, 3, 4, 9. A long probe: all nine, in order. A group discussion: 8, then 7, then 9. Rung 9 must name its own cost, and rung 8 is never skipped.

Last word before you walk in

You will not be marked on how much of this you remember. You will be marked on whether you can hold a national position and still describe the other side accurately; whether you say “I am not certain, sir, I would want to check”; whether you distinguish what you know from what you have read somewhere; and whether you can connect a sixty-year-old ridge in Ladakh to a responsibility you might one day carry. Know ten things properly rather than a hundred vaguely. Then talk like yourself.
