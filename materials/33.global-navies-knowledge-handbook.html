Snapshot and how to read

A navy is the part of a state's power that works across the 70% of the planet covered by water. The United States has the most capable and globally deployed navy. China has overtaken it in number of ships and is closing capability gaps. Russia's fleet is smaller but nuclear-armed and has been hurt in the Black Sea. Mid-sized navies such as those of the UK, France, Japan and India matter through alliances, technology and geography.

Part I · Foundations

Theory, ships, technology and history

What navies do: strategy and theory

Navies carry out a handful of tasks that are often grouped by Western doctrine as sea control (using an area of sea and denying it to the enemy), sea denial (preventing the enemy from using it, without necessarily using it oneself), power projection (striking land from the sea), deterrence (especially nuclear, through missile submarines) and maritime security and diplomacy (piracy, relief, port calls and exercises).

  • Alfred Thayer Mahan (1890): sea power rests on a strong fleet, bases and commerce, and decisive battle wins control of the sea.
  • Julian Corbett (1911): the sea matters because of its effect on land events and trade; controlling communications and joint operations matter more than seeking one decisive battle. A "fleet in being" can influence events without fighting.
  • Sergey Gorshkov: the Soviet admiral who built a "balanced fleet" in the Cold War, with a strong submarine arm and defence of bastions for ballistic-missile submarines. Interpretation
  • Anti-access/area denial (A2/AD): using missiles, submarines, mines and sensors to keep a stronger navy out of a region.
Sea control versus sea denial

The weaker side in a contest usually tries sea denial, and the stronger side tries sea control. Ukraine's attacks on Russia's Black Sea Fleet are a recent example of denial by a state with almost no conventional navy. A candidate who can use the two terms precisely and give an example sounds well prepared.

Fleet strength is usually counted in hulls and displacement, but both mislead. A large number of small patrol ships is not equal to a few large destroyers, and the real measure is the ability to defend against missiles, hunt submarines, sustain operations and fight alongside others.

Naval technology and the changing battlefield

  • Missiles: long-range anti-ship cruise missiles (Harpoon, Exocet, BrahMos, YJ-18, Kalibr), anti-ship ballistic missiles (China's DF-21D and DF-26) and hypersonic weapons (Russia's Zircon, Chinese glide vehicles) shorten warning times. Re-verify systems and deployments
  • Defence: Aegis-type combat systems with vertical launch cells, layered missile defence, electronic warfare and decoys.
  • Undersea warfare: quieter submarines, towed sonar, seabed sensors and maritime patrol aircraft such as the P-8 (India's P-8I). Finding submarines is harder than building them.
  • Unmanned systems: uncrewed surface and underwater vessels, from Ukraine's explosive sea drones to US experimental large unmanned submarines. Mass and low cost are the point.
  • Space, cyber and information: satellites give targeting data and communication, and both are targets. Undersea cables carry most data. Interpretation
  • Power and propulsion: electromagnetic catapults (EMALS), lithium-ion batteries on newer submarines, directed-energy weapons under test.
The carrier question

Some analysts argue that cheap anti-ship missiles and drones make large carriers too vulnerable. Others argue that carriers, with their escorts and layered defence, remain the most flexible tool for power projection and are hard to find and hit at long range. The debate is unresolved. Disputed

Sea power in history

  • 1588Spanish Armada defeated by England, with weather and logistics as factors.
  • 1805Trafalgar: Nelson's victory gives Britain sea control for a century.
  • 1905-06Tsushima (Japan destroys the Russian fleet) and the launch of HMS Dreadnought, which changed battleship design.
  • 1916Jutland, the largest fleet action of the First World War.
  • 1922Washington Naval Treaty limits capital ships.
  • 1939-45Battle of the Atlantic: convoys, escorts and aircraft defeat the U-boat campaign.
  • 1941-42Pearl Harbor and Midway: carriers replace battleships as the decisive ships.
  • 1954USS Nautilus, the first nuclear-powered submarine.
  • 1962Cuban Missile Crisis: a naval "quarantine" is central.
  • 1982Falklands War: the British sub HMS Conqueror sinks the Argentine cruiser General Belgrano, and Exocet missiles sink HMS Sheffield and others.

Three lessons recur: sea control depends on logistics and bases as much as ships; new technology (carriers, submarines, missiles) repeatedly overturned the "decisive" platform; and navies that were underestimated, such as Japan's in 1905 and 1941, shaped the century that followed. Part II · The major navies

Who has what, and why it matters

The United States Navy

The US Navy is the only navy with a worldwide network of bases and a fleet of nuclear-powered carriers, with around eleven in recent years, plus nuclear attack and ballistic-missile submarines, Aegis destroyers and a large amphibious force. It operates through numbered fleets: the Seventh Fleet from Japan covers the Western Pacific, the Fifth Fleet from Bahrain covers the Gulf and Arabian Sea, and the Sixth covers Europe and Africa. Re-verify fleet size, carrier count and retirements

  • Strengths: carrier air power, nuclear submarines, experience of global operations, allies and basing (Japan, South Korea, Guam, Bahrain, Diego Garcia).
  • Strains: shipbuilding delays on carriers, Virginia-class attack submarines and the Columbia-class ballistic-missile submarines; ageing ships; limited maintenance capacity; and the cost of defending against cheap missiles and drones, as seen in the Red Sea. Re-verify
  • Law: the United States follows UNCLOS rules as customary law but has not ratified the convention, and conducts freedom-of-navigation operations to contest claims it regards as excessive. Established
Shipbuilding as the weak point

US naval analysts frequently warn that China's shipbuilding base is many times larger. Naval power in a long war depends on the ability to build and repair, not only on the ships on the day war begins. Re-verify the comparison figures

China's PLA Navy

China's People's Liberation Army Navy (PLAN) has become the largest navy by number of battle-force ships, with recent US defence estimates of roughly 370 battle-force ships and a plan for continued growth. It has three carriers: Liaoning (ex-Soviet Varyag, 2012), Shandong (the first built in China, 2019) and Fujian (a catapult-launched carrier, commissioned in 2025). It also operates large Type 055 destroyers, Type 075 amphibious assault ships and a growing submarine force that includes nuclear ballistic-missile submarines. Re-verify counts and commissioning dates

  • Doctrine: from "near seas" defence toward "far seas protection". Missiles such as the DF-21D and DF-26 are intended to hold US carriers at risk (A2/AD). Interpretation
  • Bases and access: a naval support base at Djibouti (2017), reported access at Ream in Cambodia, and commercial ports with potential dual use (Gwadar, Hambantota). Whether these amount to a "string of pearls" is debated. Disputed
  • South China Sea: artificial islands with runways and missile sites in the Spratlys and Paracels; coast guard and maritime-militia pressure on others' claims.
  • Indian Ocean: anti-piracy patrols since 2008, submarine visits, and research vessels that call at regional ports, which India watches closely. See handbook 10.
Quantity and quality

China leads in number of hulls and shipbuilding capacity. The United States leads in operational experience, nuclear submarines, allies and undersea warfare. A balanced answer notes that both statements are true and that the gap is narrowing in some areas. Interpretation

Russia's navy

Russia's navy is organised into the Northern, Pacific, Baltic and Black Sea fleets and the Caspian Flotilla. Its core strength is nuclear submarines: Borei-class ballistic-missile submarines and Yasen-class cruise-missile submarines, supported by the Northern Fleet's Kola Peninsula bases. Surface forces are older, and its only aircraft carrier, Admiral Kuznetsov, has spent years in repair. Re-verify

  • Strategic role: protecting "bastions" for ballistic-missile submarines and holding NATO's northern flank at risk.
  • Black Sea war: the cruiser Moskva sank in April 2022 after Ukrainian missile strikes, and sea-drone and missile attacks later forced much of the Black Sea Fleet away from Sevastopol to Novorossiysk. Turkey invoked the Montreux Convention to restrict warship passage through the Straits. Re-verify losses and current positions
  • Weapons: Kalibr cruise missiles fired from ships and submarines, and the Zircon hypersonic missile. Re-verify
  • India link: Russia built or supplied INS Vikramaditya, Kilo-class submarines, Talwar-class frigates and has leased nuclear submarines in the past. See handbook 1.

Other navies of note

  • Turkey: controls the Straits under the 1936 Montreux Convention, follows the "Blue Homeland" (Mavi Vatan) maritime claims, and has built the amphibious assault ship and drone carrier TCG Anadolu.
  • Germany and Italy: Germany is rebuilding after its 2022 defence spending increase, with frigate and submarine programmes affected by delays. Italy operates the carriers Cavour and Trieste. Re-verify
  • Israel: small but capable, with German-built Dolphin-class submarines and missile corvettes. Re-verify
  • Iran: two forces, the regular navy and the IRGC Navy. The IRGC uses fast boats, mines and missiles to threaten the Strait of Hormuz. Interpretation
  • Pakistan: acquiring Chinese-built Type 054A/P frigates and Hangor-class submarines (a programme with Chinese support), and Turkish-designed corvettes. Re-verify delivery status
  • Others: Indonesia, Vietnam and Brazil (with its nuclear-submarine ambition) are growing regional navies.
Why Pakistan matters in a global comparison

For India, the Pakistan Navy is a small but supported navy: Chinese ships and submarines add reach and complicate planning in the Arabian Sea. See handbooks 10 and 23.

Part III · Strategy and flashpoints

Alliances, contested seas and what recent wars taught

Alliances and groupings

  • NATO (1949): collective defence under Article 5, with standing maritime groups. Finland (2023) and Sweden (2024) joined, which makes the Baltic Sea largely a NATO sea.
  • AUKUS (2021): Australia, the UK and the US. Pillar I gives Australia nuclear-powered submarines; Pillar II covers advanced technology such as AI and hypersonics. Critics question the cost and non-proliferation precedent of transferring naval nuclear material to a non-nuclear-weapon state. Interpretation Re-verify current status
  • Quad: India, the US, Japan and Australia. The Malabar naval exercise now includes all four. It is not a military alliance. See handbook 23.
  • Five Eyes: an intelligence-sharing arrangement among the US, UK, Canada, Australia and New Zealand.
  • US bilateral alliances: with Japan, South Korea, the Philippines and Australia, which provide basing and joint operations.
  • China-Russia cooperation: joint exercises and patrols in the Pacific, though without a formal alliance. Interpretation
  • Ad hoc coalitions: the Red Sea operations Prosperity Guardian (US-led) and the EU's Aspides, set up after Houthi attacks on shipping.
India's pattern

India avoids formal alliances. It partners with several groupings (Quad, IORA, Colombo Security Conclave), buys arms from the US, France and Russia, and keeps its own strategic autonomy. A balanced answer explains both the benefits (access, technology) and costs (dependence, entanglement).

Several of these touch India directly: Hormuz and the Red Sea affect its energy and trade, the South China Sea affects its Indo-Pacific partners, and Taiwan could draw in the Quad. See handbooks 10 and 32.

Lessons of recent conflicts

  • Cheap drones can contest a large navy: Ukraine's uncrewed surface vessels and missiles pushed the Black Sea Fleet back without Ukraine having a conventional navy.
  • Cost-exchange problem: in the Red Sea, expensive interceptors were used against cheap drones and missiles. Magazine depth and cheaper defences matter. Re-verify
  • Merchant shipping is part of the battlespace: insurance rates, rerouting and port security can be attacked without sinking warships.
  • Seabed infrastructure is vulnerable: cable and pipeline incidents show the need for surveillance and repair capacity.
  • Industrial depth wins long wars: shipyards, munitions and repair capacity matter alongside the fleet.
  • Surface ships need layered defence: a single high-value ship loss (Moskva) has strategic and morale effects.
What India can draw from this

Invest in layered air and missile defence, uncrewed systems, maritime domain awareness (including the Information Fusion Centre-IOR), indigenous shipbuilding and a larger submarine force, and keep partnerships active. Analysis, practice and revision

Perspectives, controversies and facts

Interpretation Is China an unstoppable naval power? One view stresses its shipbuilding scale and missile range. Another stresses its limited combat experience, geographic constraints (the "first island chain") and weaker undersea warfare. Both contain truth. Disputed

Interpretation AUKUS. Supporters see a durable undersea balance; critics point to cost, delays and the precedent of non-nuclear-weapon states acquiring naval reactors.

Interpretation Are carriers obsolete? The debate pits vulnerability to missiles against the flexibility of air power at sea, as noted in the technology section.

Interpretation Chinese overseas bases. Some see debt traps and military ambitions; others see commercial ports with limited military use. Evidence varies by site.

Lesser-known facts

  • Established The United States has not ratified UNCLOS but follows most of it as customary law.
  • Established France has the world's second-largest EEZ because of its overseas territories. Re-verify
  • Established Under the Montreux Convention (1936), Turkey controls warship passage through the Bosporus and Dardanelles.
  • Established HMS Dreadnought (1906) was so influential that earlier battleships were called "pre-dreadnoughts".

Scenarios and strategic options

Presented neutrally, without recommending a political position:

Taiwan Strait crisis

A blockade or conflict would disrupt trade and test US and allied navies. India would face economic effects and pressure over its Quad role.

Growing PLAN presence in the Indian Ocean

More Chinese submarines, research ships and base access would raise India's need for surveillance, submarines and regional partnerships.

Mass unmanned warfare

Large numbers of cheap drones change the calculation for expensive ships, favouring defence, dispersion and industrial capacity.

Arctic and northern routes

Ice loss opens routes and resources, increasing military activity by Russia and interest from China and NATO states.

Answer framework and worked example

For any "Which navy matters most?" or "What should India learn?" question, move through definition, location, evidence, contest, analysis, test, position, concession and close. One compressed example follows.

Worked example: "Does China's naval growth threaten India?"

Define
PLAN growth means more ships, submarines, carriers and overseas access.
Locate
Main effort in the Western Pacific; growing presence in the Indian Ocean through anti-piracy patrols, port calls and Djibouti.
Establish
The PLAN is the largest navy by hull count, has three carriers and is building submarines; it has research and survey ships active in the Indian Ocean.
Contest
China's priority remains the Pacific, and its Indian Ocean presence is small and far from home bases.
Analyse
India's advantage is geography, bases such as Andaman and Nicobar, and the chokepoints through which Chinese trade must pass.
Test
Compare Chinese presence now with that over the next decade, and India's surveillance and submarine capacity.
Position
A growing challenge to be managed through capability, partnerships and awareness, not an immediate threat to India's control of its own waters.
Concede
Fleet figures and basing arrangements change quickly. Re-verify
Close
India's response should combine its own build-up, partnerships and watchfulness at the chokepoints.

Glossary

A2/AD
Anti-access/area denial: measures that keep a stronger force out of a region.
AIP
Air-independent propulsion, which lets conventional submarines stay submerged longer.
Aegis
US-origin naval combat system for air and missile defence.
Battle force ships
A counting convention for commissioned combatants and key support ships.
Blue-water navy
A navy able to operate globally and sustain forces far from home.
CATOBAR / STOBAR / STOVL
Carrier launch and recovery methods.
Fleet in being
A fleet whose existence constrains the enemy without fighting.
FONOP
Freedom-of-navigation operation, a naval transit that challenges excessive claims.
Second-strike capability
The ability to retaliate after an enemy nuclear attack, usually by submarines.
Sea control
Ability to use an area of sea and deny it to the enemy.
Sea denial
Preventing an enemy from using an area of sea.
SSBN / SSN / SSK
Nuclear ballistic-missile, nuclear attack and conventional submarines.
USV / UUV
Uncrewed surface and underwater vessels.
VLS
Vertical launch system for missiles on ships.
