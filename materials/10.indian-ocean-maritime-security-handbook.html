Using this handbook

This handbook treats "maritime security" broadly: not just the Indian Navy's order of battle, but the geography, history, economics and diplomacy that make the Indian Ocean central to India's security calculus. Read the contents list and jump to what you need; the Knowledge toolkit at the end is built for last-mile revision.

How to think about "maritime security"

Four layers recur across this subject: hard power (ships, submarines, aircraft), geography (chokepoints and sea lanes), economics (energy, trade, the blue economy) and diplomacy (QUAD, IORA, bilateral logistics agreements). Most exam and interview questions test whether you can move between these layers rather than recite hull numbers.

Why the Indian Ocean matters

It carries roughly two-thirds of the world's oil shipments and a third of its bulk cargo, connecting the Gulf, East Africa and Southeast Asia through a handful of narrow chokepoints. For India, it is not a periphery but the ocean it is named after.

Why it is contested

China's navy is now the world's largest by hull count and increasingly present in these waters through ports, submarines and anti-piracy deployments, while non-state threats — piracy, terrorism, smuggling — persist alongside great-power competition.

Part I · Foundations

Geography, history and doctrine

Maritime history and the making of a navy

The Indian Ocean was a maritime highway long before European ships arrived: monsoon winds carried traders between Gujarat, the Malabar coast, the Persian Gulf, East Africa and Southeast Asia for over a thousand years, and the Chola dynasty projected naval power as far as Southeast Asia in the 11th century. European arrival changed the rules — Portuguese, Dutch, French and finally British power was built and sustained by control of these sea lanes, not just land armies. The Royal Indian Navy that India inherited in 1947 was small and coastal; the modern Indian Navy grew out of it slowly, with the first aircraft carrier, INS Vikrant (the original, a former British carrier), commissioned in 1961.

1971: the Navy's coming of age

The 1971 war is the Navy's defining chapter: Operation Trident and Operation Python struck Karachi harbour with missile boats, and the Eastern Fleet's blockade of East Pakistan, including the aircraft carrier INS Vikrant's air strikes, cut off any seaborne relief or escape — a rare case of naval power directly shaping a land war's outcome and Bangladesh's independence.

Two lessons recur from this history: sea power has repeatedly decided outcomes that armies could not, and neglect of the Navy — which happened for long stretches of the Cold War — is expensive to reverse.

The Indian Ocean's geography and chokepoints

The Indian Ocean is defined as much by its narrow entry and exit points as by its vast open water — control of a handful of straits shapes the security of the whole basin.

India's geographic advantage

Interpretation India's peninsula and the Andaman & Nicobar Islands sit astride the sea lanes linking the Gulf to Malacca — any power moving between the Middle East and East Asia by sea passes close to Indian shores. This is the single most repeated strategic fact in this subject.

Doctrine, SAGAR and command architecture

India's maritime doctrine has evolved from a coastal defence force to a "net security provider" in the Indian Ocean Region. The Navy's own doctrine documents (updated periodically, most recently around 2015) describe roles across the spectrum: deterrence, sea control, power projection, constabulary duties (anti-piracy, anti-smuggling) and benign roles (disaster relief, search and rescue).

  • SAGAR (Security and Growth for All in the Region), announced in 2015, is India's public framework for the Indian Ocean — capacity-building for smaller littoral states, maritime domain awareness, disaster response and freedom of navigation, without alliance obligations.
  • Command structure: Western Naval Command (Mumbai), Eastern Naval Command (Visakhapatnam) and Southern Naval Command (Kochi, training-focused), plus the tri-service Andaman & Nicobar Command.
  • Mission-based deployments since 2017 keep Indian warships continuously stationed at choke points — the northern Arabian Sea, the Gulf of Aden, the Malacca approaches and the Sunda-Lombok approaches — rather than patrolling only when a crisis emerges.

A distinction worth making. SAGAR is a diplomatic framework, not a formal alliance; India's approach in the Indian Ocean deliberately avoids the language of blocs even while it deepens practical cooperation with the US, Japan, Australia, France and Indian Ocean littoral states.

Andaman & Nicobar Command

The Andaman & Nicobar Islands sit closer to Southeast Asia than to the Indian mainland — barely 90 nautical miles from Indonesia at the closest point — placing them directly astride the approaches to the Strait of Malacca. The Andaman & Nicobar Command (2001) is India's only tri-service theatre command, integrating Army, Navy, Air Force and Coast Guard assets under a single commander, and is often cited as the template for the wider theatrisation reform now under discussion for the rest of the armed forces.

Strategic value

Air and naval bases here can monitor and, in a crisis, interdict traffic approaching Malacca — a standing point of leverage often discussed as a counter to China's "Malacca dilemma".

Constraints

Infrastructure (runways, jetties, fuel storage) has historically lagged the islands' strategic importance, though upgrades have accelerated since the 2010s. Re-verify

Part II · Contested waters

Seas, threats and rivals

The Arabian Sea and the western seaboard

The Arabian Sea connects India to the Gulf, the source of most of its energy imports and home to a large Indian expatriate population whose remittances matter to the economy. It is also the route by which most seaborne terrorist and smuggling threats against India's west coast have historically travelled, including the boats used in the 26 November 2008 Mumbai attacks. India maintains a continuous naval presence here, including in the Gulf of Oman and near the Strait of Hormuz, both to protect shipping and to be able to evacuate Indian citizens from Gulf crises — as it did during the 1990 Gulf War (Operation Ghar Wapsi-era evacuations) and again during regional flare-ups since.

A recurring mission: hostage and piracy response

Indian warships escorted merchant shipping and freed hijacked vessels off Somalia through the 2010s as part of international anti-piracy patrols, and in early 2024 an Indian warship (INS Kolkata) intervened after Houthi attacks on a merchant vessel in the Gulf of Aden — the kind of unglamorous, high-frequency mission that dominates actual naval operations, more than headline war scenarios do.

The Bay of Bengal and the eastern seaboard

The Bay of Bengal links India to Bangladesh, Myanmar, Sri Lanka and, through the Andaman Sea, to Southeast Asia and the approaches to Malacca. It has become a more active zone of cooperation in the last decade: BIMSTEC (Bay of Bengal Initiative for Multi-Sectoral Technical and Economic Cooperation) groups India with its eastern littoral neighbours, and the Bay is central to India's "Act East" connectivity projects, including the Kaladan Multi-Modal Transit Transport Project linking north-east India to Myanmar's Sittwe port.

Cooperation

Bangladesh, Sri Lanka and India run joint coastal-surveillance and hydrography programmes; Sri Lanka's ports (Colombo, Hambantota, Trincomalee) sit directly on India's southern maritime approach.

Friction

Chinese-funded port and infrastructure projects in Bangladesh, Myanmar and Sri Lanka have raised Indian concerns about dual-use access, discussed further in the China section below.

Piracy, terrorism and coastal security

The 26/11 Mumbai attacks (2008), carried out by ten terrorists who arrived by sea after hijacking an Indian fishing trawler, are the defining case study in India's coastal-security failures and the reforms that followed.

  • 200826/11 attacks expose gaps between Navy, Coast Guard, state marine police and intelligence agencies.
  • 2009Coastal Security Scheme strengthened; the Indian Navy designated overall authority for coastal defence.
  • 2009Coastal radar chain project launched to cover the entire coastline with static sensors.
  • 2010sSagar Prahari Bal (Navy) and expanded Coast Guard patrol craft strength for close-coast surveillance.
  • OngoingRegistration of fishing boats and biometric identity cards for fishermen, still incomplete in places. Re-verify

Somali piracy, in brief. Piracy off Somalia peaked around 2010–2011 and was suppressed by a combination of international naval patrols (including India's), armed guards on merchant ships and onshore stabilisation — a template regularly cited for the more recent Red Sea/Houthi shipping crisis, though the underlying causes (a land conflict versus a state actor) differ sharply. Interpretation

China's footprint in the Indian Ocean

China's growing presence in the Indian Ocean Region is the single most discussed maritime security concern in Indian strategic circles. The "String of Pearls" is a Western/Indian analytical term (not a Chinese one) for a network of ports and facilities built or funded by China across the region — commercial by design, but with potential dual-use military value.

Contested ports and facilities

How seriously to take "debt-trap diplomacy"

Disputed Some scholars argue the Hambantota case reflects Sri Lanka's own borrowing and governance failures more than a deliberate Chinese strategy; others point to a consistent pattern across several countries. Present both readings rather than asserting either as settled fact — a good example of the "tag" system in practice.

Chinese naval activity in the region — submarine deployments to Karachi and Colombo, research/survey vessels tracked near Indian waters, and regular anti-piracy task-force rotations off the Horn of Africa since 2008 — is closely monitored by India's Information Fusion Centre (see Partnerships, below).

Part III · Capability and economy

Fighting and sustaining at sea

Submarines and the undersea contest

Submarines are widely seen as India's most serious capability gap in the maritime domain — numbers have declined even as China's undersea fleet has grown.

  • Conventional fleet: six Kalvari-class (French Scorpene design, built at Mazagon Dock) delivered from 2017; ageing Russian Kilo-class (Sindhughosh-class) and German HDW boats nearing retirement.
  • Project 75(I): a long-delayed follow-on programme for six new submarines with air-independent propulsion (longer underwater endurance), still in the contracting stage. Re-verify
  • Strategic submarines: INS Arihant (2016) and INS Arighaat (2024) give India a sea-based leg of its nuclear triad; a third and further boats are reported to be under construction. Re-verify
  • China's undersea presence: port calls and reported deployments by Chinese submarines to Pakistan and Sri Lanka since 2014 have driven much of India's investment in anti-submarine warfare.
Why this gap matters more than headline numbers suggest

Interpretation Submarines are the most effective platform for both deterrence and sea denial in a narrow, choke-point-heavy ocean like this one. A fleet that is shrinking in relative terms weakens India's ability to threaten an adversary's shipping in a crisis — arguably a bigger strategic problem than fighter-squadron shortages.

Aircraft carriers and naval aviation

India operates two aircraft carriers — INS Vikramaditya (a converted Russian carrier, commissioned 2013) and the indigenously built INS Vikrant, commissioned on 2 September 2022, only the sixth country to design and build a carrier of this size. A Rafale-M order (2025) for Vikrant's air wing followed years of trials against the Swedish Gripen and other contenders. Re-verify

The case for carriers

Mobile air power that does not depend on a foreign country granting basing or overflight rights — valuable across a vast ocean where India's own land bases cannot reach every chokepoint.

The case against

Carriers are expensive, need large escort fleets, and are vulnerable to long-range anti-ship missiles — some analysts argue submarines and land-based aircraft give better value for the same money. Interpretation

A third carrier has been debated for years without a firm decision — a useful example of how budget competition between the Navy, Army and Air Force plays out in practice. Re-verify

Patrol, ASW, drones and domain awareness

  • Maritime patrol aircraft: P-8I (Boeing, a variant used by only a handful of navies worldwide) fly long-range anti-submarine and surveillance missions from Indian bases across the whole Indian Ocean.
  • Helicopters: MH-60R "Romeo" (US) for ship-based anti-submarine and anti-surface warfare, replacing older Sea King helicopters.
  • Uncrewed systems: MQ-9B Sea Guardian/Predator drones (contracted 2024) for long-endurance maritime surveillance; smaller ship-launched drones for close-in reconnaissance. Re-verify
  • Maritime domain awareness (MDA): a coastal radar chain, the National Command Control Communication and Intelligence (NC3I) network, and satellite tracking of shipping (AIS) feed a real-time picture to naval headquarters.

Why MDA is now as important as weapons. Knowing where every ship, fishing boat and submarine contact is, in real time and shared with partners, does more to prevent both terrorism and great-power incidents than any single weapon system — the logic behind the Information Fusion Centre described below.

Energy security, SLOCs, ports and the blue economy

India imports the large majority of its crude oil, most of it arriving by sea from the Gulf, Russia and, increasingly, the Americas — making the security of Sea Lines of Communication (SLOCs) a direct economic-security issue, not an abstract one. Any prolonged disruption at Hormuz or in the Red Sea raises freight and insurance costs and can move fuel prices within weeks.

Ports and connectivity

The Sagarmala programme (2015) modernises Indian ports and coastal shipping; Chabahar port in Iran (developed with Indian investment) gives India a land-sea route into Afghanistan and Central Asia bypassing Pakistan.

The blue economy

Fisheries, offshore energy, seabed minerals (including deep-sea polymetallic nodules explored under a UN framework) and coastal tourism are increasingly framed as economic pillars alongside security ones, under India's Blue Economy policy framework. Re-verify

Interpretation Energy security in this domain is less about any single chokepoint failing than about diversification — more suppliers, more routes and larger strategic petroleum reserves all reduce the leverage any one disruption can exert.

Part IV · Partnerships

Institutions and diplomacy

QUAD, IORA and India's maritime partnerships

  • Exercise Malabar: begun as a bilateral India-US naval exercise in 1992, joined by Japan in 2015 and made quadrilateral with Australia's return in 2020 — now the most visible symbol of QUAD naval cooperation.
  • QUAD (India, US, Japan, Australia) launched the Indo-Pacific Partnership for Maritime Domain Awareness (IPMDA) in 2022, sharing satellite-based tracking of "dark" (transponder-off) shipping with Indian Ocean and Pacific partners.
  • IORA (Indian Ocean Rim Association), founded 1997, groups over 20 littoral states for economic and maritime-safety cooperation — a forum, not an alliance, and one where China is a "dialogue partner" but not a member.
  • Information Fusion Centre – Indian Ocean Region (IFC-IOR), set up in December 2018 at Gurugram, hosts liaison officers from dozens of partner navies to share shipping and threat data in real time.
  • Bilateral logistics agreements (LEMOA with the US, 2016; similar agreements with France, Japan, Australia and others) let Indian ships refuel and resupply at partner bases across the ocean, extending reach without needing India's own bases everywhere.
Partnership without alliance

Interpretation India deliberately keeps QUAD focused on non-traditional security (HADR, maritime domain awareness, technology) rather than a formal collective-defence pact, consistent with its long-standing preference for strategic autonomy even as practical cooperation with the US and its allies deepens.

Knowledge toolkit

Perspectives, practice and revision

Perspectives, controversies, lesser-known facts

  • Interpretation Is China's Indian Ocean presence primarily commercial (protecting trade routes and citizens) or primarily strategic (encircling India)? Both readings have serious proponents; Chinese naval deployments have grown alongside, not instead of, commercial interests.
  • Disputed "Debt-trap diplomacy" as a deliberate Chinese strategy versus a consequence of recipient countries' own governance and borrowing choices — see the Hambantota discussion above.
  • Established India's Navy is the only one of the three services that entered the 21st century already majority-indigenised in shipbuilding, even though weapons, sensors and engines remain substantially imported.
  • Interpretation Whether a third aircraft carrier is worth the investment compared to more submarines, land-based missiles or satellites remains one of the most genuinely contested debates inside India's own naval establishment.
  • Established — a lesser-known fact — the 1971 naval missile-boat attacks on Karachi (Operation Trident) used Soviet-supplied Osa-class boats and were India's first use of anti-ship missiles in combat, years before most Western navies had similar experience.

Scenarios and India's strategic options

Presented neutrally, without recommending a single course of action.

Scenario: a Hormuz or Red Sea closure

A prolonged closure or heavy attack risk in either chokepoint would raise freight and insurance costs sharply and could require the Navy to escort Indian-flagged or Indian-linked shipping, as it already does on a smaller scale in the Gulf of Aden and Red Sea.

Scenario: a Taiwan Strait crisis

A conflict or blockade near Taiwan would likely disrupt shipping through the South China Sea and Malacca, indirectly affecting Indian trade and potentially drawing QUAD navies into a wider crisis-response posture even without direct Indian involvement.

Options under discussion

  • Accelerate submarine and anti-submarine investment relative to surface combatants and a third carrier.
  • Expand Andaman & Nicobar basing and infrastructure to strengthen leverage near Malacca.
  • Deepen IFC-IOR and IPMDA data-sharing with more littoral states, including some also courted by China.
  • Balance strategic autonomy with QUAD naval cooperation as China's presence in the region grows.

Glossary

SLOC
Sea Line of Communication — a maritime route along which trade, military supplies or reinforcements move.
SAGAR
Security and Growth for All in the Region — India's 2015 framework for engagement with the Indian Ocean littoral.
String of Pearls
An Indian/Western analytical term for a network of Chinese-linked ports and facilities across the Indian Ocean; not a term China itself uses.
IORA
Indian Ocean Rim Association — a 20-plus-member forum for economic and maritime cooperation among Indian Ocean littoral states.
IFC-IOR
Information Fusion Centre – Indian Ocean Region, based at Gurugram, sharing maritime domain awareness data among partner navies.
QUAD
The Quadrilateral Security Dialogue among India, the United States, Japan and Australia.
IPMDA
Indo-Pacific Partnership for Maritime Domain Awareness — a QUAD initiative sharing satellite-based ship-tracking data.
Malacca dilemma
China's own term for its strategic vulnerability from dependence on the narrow, easily monitored Strait of Malacca for trade and energy imports.
Net security provider
A description of a state whose naval capacity exceeds its own needs, allowing it to provide security services (anti-piracy, disaster relief, surveillance) to a wider region.
Air-independent propulsion (AIP)
Submarine technology that lets a conventional submarine stay submerged far longer than battery power alone allows.
Blue economy
Sustainable economic use of ocean resources — fisheries, offshore energy, tourism and seabed minerals — alongside conventional maritime security.
Sagarmala
India's 2015 programme to modernise ports and port-linked infrastructure and reduce logistics costs.
Mission-based deployment
The Indian Navy's post-2017 practice of keeping ships continuously stationed at key chokepoints rather than patrolling only reactively.

Rapid revision sheet

The one-page version

  • India's coastline (7,516 km) and EEZ (~2.02M km²) sit astride the sea lanes linking the Gulf to East Asia — geography is India's core maritime advantage.
  • Five chokepoints to know cold: Hormuz, Bab-el-Mandeb, Malacca, Sunda/Lombok, Mozambique Channel.
  • 1971 (Operations Trident/Python, the Eastern Fleet blockade) is the Navy's defining historical case study.
  • SAGAR (2015) is India's doctrine label for the Indian Ocean — cooperative, not alliance-based.
  • 26/11 (2008) reshaped coastal security; gaps in fishing-boat registration and coordination remain.
  • China's footprint — Gwadar, Hambantota, Djibouti, Kyaukpyu — is the most-discussed contemporary issue; present both the "strategic encirclement" and "commercial + governance failure" readings.
  • Submarines, not carriers, are the most commonly cited capability gap; INS Vikrant (2022) and Arighaat (2024) are the flagship recent inductions either way.
  • QUAD/Malabar/IPMDA/IFC-IOR are the institutional architecture of India's Indo-Pacific maritime diplomacy — know what each one actually does.
