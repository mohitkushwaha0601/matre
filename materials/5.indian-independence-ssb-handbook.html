Using this handbook

This is a reference document, not a novel. Read Section 2 and Section 44 first; then dip into whatever the board is likely to ask you about. Nothing here needs to be memorised word for word — what you need is the shape of the story, roughly a dozen dates you never get wrong, and the ability to see more than one side of every question.

Three labels used throughout

Good history separates what happened from what it meant. Where a claim is contested, this handbook says so, using three tags:

Established Documented and broadly agreed across schools of history — dates, texts, statutes, orders, outcomes.

Interpretation A reasonable reading of evidence that serious historians argue about. You may adopt one, but say that you are adopting one.

Disputed Figures or claims where the evidence is genuinely weak, politically loaded, or where estimates vary by an order of magnitude. Quote a range, never a single number.

The single most useful habit

Learn to say “the established figure is X, though estimates range from Y to Z, and here is why they differ.” An Interviewing Officer is not testing whether you know the number. He is testing whether you know the difference between knowing and guessing — which is exactly the quality that matters when you are reporting a situation up the chain of command.

What this handbook deliberately does

  • Treats colonialism as a system, not a villain. Anger is easy and uninformative. Understanding how revenue, law, army and ideology fitted together is what produces good analysis.
  • Gives the British case as the British made it, so you can dismantle it properly instead of attacking a caricature.
  • Refuses hero-versus-hero framing. Gandhi versus Bose, Nehru versus Patel, Congress versus revolutionaries — these are poor questions. “What did each contribute, and what were the costs of each approach?” is a good question.
  • Keeps the military dimension visible. The Indian Army is the thread that runs from Plassey through 1857, two world wars, the INA trials, the RIN mutiny and the partition of the armed forces in 1947. For a defence aspirant this thread matters more than any other.

Snapshot: 450 years in one page

If you remember nothing else, remember this arc: Europe arrived for spices; stayed for textiles; conquered because India was politically fragmented and militarily out-financed; governed through Indian collaborators, Indian money and an Indian army; taught English to create clerks and accidentally created nationalists; broke under the cumulative weight of mass mobilisation, a bankrupting world war and the loss of confidence in the loyalty of Indian troops; and left in a hurry, dividing the country on the way out.

Interview edge

If asked “tell me about the freedom struggle,” never start at 1857 or at Gandhi. Start with structure: “Sir, I see it in three layers — how we lost sovereignty between 1757 and 1849, how the colonial system worked, and how the national movement grew from petitions in the 1880s to mass mobilisation after 1919.” Then offer one specific example from each layer. Thirty seconds of structure beats three minutes of anecdote.

Part I

Traders to rulers, 1498–1858

India on the eve of contact

The most common error in this topic is to imagine a weak, backward India passively conquered by a superior Europe. The reality is the opposite at the start and only slowly reverses. In 1700, Mughal India was among the two largest economies on earth, the world’s dominant manufacturer of cotton textiles, and a net importer of bullion — Europe had almost nothing India wanted except silver.

The economic picture

  • Interpretation Angus Maddison’s widely cited estimates put India at roughly 24–25 per cent of world GDP around 1700, comparable to Qing China, against Britain’s 2–3 per cent. These are reconstructions from fragmentary evidence, not census data — use them for scale, not precision.
  • India dominated the global textile trade. Calico (from Calicut), chintz, muslin (Dhaka), bandanna, dungaree, gingham, khaki, seersucker, pyjama — the English language absorbed Indian cloth vocabulary because Britain bought Indian cloth.
  • Britain’s Calico Acts of 1700 and 1721 banned or restricted the import of Indian printed cottons to protect English weavers. Protectionism ran against India long before the drain debate began.
  • Indian manufacturing was organised, not primitive: advanced dyeing (especially fast reds), shipbuilding at Surat and later Bombay, wootz steel exported for centuries, sophisticated credit instruments (hundi) and banking houses such as the Jagat Seths of Murshidabad.

The political picture

Aurangzeb died in 1707 after a reign that expanded the Mughal empire to its greatest extent while exhausting its finances in the Deccan wars. The eighteenth century then produced not a vacuum but a crowded field of successor states: Bengal under Murshid Quli Khan and his successors, Awadh under the Nawabs, Hyderabad under the Nizam, the Maratha confederacy with the Peshwa at Pune and the Scindia, Holkar, Gaekwad and Bhonsle houses, Mysore under Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan, the Sikh misls consolidating into Ranjit Singh’s Punjab, the Rajput states, the Rohillas, Travancore in the far south.

A correction worth carrying into an interview. Older textbooks describe the eighteenth century as one of “decline and chaos.” Modern scholarship (Christopher Bayly, Muzaffar Alam, Andre Wink, Seema Alavi) shows many successor states were economically dynamic — Bengal and Awadh in particular — with expanding commerce, revenue farming and military modernisation. The problem was not weakness but fragmentation: a dozen competent states that could not combine, facing one organisation with global credit and a permanent navy.

Three shocks that softened the ground

  • Nadir Shah’s invasion, 1739. The Persian ruler defeated the Mughals at Karnal, sacked Delhi with a general massacre, and carried away the Peacock Throne and the Koh-i-Noor. Mughal prestige never recovered.
  • Ahmad Shah Abdali’s raids, 1748–1767. Repeated Afghan invasions drained the north.
  • Third Battle of Panipat, 14 January 1761. Abdali crushed the Marathas — the one Indian power capable of building a subcontinental successor empire. Interpretation Many historians see Panipat as the moment that left the field open to the English, though Maratha power revived substantially by the 1770s under Mahadji Scindia.

Arrival of the Europeans

Four European trading nations mattered — Portugal, the Netherlands, England and France — plus minor Danish and Austrian ventures. Each arrived for the same reason: the Ottoman and Venetian control of overland spice routes made a sea route to the source extremely profitable. Each behaved differently, and the differences explain who survived.

Portugal: first in, first out

  • 20 May 1498 — Vasco da Gama reaches Calicut, guided across the Arabian Sea from Malindi. The Zamorin of Calicut receives him; Arab merchants resist the intrusion.
  • 1500 — Pedro Álvares Cabral; 1502 — da Gama’s violent second voyage. 1505–09 — Francisco de Almeida, first Viceroy, pursues the Blue Water Policy (cartaz system of naval passes; control the sea, not the land).
  • 1509–15 — Afonso de Albuquerque, the real founder of the Portuguese empire in Asia. Captures Goa in 1510 from the Sultan of Bijapur. Later Portuguese possessions: Daman, Diu, Bassein, Salsette, Bombay, Hooghly, São Tomé de Meliapore.
  • 1530 — Nino da Cunha shifts the capital from Cochin to Goa.
  • Decline: the union of the Portuguese and Spanish crowns (1580–1640) exposed Portugal to Dutch and English attack; Shah Jahan expelled them from Hooghly in 1632; religious aggressiveness (the Goa Inquisition, established 1560) made them unpopular; and their model of monopoly-by-force could not match Dutch and English joint-stock capital. Goa, Daman and Diu remained Portuguese until 1961 — the longest European presence in India by far, 451 years.

The Dutch: the efficient competitor

  • 1602 — the Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie (VOC) is formed by merging rival Dutch companies. It was the world’s first company to issue tradeable shares to the public, and for a time the richest corporation in history.
  • Factories at Masulipatnam (1605), Pulicat (1610, their main early base, where they struck the “pagoda” coin), Surat, Chinsurah, Nagapattinam, Cochin.
  • Dutch priority was the Indonesian spice islands; India was mainly a source of cotton textiles to exchange for spices. That strategic choice meant they never invested in Indian territorial power.
  • Battle of Bedara (Hooghly/Chinsurah), 1759 — the English defeat the Dutch in Bengal, effectively ending Dutch ambitions in India. Most Dutch possessions passed to Britain by the Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824.

England: the survivor

  • 31 December 1600 — Royal Charter from Elizabeth I to “the Governor and Company of Merchants of London trading into the East Indies”: a 15-year monopoly, later renewed and extended.
  • 1608 — Captain William Hawkins arrives at Surat and reaches Jahangir’s court; charms the Emperor personally but fails to win trading rights against Portuguese influence.
  • 1612 — Captain Thomas Best defeats a Portuguese fleet off Swally (Suvali) near Surat; a second defeat in 1615 convinces the Mughals the English are useful naval allies. 1613 — first English factory at Surat.
  • 1615–19 — Sir Thomas Roe, ambassador of James I, secures a firman for trade and factories. His famous advice to the Company: “let this be received as a rule that if you will profit, seek it at sea and in quiet trade… it is an error to affect garrisons and land wars in India.” The Company would spend the next two centuries ignoring him.
  • 1639 — Francis Day obtains Madraspatnam from the Nayak of Chandragiri; Fort St George built — the first English fortified settlement and the nucleus of Madras.
  • 1661 — Bombay comes to Charles II as part of Catherine of Braganza’s dowry; leased to the Company in 1668 for £10 a year. Gerald Aungier develops it; the deep natural harbour makes it the Company’s western headquarters, replacing Surat.
  • 1690 — Job Charnock settles at Sutanuti; the villages of Sutanuti, Kalikata and Govindpur are acquired in 1698; Fort William completed 1700 — Calcutta. Disputed In 2003 the Calcutta High Court held that Charnock should not be called the city’s “founder” and that 1690 is not a foundation date — the settlements predate him.
  • 1717 — Emperor Farrukhsiyar’s farman, obtained after the English surgeon William Hamilton cured the Emperor: duty-free trade in Bengal for an annual payment of Rs 3,000, the right to issue dastaks (passes), and permission to rent more villages. Called the Company’s “Magna Carta” — and the systematic abuse of the dastak by private English traders became the direct cause of conflict with the Nawabs of Bengal.

France: the near-miss

  • 1664 — the Compagnie des Indes Orientales founded under Colbert as a state enterprise, funded and directed by the French crown. That dependence on Versailles, always distracted by European wars, proved fatal.
  • Surat (1668), Masulipatnam (1669), Pondicherry (1673, founded by François Martin — their headquarters), Chandernagore (1690), Mahe, Karaikal, Yanam.
  • Joseph François Dupleix (Governor-General 1742–54) invented the technique that the English then used better than he did: intervene in Indian succession disputes, install a client, and be paid in revenue rights and territory rather than trade profits. He raised sepoy battalions drilled in European fashion — the model for every colonial army that followed.

The point most candidates miss

England did not win because of superior soldiers. It won because of superior finance. The Financial Revolution gave Britain the ability to borrow enormous sums cheaply and sustain war longer than any opponent. A Maratha or Mysorean army could win battles; it could not lose three campaigns in a row and still pay its troops. The Company could. That is a strategic lesson with permanent relevance — wars are won by the side that can sustain the cost of not winning yet.

The Anglo-French contest: the Carnatic Wars

Between 1746 and 1763 the English and French fought three wars in south India that were extensions of European conflicts. They are important for three reasons: they proved that small European-trained forces could defeat much larger Indian armies; they taught both sides the technique of the puppet ruler; and they eliminated France, leaving Britain without a European rival on the subcontinent.

  • 1746–48First Carnatic War — extension of the War of Austrian Succession. La Bourdonnais captures Madras (1746). At the Battle of St Thomé (Adyar), 1746, a small French force with Indian sepoys defeats a far larger army of the Nawab of Carnatic — the demonstration effect that changed everything. Madras returned to Britain by the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle (1748) in exchange for Louisbourg in North America.
  • 1749–54Second Carnatic War — no European war behind it; purely a contest of Indian client-making. Dupleix backs Chanda Sahib in the Carnatic and Muzaffar Jung in Hyderabad; the English back Muhammad Ali and Nasir Jung. Robert Clive’s seizure and defence of Arcot (1751) relieves pressure on Trichinopoly and makes his reputation. Dupleix is recalled in disgrace in 1754; Treaty of Pondicherry ends the war.
  • 1758–63Third Carnatic War — extension of the Seven Years’ War, the first genuinely global conflict. Count de Lally takes Fort St David but fails at Madras. Battle of Wandiwash, 22 January 1760 — Sir Eyre Coote decisively defeats Lally. Pondicherry falls in 1761. The Treaty of Paris (1763) restores French factories but forbids their fortification or the keeping of troops — France is reduced to a commercial presence.

Why France lost. Four standard reasons: (1) the English company was commercially stronger and could fund war from trade; (2) English naval superiority allowed reinforcement and cut French supply; (3) the French company depended on a government fighting for survival in Europe; (4) after 1757 the English held Bengal — the richest revenue province in India — and could pay for the south out of Bengali taxes. Point 4 is the one that wins marks.

Plassey to Paramountcy: the conquest of India

Conquest took ninety-two years, from Plassey in 1757 to the annexation of Punjab in 1849. It was not a single invasion but a rolling process of war, subsidised alliance, debt, annexation and absorption — funded increasingly from Indian revenue and fought increasingly by Indian soldiers.

Bengal: the foundation stone

The road to Plassey

Alivardi Khan’s grandson Siraj-ud-Daulah became Nawab in April 1756 at about twenty-three, facing a hostile court faction and an English company that was fortifying Calcutta without permission and abusing the 1717 dastak — private English traders were evading customs duty on internal trade, which Indian merchants had to pay. This was a straightforward challenge to the Nawab’s sovereignty and revenue.

  • June 1756 — Siraj captures Calcutta. The “Black Hole of Calcutta” episode follows. Disputed J.Z. Holwell claimed 146 prisoners were confined overnight in a small room and 123 died. Modern historians regard the numbers as heavily inflated — the room could not hold 146 — and some question the account entirely; but that something happened, and that it was used ruthlessly as propaganda for decades, is not disputed.
  • January 1757 — Clive and Admiral Watson retake Calcutta; Treaty of Alinagar restores Company privileges. In March the English take French Chandernagore, removing Siraj’s possible European counterweight.
  • Clive then conspires with the disaffected: Mir Jafar (the Nawab’s commander-in-chief), the banking house of the Jagat Seths, the merchant Amichand and Rai Durlabh.

Battle of Plassey, 23 June 1757

  • Forces: roughly 3,000 Company troops (about two-thirds Indian sepoys) with 10 guns, against a Nawabi army of some 50,000 with French artillerymen.
  • The bulk of Siraj’s army under Mir Jafar and Rai Durlabh stood aside. Only the division under Mir Madan and Mohan Lal fought; Mir Madan was killed by a cannon shot, and a monsoon shower soaked the Nawab’s powder while the English covered theirs.
  • Casualties were trivial — around 22 Company dead. Established Plassey was a transaction, not a battle. It is the classic illustration that political subversion can be cheaper than combat.
  • Siraj fled and was killed. Mir Jafar became Nawab and paid enormous sums: the Company, its servants and Clive personally extracted a fortune. Clive took roughly £234,000 for himself — equivalent to many tens of millions today — and later told Parliament he “stood astonished at his own moderation.”

Battle of Buxar, 22 October 1764 — the decisive one

Mir Jafar could not pay; he was replaced by Mir Qasim (1760), an able ruler who shifted his capital to Munger, modernised his army and — crucially — abolished all internal duties so that Indian merchants would not be disadvantaged against dastak-abusing Englishmen. The Company treated this equality as an outrage. War followed; the massacre of English prisoners at Patna followed that.

  • At Buxar, Major Hector Munro with about 7,000 troops defeated the combined armies of Mir Qasim, Shuja-ud-Daula (Nawab of Awadh) and the Mughal Emperor Shah Alam II — perhaps 40,000 men.
  • This is the militarily decisive engagement of the conquest. Plassey gave the Company Bengal’s politics; Buxar gave it legal, imperial sanction and defeated the last coalition capable of expelling it from the Gangetic plain.
  • Treaty of Allahabad, August 1765 — Clive grants Shah Alam II an annual tribute of Rs 26 lakh and the districts of Kora and Allahabad; in return the Emperor confers the Diwani (right to collect revenue) of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa on the Company. Awadh is restored to Shuja-ud-Daula as a buffer state on payment of an indemnity.
Why 1765 matters more than 1757

With the Diwani the Company became a territorial sovereign collecting taxes while still calling itself a trading firm. From this point the “investment” — goods sent to England — could be bought with Bengal’s own revenue rather than with silver shipped from London. Exports that had previously been paid for were now, in effect, extracted. This is the technical core of the “drain of wealth” argument, and it begins precisely here.

Dual Government and the famine of 1770

Clive’s Dual Government (1765–72) split power perversely: the Company held the revenue, the Nawab held the administration and policing, and neither held responsibility. Revenue collection intensified; the deputy diwans Muhammad Reza Khan and Shitab Rai did the actual work under Company pressure.

The Bengal Famine of 1770 (Bengali year 1176, hence Chhiyattorer Monnontor) followed failed monsoons in 1768 and 1769. Disputed Contemporary Company estimates suggested about one-third of Bengal’s population died, often quoted as 10 million; modern demographers regard that as too high and suggest a lower but still catastrophic toll. What is not disputed is that land revenue collection was maintained and in some districts increased during the famine, that grain speculation by Company servants occurred, and that the episode caused revulsion in Britain — contributing directly to the first Parliamentary regulation of the Company in 1773.

Tipu is worth knowing properly. He deployed iron-cased rockets in large formations — the Mysorean rockets captured at Seringapatam were studied by William Congreve and led directly to the Congreve rocket used by the British in the Napoleonic wars and at Fort McHenry in 1814. Anglo-Maratha Wars (1775–1819)

  • First (1775–82) — arising from a Peshwa succession dispute and the Treaty of Surat with Raghunath Rao. The Company suffered the humiliating Convention of Wadgaon (1779). The Treaty of Salbai (1782) restored the status quo and gave the British twenty years of peace with the Marathas — allowing them to concentrate on Mysore.
  • Second (1803–05) — after defeat by Holkar, Peshwa Baji Rao II signed the Treaty of Bassein (1802), accepting a subsidiary alliance. The other Maratha chiefs refused to accept the Peshwa selling the confederacy. Arthur Wellesley (the future Duke of Wellington) won at Assaye and Argaon (1803), Lake took Delhi and Aligarh. Wellington later called Assaye the hardest-fought battle of his career — harder than Waterloo.
  • Third (1817–19) — Hastings’ campaign against the Pindaris merged into a final Maratha war. The Peshwa was defeated at Khadki and Koregaon, deposed and pensioned off to Bithur; the office of Peshwa was abolished. Established After 1819 no Indian power could challenge the Company in the field except the Sikhs.

Anglo-Sikh Wars (1845–49)

Ranjit Singh (ruled 1799–1839) built a genuinely modern state and the Fauj-i-Khas, trained by European officers including Ventura and Allard — probably the most formidable Indian army the British ever faced. He kept peace with the Company through the Treaty of Amritsar (1809). Within six years of his death, court intrigue and an over-mighty, under-controlled army produced war.

  • First War (1845–46) — Mudki, Ferozeshah, Aliwal, Sobraon. The British won narrowly and partly because of the treachery of the Sikh commanders Lal Singh and Tej Singh. Treaty of Lahore and the Treaty of Amritsar — Kashmir was sold to Gulab Singh of Jammu for 75 lakh rupees, creating the princely state of Jammu and Kashmir whose status would matter enormously in 1947.
  • Second War (1848–49) — Chillianwala (a bloody near-defeat for the British) and Gujrat (decisive). Punjab annexed, 29 March 1849; Maharaja Duleep Singh deposed; the Koh-i-Noor sent to Queen Victoria.

The two instruments of bloodless conquest

Subsidiary Alliance — Lord Wellesley (1798–1805)

Terms imposed on an Indian ruler:

  • Accept a British force stationed in his territory and pay for it — in cash or, increasingly, by ceding territory.
  • Accept a British Resident at court.
  • Dismiss all Europeans of other nationalities.
  • Conduct no diplomacy or war without British consent.

Result: the ruler kept his throne and lost his sovereignty; his army decayed; his revenue was squeezed to pay the subsidy; disorder followed; and disorder justified annexation. Hyderabad (1798) was first; then Mysore, Tanjore, Awadh (1801), the Peshwa (1802), Scindia and Bhonsle (1803).

Doctrine of Lapse — Lord Dalhousie (1848–56)

A dependent state without a natural heir “lapsed” to the paramount power; adopted heirs were not recognised for succession, though sometimes for private property and titles.

  • Satara 1848 · Jaitpur & Sambalpur 1849 · Baghat 1850 · Udaipur (Chhattisgarh) 1852 · Jhansi 1853 · Nagpur 1854.
  • Titles and pensions withdrawn: the Nawab of Carnatic, the Raja of Tanjore, and the Peshwa’s pension refused to his adopted son Nana Saheb.
  • Awadh annexed 1856 — not by lapse but on the ground of misgovernance, despite Awadh being a loyal ally since 1765. Wajid Ali Shah was deposed and exiled to Calcutta.

Awadh mattered disproportionately: a very large share of the Bengal Army’s sepoys were high-caste recruits from Awadh, and annexation hurt their families’ status and revenue terms directly. Interpretation Many historians treat this as the single most important political cause of 1857.

Why the Company won — six reasons

This is a favourite interview question because it separates candidates who have memorised battles from those who can analyse. Six factors, in rough order of importance.

  1. Political fragmentation and the absence of a national idea. There was no “India” to defend in the eighteenth-century political sense. Rulers allied with the Company against their Indian rivals, repeatedly, until it was too late. The British were never fighting more than one major Indian power at a time — and usually had Indian allies.
  2. Finance and credit. Indian bankers — the Jagat Seths, the house of Gopaldas, Arjunji Nathji Travadi — financed the Company because it paid reliably and won. Britain could also borrow at low rates against a national debt market. Indian states relied on land revenue, which is inelastic and collapses in a bad monsoon.
  3. Military system, not military technology. The technological gap in weapons was modest — Mysore had rockets and good artillery; Sikh artillery was excellent. The real gap was organisational: regular pay, standard drill, disciplined infantry volleys, a professional officer corps, integrated artillery and logistics, and above all continuity of command. European-style battalions could manoeuvre and reload under fire; feudal levies could not.
  4. Sea power. The Royal Navy could reinforce, resupply, blockade and shift forces between Madras, Bombay and Calcutta. No Indian power maintained a blue-water navy after the decline of the Marathas’ Angre fleet (Kanhoji Angre died 1729; Gheria fell 1756).
  5. Diplomacy and intelligence. Residents at every court, a network of newswriters, systematic map-making (the Great Trigonometrical Survey from 1802), and a willingness to bribe, subvert and wait. Clive at Plassey is the template.
  6. Indian manpower. Established India was conquered principally by Indian soldiers. By 1857 the Company’s army numbered roughly 300,000, of whom about 240,000 were Indian sepoys — a ratio of nearly five to one. This is the single most uncomfortable and most important fact in the entire subject.
Interview edge — the question behind the question

If asked “how could a trading company conquer India?”, the answer the board wants is not a list. It is the recognition that no external power can hold a large country without internal collaboration — bankers who financed it, princes who allied with it, zamindars who collected for it, clerks who administered it and sepoys who fought for it. That is also why the national movement’s real breakthrough was not any single protest but the slow withdrawal of collaboration: Gandhi understood this better than anyone, which is why his central technique was non-cooperation rather than confrontation.

Part II

The colonial system: how the Raj actually worked

Colonial administration

The Raj was a remarkably thin structure. At its height around 1900, roughly 1,100 covenanted civil servants and some 70,000 British troops governed close to 300 million people. It worked because it rested on Indian revenue, Indian troops, Indian clerks and Indian intermediaries — and because it built three things the previous order lacked: a uniform legal code, a centralised bureaucracy and a modern communications grid.

The pillars of the structure

1. The Indian Civil Service — “the steel frame”

  • Cornwallis (1786–93) is usually credited with creating a professional, relatively uncorrupt service — by raising salaries sharply and excluding Indians from all higher posts. The two went together: “every native of Hindustan, I verily believe, is corrupt” was his stated premise.
  • The district was the basic unit; the District Collector/Magistrate combined revenue, magisterial and often judicial power — a concentration deliberately avoided in Britain itself.
  • Indianisation was glacial: by 1915 Indians held roughly 5 per cent of ICS posts; by 1947 about half, largely because recruitment in India began only in 1922 (following the Lee Commission of 1924 and Montagu–Chelmsford reforms).
  • Interpretation The ICS was simultaneously an instrument of subordination and the administrative spine independent India inherited and retained. Both statements are true; a good answer holds them together.

2. The Army

  • Three Presidency armies (Bengal, Madras, Bombay), unified into the Indian Army in 1895 and reorganised by Lord Kitchener (1902–09).
  • After 1857 the ratio of European to Indian troops was fixed at roughly 1:2 (from about 1:5 before); artillery was placed almost entirely in European hands; and regiments were deliberately mixed by caste, region and religion so that they could not combine — the doctrine of “divide and counterpoise.”
  • The “martial races” theory (Lord Roberts and others) concentrated recruitment on Punjabis, Sikhs, Gurkhas, Pathans, Dogras, Rajputs, Marathas and Jats, and reduced recruitment from Bengal and much of the south. Interpretation This was pseudo-scientific ethnography serving a security purpose: recruit from regions that had not rebelled in 1857, and from populations with limited access to the new English-educated politics.
  • The Indian Army was used far beyond India — China (1839–42, 1860, 1900), Afghanistan (1839–42, 1878–80, 1919), Persia, Abyssinia (1868), Burma, Egypt, Sudan, East Africa, Mesopotamia, France, Gallipoli, Palestine, Italy, North Africa, Malaya. India paid for these expeditions.

3. The police and the law

  • Police Act 1861, following the Police Commission of 1860 — created the structure (IG at provincial level, SP at district, subordinate to the Collector) that independent India largely retained until well after 1947. It was designed as a regime-protection force, not a citizen-service force.
  • Codification: Indian Penal Code (1860), Code of Criminal Procedure (1861, revised 1872, 1898), Indian Evidence Act (1872), Indian Contract Act (1872). A genuine and lasting achievement — a single legal system for a subcontinent.
  • But justice was not equal. Europeans enjoyed separate trial rights; the Ilbert Bill controversy of 1883 exposed this starkly (see below).
  • Criminal Tribes Act 1871 — declared entire communities “criminal by birth”, subject to registration and surveillance. Repealed only in 1952; the communities were then styled “denotified tribes” and the stigma persists. This is a strong, under-used example of colonial law as social control.

4. The princely states

  • Roughly two-fifths of the subcontinent’s area and a quarter of its population lay in some 560–580 princely states, from Hyderabad (the size of Italy) down to estates of a few square kilometres.
  • After 1858 annexation stopped; instead the Crown exercised paramountcy — states controlled their internal affairs but surrendered defence, foreign relations and communications, and accepted a Resident. Gun salutes (21, 19, 17, down to non-salute) codified the hierarchy.
  • Interpretation The states were a conservative ballast deliberately preserved: they gave the Raj allies against nationalism, reduced administrative cost, and — as the Chamber of Princes (1921) showed — could be used as a counterweight in constitutional bargaining. In 1947 their theoretical return to independence became the most dangerous problem India faced.

Two revealing episodes

The Ilbert Bill, 1883

Viceroy Ripon’s law member C.P. Ilbert proposed allowing Indian judges in the mofussil to try European defendants. The British community in India erupted in an organised, well-funded agitation — the “white mutiny” — with racial language of extraordinary crudity. The Bill was gutted: Europeans retained the right to a jury at least half European.

Why it matters: educated Indians learned two lessons. First, that British claims of equality before law were conditional on race. Second — and more important — that organised agitation works. The Congress was founded two years later, and several founders said so explicitly.

Vernacular Press & Arms Acts, 1878

Lytton’s Vernacular Press Act imposed pre-censorship on Indian-language newspapers while exempting the English-language press — a distinction so nakedly racial that it was called the “Gagging Act.” Repealed by Ripon in 1882. The Indian Arms Act 1878 required Indians to be licensed to possess arms, with Europeans exempt.

Both were passed while the Delhi Durbar of 1877 proclaimed Victoria Empress of India amid famine in the Deccan that was killing millions. The contrast was noted at the time and used relentlessly by nationalists.

Land revenue and the peasant

Land revenue was the state’s main income — often 50 per cent or more of total revenue in the nineteenth century. How it was assessed and collected determined rural society more than any other colonial policy. Three systems covered India, and all three shared one feature the pre-colonial systems lacked: the demand was fixed in cash, payable on a fixed date, enforced by sale of the land.

What actually changed in the village

  • Cash, not kind. Pre-colonial revenue was frequently taken as a share of the crop, so a bad harvest automatically reduced the demand. A fixed cash demand transfers all weather risk to the cultivator.
  • Rigidity. Mughal and regional administrations routinely granted remissions (taqavi loans, suspensions) in bad years. Early colonial practice was far less flexible — partly because officials were judged on collection figures.
  • Land as a commodity. Once land could be sold to satisfy debt or revenue arrears, the moneylender became the decisive figure in rural India. The result was mass land alienation — so severe in Punjab that the government passed the Punjab Land Alienation Act, 1900, forbidding transfer of land from “agricultural” to “non-agricultural” tribes, an admission of policy failure.
  • Commercialisation. Indigo, opium, cotton, jute, tea, oilseeds and groundnut expanded. Where it was voluntary and prices were good it raised incomes; where it was coerced — the tinkathia system in Champaran obliging tenants to plant indigo on three-twentieths of their land — it produced revolt.
  • De-industrialisation of the countryside. Weavers, spinners and artisans displaced by machine-made imports had nowhere to go but back to the land, raising the proportion of the population dependent on agriculture. Interpretation The scale of this shift is debated; that it occurred in the cotton textile sector between roughly 1820 and 1870 is not.

A fact worth quoting. The Deccan Riots of 1875 — peasants in Poona and Ahmednagar attacking moneylenders and, revealingly, burning the bonds and account books rather than the people — produced the Deccan Riots Commission and the Deccan Agriculturists’ Relief Act 1879. The targeting of documents rather than persons tells you the grievance was legal-financial, not communal. It is a precise, memorable example of the rural crisis.

Economic exploitation: the drain debate

This is the section where candidates most often lose balance. The correct approach is to state the mechanism precisely, give the nationalist case, give the revisionist counter-case, and then say what you think and why. Anyone can be angry; very few can explain how the money moved.

The mechanism, stated precisely

  1. Unrequited exports. After 1765 the Company purchased its Indian “investment” — textiles, saltpetre, indigo, later opium — using Bengal’s land revenue. Goods left India; no equivalent value entered. This is the technical definition of a drain: an export surplus for which no payment is received.
  2. Home Charges. After 1858 India annually remitted to London: interest on the public debt (including debt incurred to suppress the 1857 revolt), the entire cost of the India Office, pensions and furlough pay of British officials, military expenditure abroad, guaranteed 5 per cent returns to railway shareholders, and stores purchased in Britain. By the 1900s Home Charges ran at roughly £16–20 million a year.
  3. The Council Bills mechanism. The Secretary of State sold bills in London drawn on the Indian treasury; buyers used them to pay for Indian exports. India’s export surplus was therefore converted into sterling in London rather than into imports or gold for India.
  4. Trade policy asymmetry. British cotton goods entered India at low or nil duty; Indian textiles entering Britain had faced heavy duties earlier. When India did impose a cotton import duty in 1894 for revenue reasons, Lancashire pressure forced a countervailing excise duty on Indian mill cloth in 1896 to cancel the advantage. Indian nationalists never forgot this; it became the single most effective illustration of subordination of Indian interests.
  5. Railway finance. Built with a guaranteed return of about 5 per cent to British investors, paid by the Indian taxpayer regardless of profitability. Rolling stock, rails and engineering were bought in Britain. Interpretation This is called “private enterprise at public risk” — an accurate description of the guarantee system, whatever one thinks of the railways’ ultimate value.
  6. World War II sterling balances. India supplied goods, services and 2.5 million soldiers; Britain paid in blocked sterling credits that India could not spend during the war. By 1945 these stood at roughly £1.3 billion, and Britain — the world’s largest debtor — owed its largest single debt to its own colony. They were released only slowly after 1947.

The nationalist economists

  • Dadabhai Naoroji (1825–1917), the “Grand Old Man of India”: first articulated the drain theory from the 1860s; founded the East India Association (1866); first Indian elected to the British House of Commons (Central Finsbury, 1892, as a Liberal); book Poverty and Un-British Rule in India (1901). His estimate of Indian per-capita income (about Rs 20 a year in 1870) was the first serious attempt at national income accounting anywhere.
  • Romesh Chunder Dutt, The Economic History of India (1902, 1904) — the systematic historical case, focused on land revenue and de-industrialisation.
  • Mahadev Govind Ranade — argued for state-supported industrialisation; the intellectual father of Indian economic nationalism.
  • G.V. Joshi, G. Subramania Iyer, Prithwis Chandra Ray — the wider school.

Interpretation The historical significance of this school is not that its numbers were exact — they were rough — but that it shifted nationalist argument from sentiment to economics. Bipan Chandra argued that the economic critique was the single most powerful solvent of British moral legitimacy, because it attacked the claim that British rule was materially beneficial. That claim was the Raj’s core justification.

Two sides, fairly stated

The case that colonialism impoverished India

  • India’s share of world manufacturing output fell from roughly 25 per cent in 1750 to about 2 per cent by 1900 (Paul Bairoch’s estimates).
  • Per-capita income stagnated: on most estimates there was little or no growth in Indian per-capita income between 1900 and 1947, while Britain’s doubled.
  • Life expectancy at independence was around 32 years; literacy around 16–18 per cent; famine deaths in the millions repeatedly.
  • Industrial policy was actively obstructive: the tariff structure, the countervailing excise, the stores purchase rule, the refusal to fund technical education, the absence of a central bank until 1935.
  • Utsa Patnaik has estimated the total drain 1765–1938 at roughly $45 trillion at compound interest. Disputed This figure is widely circulated and widely criticised — the compounding assumption and the counterfactual are contested. Quote it as “an estimate by Utsa Patnaik, using assumptions other economists dispute,” never as fact.

The revisionist counter-case

  • Tirthankar Roy and others argue Indian economic stagnation owed much to geography and ecology — monsoon dependence, low soil fertility, scarce water — and that colonial policy was more often indifferent than actively extractive.
  • The Home Charges, measured against national income, were on the order of 1–1.5 per cent of GDP annually — significant but arguably not sufficient alone to explain stagnation.
  • Some Indian sectors grew substantially under the Raj: Bombay and Ahmedabad cotton mills (Indian-owned, from 1854), Tata Iron and Steel (1907, Jamshedpur — at one point the largest steel plant in the British Empire), jute, coal, cement, tea.
  • Institutions with lasting value: uniform civil and criminal law, an all-India administration, the railway and telegraph network, modern universities, land survey and property records, the Census (from 1871–72) and the Archaeological Survey.
  • The honest counter to the counter: many of these benefits were by-products of imperial requirements rather than developmental intent; and the correct test is not “did anything improve?” but “did India do better or worse than comparable countries that were not colonised?” On that test — Japan is the standard comparison — India did substantially worse.
Interview edge — the balanced formulation

A safe, honest and impressive answer: “Sir, I would separate intent from effect. The effect on Indian manufacturing and on per-capita income was clearly negative — India went from roughly a quarter of world output to about four per cent. Some institutions the British built were genuinely valuable and we retained them. But the test I apply is opportunity cost: the revenue that went out as Home Charges and the tariff policy that protected Lancashire were resources not available for Indian industry or education. The Raj’s real failure is not that it took everything — it is that it invested almost nothing in Indian human capital, and that is why we started 1947 with 16 per cent literacy.” Specific, fair, and it ends on a number.

Famine, policy and demography

Between 1765 and 1947 India suffered a series of catastrophic famines. Drought caused crop failure; policy determined whether crop failure became mass death. This is the sharpest available illustration of the difference between misfortune and misgovernment.

Why policy mattered

  • Doctrinaire political economy. Officials trained in laissez-faire believed intervention in the grain market would discourage private trade and prolong scarcity. Lytton’s administration in 1877 actively prevented some local relief efforts and prohibited “interference” with grain prices.
  • The Temple wage. Sir Richard Temple’s 1877 relief ration of about 450 grams of grain a day for hard labour in a relief camp — less than the ration at Buchenwald, as later critics noted — was set deliberately low to deter applicants.
  • Railways cut both ways. They could move grain to deficit areas; they also enabled grain to be moved out of a famine district to a higher-paying market. Both happened.
  • Reform did come. The Famine Commissions — Strachey (1880), Lyall (1897), MacDonnell (1901) — produced the Famine Codes, systematising early warning, relief works and gratuitous relief. Famine mortality did decline in the early twentieth century. The record is one of catastrophic early failure followed by partial, bureaucratic improvement.

Bengal 1943 — the case to know in detail

It is worth knowing one famine thoroughly, and 1943 is the one most likely to be raised.

  • Context: Burma fell to Japan in 1942, cutting off about 15 per cent of Bengal’s rice imports. War inflation and military procurement raised prices sharply. A cyclone and flooding in October 1942 damaged the aman crop in coastal Bengal, followed by fungal disease.
  • The “denial policy.” Fearing a Japanese landing, authorities confiscated or destroyed surplus rice stocks and removed or destroyed tens of thousands of country boats in the coastal districts — devastating the fishing and transport economy of exactly the population that later starved.
  • Priority allocation. Calcutta’s war industries and the armed forces received guaranteed supply; rural Bengal did not. Inter-provincial trade barriers erected by other provinces stopped rice moving into Bengal.
  • Refusal of imports. The War Cabinet repeatedly declined or reduced shipping for food imports on the grounds of competing military demands. Disputed Churchill’s personal role is fiercely argued: Madhusree Mukerjee and others charge deliberate callousness and cite his recorded remarks; Arthur Herman and others argue shipping was genuinely scarce in 1943 and that Wavell, on becoming Viceroy in October 1943, did secure supplies and used the army to distribute them. The shipping constraint was real and the political indifference was real.
  • The analytical frame. Amartya Sen’s Poverty and Famines (1981) used Bengal 1943 to establish that famines are usually not caused by an absolute shortage of food but by a collapse in some groups’ “exchange entitlements” — their ability to command food through wages, sale of assets or production. Bengal’s 1943 food availability per head was not dramatically below 1941. Sen also argued that no substantial famine has occurred in a functioning democracy with a free press. That single sentence is the most powerful thing you can say about colonial famine, because it connects economics to political accountability — which is exactly the connection the Raj lacked.
Lecturette use

“Famine under colonial rule” is a strong three-minute lecturette because it has a clean structure: the event (one famine described in specifics), the cause (entitlement collapse, not absolute shortage), the policy failure (no accountability, no free press, doctrinal non-intervention), and the lesson (India has not had a famine since 1947 — democracy and the Public Distribution System are the reason). It ends on a constructive national note rather than a grievance, which is exactly the tone that works.

Railways, telegraph, canals: the double-edged infrastructure

The infrastructure question is a classic “two-sided” interview topic. Learn the facts and the argument on both sides; do not take a simple position.

  • 1853First passenger railway, Bori Bunder to Thane, 34 km, 16 April 1853 — Dalhousie’s minute of 1853 set out the network plan. First telegraph line, Calcutta–Diamond Harbour, the same year.
  • 1854Howrah–Hooghly line opens in Bengal; the Ganges Canal, then the largest irrigation work in the world, is inaugurated.
  • 1857The telegraph proves decisive in suppressing the revolt — Punjab was held partly because Lawrence had warning. A condemned rebel reportedly pointed at the telegraph wire and said, “that is the accursed string that strangles us.”
  • 1900About 39,000 km of track — the fourth-largest network in the world.
  • 1947About 65,000 km; roughly 40 per cent of it went to Pakistan or was cut by the new border, and several lines were severed mid-route.

What the infrastructure did for the Raj

  • Rapid movement of troops — the explicit primary justification after 1857.
  • Moved raw cotton, jute, wheat and oilseeds to ports for export, and British manufactures inland.
  • Routes ran port-ward, from the interior to Bombay, Calcutta, Madras and Karachi, rather than knitting Indian regions to one another — an extractive geometry.
  • Freight rates were structured to favour long-haul export traffic over short-haul internal trade.
  • Financed on guaranteed return, so the Indian taxpayer bore losses while British shareholders took profits until the system was progressively purchased by the state from 1869.

What it did for India — including unintended effects

  • Created a national market and, over time, damped regional price differences.
  • Made pilgrimage, migration and — crucially — political organisation across a subcontinent possible. The Congress could not have functioned as an all-India body without railways, telegraph and the postal service.
  • Gandhi’s mass movements travelled by third-class rail. His 1917 tour of India, undertaken on Gokhale’s advice, was a railway journey. The Raj built the nervous system of the movement that displaced it.
  • The third-class carriage itself became a political symbol — Gandhi travelled third class deliberately and wrote about its conditions.
  • Irrigation canals in Punjab and Sind genuinely raised output and created the canal colonies — with their own grievances, as the Punjab Colonisation Bill agitation of 1907 (in which Lala Lajpat Rai and Ajit Singh were deported) demonstrated.

The formulation to use: “The railways were built for imperial reasons with imperial finance, and their route structure shows it. But infrastructure, once built, does not obey the intention of its builder. The same network that moved troops in 1857 moved the Congress in 1920. That is worth remembering whenever we assess any technology — capability is neutral; the user decides.”

Society, religion and the reform movements

The nineteenth century produced an intellectual upheaval sometimes called the Indian Renaissance. It had three impulses that pulled in different directions: reform Indian society by Western standards; revive Indian society by returning to a purer past; and defend Indian society against both. The most sophisticated point in this section

Colonial rule created a trap that shaped Indian politics permanently. The British justified their presence partly by pointing at Indian social evils — sati, child marriage, untouchability. That made social reform look like collaboration, and defending custom look like patriotism. Many nationalists therefore postponed the social question to preserve political unity. Ambedkar’s lifelong charge against the Congress is precisely this: that it fought for the freedom of a nation without first securing the freedom of a fifth of its people. Tilak’s position in 1891 and Ambedkar’s position in 1932 are the two ends of the same argument, and it was never fully resolved before 1947.

Education, English and the press

The Raj’s education policy is the clearest example of a colonial measure producing the opposite of its intended effect. It was designed to produce clerks. It produced Gandhi, Nehru, Jinnah, Ambedkar, Bose, Tilak and Gokhale.

The policy sequence

  • Charter Act 1813 — Rs 1 lakh a year for “the revival and improvement of literature and the encouragement of the learned natives of India.” Almost nothing was spent for two decades while Orientalists and Anglicists argued.
  • Macaulay’s Minute on Education, 2 February 1835 — the Anglicist victory. Its notorious sentences: that “a single shelf of a good European library was worth the whole native literature of India and Arabia”, and that the aim was “to form a class who may be interpreters between us and the millions whom we govern; a class of persons, Indian in blood and colour, but English in taste, in opinions, in morals and in intellect.” Bentinck’s resolution of 7 March 1835 made English the medium of higher education and of official business.
  • Downward filtration theory — educate a thin elite and let knowledge trickle down. It never did; mass literacy was simply not funded.
  • Wood’s Despatch, 1854 — the “Magna Carta of English education in India.” Proposed a Department of Public Instruction in each province, a graded school system, universities at Calcutta, Bombay and Madras (all established 1857), grants-in-aid to private schools, vernacular primary education and teacher training. It abandoned pure filtration in principle, though funding remained thin.
  • Hunter Commission 1882 (primary and secondary education), Indian Universities Act 1904 (Curzon’s tightening of government control, deeply resented as a curb on nationalist influence in colleges), Sadler Commission 1917–19, Hartog Committee 1929, Wardha Scheme / Basic Education 1937 (Gandhi’s Nai Talim — education through productive craft in the mother tongue).

The unintended consequences

  1. A common language for a national elite. A Bengali, a Tamil and a Punjabi could now argue politics in the same language. Every early Congress session was conducted in English. This is an uncomfortable fact but a decisive one.
  2. Access to the vocabulary of liberty. Mill, Burke, Locke, Rousseau, Mazzini, Garibaldi, the American Declaration, the Irish Home Rule struggle, the French Revolution — Indians read all of it and then asked why the principles stopped at Suez. Nationalists turned Britain’s own liberal tradition into the indictment.
  3. An educated unemployed class. Graduates far outnumbered the government jobs available, and the good jobs were reserved for Europeans. Frustrated ambition is combustible.
  4. Vernacular revival. Print, not just English, transformed politics. Bengali, Marathi, Hindi, Urdu, Tamil and Telugu literature flowered; novels and newspapers created shared publics.

Interview edge

If asked whether English education was good or bad for India, the answer is not a verdict but a distinction: “It was introduced for a colonial purpose and it succeeded at that purpose — it produced the clerical class the Raj needed. But it also gave India a link language and access to liberal political thought, which the national movement used against the Raj. The real failure of colonial education is not English; it is that after 190 years, literacy at independence was about 16 per cent. Judge a system by what it did for the many, not the few.”

Part III

Resistance: from local revolt to national rupture

Early and tribal resistance, 1763–1856

British rule was resisted from the day it began. Between Buxar and 1857 there were, by one count, more than forty significant armed risings. They failed because they were local, leaderless beyond the district, unconnected to one another and usually backward-looking — but they mattered, because they produced a tradition of resistance and they show that “British rule was accepted until 1857” is simply false.

Why the Indigo Revolt deserves more attention than it gets

It shows every element of the later Gandhian method fifty years early: a specific, winnable economic demand; mass but disciplined refusal rather than armed attack; use of the colonial state’s own courts and laws; and — crucially — an alliance between the peasantry and the urban educated class through the press. When Gandhi went to Champaran in 1917 he was not inventing a technique; he was perfecting one that Bengal’s ryots had already demonstrated.

The Revolt of 1857

The largest anti-colonial uprising anywhere in the nineteenth century. It shook the Raj to its foundations, was suppressed with extraordinary brutality on both sides, and changed everything about how India was governed thereafter. What to call it is itself one of the most instructive debates in Indian history.

Causes — in four layers

Political

  • Doctrine of Lapse — Jhansi, Nagpur, Satara; Nana Saheb’s refused pension; the Mughal emperor told his successors must leave the Red Fort and drop the imperial title.
  • Annexation of Awadh, 1856 — a loyal ally deposed on grounds of misrule. The Awadh taluqdars lost roughly half their estates in the subsequent summary settlement, and Awadh supplied a very large proportion of the Bengal Army’s sepoys.
  • Systematic exclusion of Indians from higher office; the humiliating reduction of Indian princes to pensioners.

Economic

  • Heavy, inflexible land revenue; Inam Commission in Bombay (1852) resuming rent-free tenures; large-scale dispossession of old landed families.
  • Ruin of artisans and weavers by machine imports; decline of courts that had patronised craftsmen and musicians — annexation destroyed whole urban economies (Lucknow above all).

Military — the grievances of the sepoy

  • Wide disparity in pay and total exclusion from officer rank. The highest an Indian could rise was Subedar.
  • Loss of the bhatta (foreign service allowance) after the annexation of Sind and Punjab meant service there ceased to be “foreign”, cutting pay in real terms.
  • General Service Enlistment Act, 1856 — required all new recruits to serve overseas if ordered. For high-caste Hindus, crossing the kala pani meant loss of caste. It looked like a calculated attack on religion.
  • A ratio of roughly 1 European to 5 Indian soldiers — and many European regiments were absent in the Crimean and Persian wars.
  • Earlier warnings ignored: Vellore 1806; the 47th Native Infantry at Barrackpore 1824 (refusal to go to Burma by sea, ending in the regiment being fired on and disbanded); the 22nd NI 1849.

Social and religious

  • Reform legislation — sati abolition, widow remarriage, the Religious Disabilities Act 1850 allowing converts to inherit — was read as a state project of Christianisation.
  • Aggressive missionary activity after 1813, including preaching in jails and to sepoys by some officers.
  • Racial arrogance in daily life. Contemporary British writers themselves recorded it.

The immediate spark

The new Pattern 1853 Enfield rifle required a greased cartridge to be bitten open before loading. Rumour — and probably fact, at least initially — held that the grease contained cow and pig fat, polluting Hindus and Muslims simultaneously. Established The authorities admitted the danger and changed the drill and the grease, but by then the rumour had done its work. The famous circulation of chapatis from village to village in early 1857, never satisfactorily explained, signalled that something was expected.

Course of the revolt

  • 29 Mar 1857Mangal Pandey, 34th Bengal Native Infantry, attacks his officers at Barrackpore; hanged 8 April; his regiment disbanded.
  • 9 MayAt Meerut, 85 sowars of the 3rd Light Cavalry who refused the cartridges are court-martialled, sentenced to ten years and publicly shackled.
  • 10 MayMeerut rises. Prisoners freed, officers killed, the cantonment burned. The rebels march on Delhi that night — 60 km away, and unpursued.
  • 11 MayDelhi seized. The reluctant, elderly Bahadur Shah II “Zafar” is proclaimed Shahenshah-e-Hindustan, giving the revolt a symbol of legitimacy that all groups could accept.
  • May–JulRevolt spreads across the Gangetic plain and central India: Kanpur, Lucknow, Jhansi, Bareilly, Allahabad, Banaras, Faizabad, Gwalior’s contingent.
  • Jun–NovSiege of Lucknow — Henry Lawrence killed; the Residency holds out; relieved twice (Havelock/Outram in September, Campbell in November); finally retaken March 1858.
  • 27 JunSatichaura Ghat, Kanpur — the surrendered garrison is fired on while embarking. In July the surviving women and children are killed at Bibighar. These killings dominated British opinion and licensed reprisals of extreme savagery.
  • 14–21 SepDelhi recaptured after a bitter assault from the Ridge; John Nicholson killed. Bahadur Shah surrenders at Humayun’s Tomb; Major Hodson shoots his sons and grandson at what is now Khooni Darwaza. The emperor is tried and exiled to Rangoon, where he dies in 1862 — the end of the Mughal dynasty.
  • Mar–Jun 1858Central India campaign under Sir Hugh Rose. Jhansi stormed in April; Rani Lakshmibai, with Tantia Tope, seizes Gwalior in June and is killed in action at Kotah-ki-Serai on 17 June 1858. Rose called her “the bravest and best military leader of the rebels” — an assessment from the man who defeated her.
  • Jul 1858–Apr 1859Mopping up. Tantia Tope fights a guerrilla campaign across central India, is betrayed by Man Singh and hanged on 18 April 1859. Peace proclaimed 8 July 1858.

Why it failed — seven reasons

  1. Limited geographic spread. Punjab, Sind, Rajputana, most of Bengal, Bombay and Madras stayed quiet. Punjab, annexed only eight years earlier, actively supplied troops — Sikh soldiers had no loyalty to a Mughal restoration and had fought the Bengal Army in 1846 and 1849.
  2. No unified command or common aim. Some fought to restore the Mughal, some the Peshwa, some the Nawab of Awadh, some merely for their jagir. There was no shared vision of what would replace the British.
  3. The educated middle class and the merchants stayed out or supported the British. Men like Sir Syed Ahmad Khan and the Bengali intelligentsia saw the revolt as reactionary — an attempt to restore the old order they were trying to reform.
  4. Most princes remained loyal. Scindia, Holkar, the Nizam, the Sikh chiefs, Nepal (Jung Bahadur sent Gurkha troops). Canning later said the loyal princes “acted as breakwaters to the storm which would otherwise have swept over us in one great wave.”
  5. Material and technical inferiority. Rebels often had older muskets; the British had the Enfield, better artillery, and the telegraph.
  6. British reinforcement capacity. Troops were diverted from the Crimea, China and Persia. Imperial reach beat regional revolt.
  7. Leadership. The rebels had brave commanders; the British had a coordinated strategy and unity of command.

Consequences

  • Government of India Act 1858 — Company rule abolished; Secretary of State and Council of India; Governor-General also Viceroy.
  • Queen’s Proclamation, 1 November 1858, read at Allahabad by Canning: no further annexation; treaties with princes to be respected; the right to adopt recognised; religious non-interference; and a promise — repeatedly quoted back at the British for ninety years — that Indians would be admitted to office “freely and impartially… without distinction of race or creed.”
  • Army reorganisation (Peel Commission 1859): European-to-Indian ratio raised to about 1:2; artillery reserved for Europeans; “divide and counterpoise” recruitment; the martial-races policy; separation of Indian regiments by caste and region.
  • Alliance with conservative India — princes and landlords became the Raj’s social base, a decisive shift away from the reformist impulse of the 1830s–50s.
  • Racial hardening. The trust between British officers and Indian soldiers never returned. Social segregation — civil lines, clubs, hill stations — became systematic.
  • Financial burden. The cost of suppression, roughly £50 million, was charged to India. Indians paid for their own reconquest, and the debt service appeared in the Home Charges for decades.

What to call it — the naming debate

“Sepoy Mutiny”

The official British view. Evidence for it: it began in the army, over a military grievance; most rebel leaders were soldiers; there was no programme beyond restoring old rulers.

Against: it fails to explain the mass civilian participation in Awadh, the attacks on revenue records and moneylenders’ bonds across the Doab, or the involvement of peasants and taluqdars who had never held a musket for the Company.

“First War of Independence”

V.D. Savarkar’s 1909 book of that name, banned before publication, gave the phrase currency. Evidence for it: the scale, the cross-communal unity around Bahadur Shah, the appeal of the Azamgarh Proclamation to all classes, the explicit aim of expelling the British.

Against: R.C. Majumdar — a nationalist historian, which makes his view weighty — concluded it was “neither first, nor national, nor a war of independence.” There was no concept of a modern nation, no common political programme, and large parts of India took no part.

Interpretation The scholarly middle ground, associated with S.B. Chaudhuri and Eric Stokes, is that it was a “civil rebellion” or a series of regionally distinct revolts sharing a trigger: a military mutiny that detonated a much wider agrarian and political crisis, whose composition varied district by district depending on who had lost most under British settlement. Stokes’s work showed that the local pattern of revolt correlates closely with the local pattern of revenue over-assessment and land transfer — the sharpest empirical finding in the field.

Interview edge — how to answer “was 1857 the first war of independence?”

Do not pick a label and defend it. Say: “Sir, it depends on the test. If the test is scale and intent to expel the British, it qualifies — it was far more than a mutiny, and in Awadh it was a popular war. If the test is a national consciousness and a shared alternative political vision, it does not — there was no idea of India yet. What I would say is that 1857 was the last attempt of the old order and, at the same time, the event that made a new order necessary. Modern nationalism begins after it, and partly because of it.” That answer is accurate, balanced, and shows you know the historiography without name-dropping.

Lesser-known facts about 1857

  • The Bengal Army, where the revolt was concentrated, had a strikingly high proportion of Brahmins and Rajputs from Awadh and Bihar — a deliberate recruitment policy that created exactly the caste solidarity that made mutiny contagious.
  • Delhi during the revolt was governed by a Court of Administration of ten members — six from the army, four civilians — which issued orders, regulated prices and tried to restrain looting. It is the only functioning rebel government structure of the revolt.
  • The Azamgarh Proclamation (August 1857) appealed explicitly to zamindars, merchants, public servants, artisans and pandits and maulvis in turn, promising redress to each — the closest thing the revolt produced to a manifesto.
  • British reprisals included blowing prisoners from cannon, mass hangings along the Grand Trunk Road, and the sacking of Delhi with widespread expulsion of its Muslim population. Some British officers, including Canning — nicknamed “Clemency Canning” in derision — tried to restrain it.
  • Karl Marx wrote extensively on 1857 for the New-York Daily Tribune, calling it a national revolt and noting the irony that Britain’s own instrument — the sepoy army — had turned on it.
  • The last Mughal Emperor was a distinguished Urdu poet who wrote, in exile, “kitna hai badnaseeb Zafar, dafn ke liye / do gaz zameen bhi na mili, ku-e-yaar mein” — how unfortunate is Zafar, that for burial he could not get even two yards of earth in the land of the beloved. He is buried in Yangon.

Crown Rule, 1858–1905

The half-century after 1857 built the machinery of the modern Indian state and, simultaneously, the conditions for organised nationalism. The Raj became more efficient, more centralised, more racially exclusive — and far more resented by the class it had educated.

Local self-government

Lord Ripon (1880–84) is the one Viceroy with a genuinely liberal record: he repealed the Vernacular Press Act, passed the first Factory Act (1881), and issued the Resolution on Local Self-Government (1882) — introducing elected majorities and non-official chairmen in local boards, and calling it an instrument of “political and popular education.” He was right: municipal politics was where a generation of nationalists learned to organise, campaign and administer.

Curzon: the Viceroy who accelerated nationalism

Lord Curzon (1899–1905) was the most energetic and the most counter-productive Viceroy. His record is genuinely mixed, and that is exactly why he makes a good discussion topic.

Constructive

  • Founded the Archaeological Survey of India in its modern form and passed the Ancient Monuments Preservation Act 1904 — the Taj Mahal, Fatehpur Sikri and much else were restored on his orders.
  • Agricultural Research Institute at Pusa; Cooperative Credit Societies Act 1904; a Police Commission; Railway Board; famine relief and irrigation commissions; Punjab Land Alienation Act 1900.
  • Created the North-West Frontier Province (1901).

Destructive

  • Calcutta Corporation Act 1899 — reduced elected Indian members.
  • Indian Universities Act 1904 — tightened government control over universities and colleges; understood as an attack on the nurseries of nationalism.
  • Official Secrets Act amendment 1904 — widened the definition of sedition in relation to the press.
  • Partition of Bengal 1905 — the decision that ignited the first mass movement.
  • His contempt was explicit and public: he told a Calcutta University convocation in 1905 that “the highest ideal of truth is to a large extent a Western conception” — an insult that was reported across India and did him incalculable political damage.

Part IV

The rise of nationalism, 1870–1918

Roots of Indian nationalism

Nationalism did not arrive fully formed in 1885. It was assembled from about eight distinct sources over half a century. Being able to list them in order, with an example each, is worth more in an interview than any number of dates.

  1. Administrative and economic unification. One legal code, one currency, one bureaucracy, one railway and postal network. Paradoxically the Raj created the physical India that nationalism could imagine as a unit.
  2. Western education and political ideas. Liberalism, nationalism and representative government studied by Indians who then applied them to their own condition. The Italian Risorgimento (Mazzini, Garibaldi) and the Irish struggle were the favourite models.
  3. The press and vernacular literature. By 1900 there were hundreds of Indian-owned newspapers creating a genuinely national public opinion.
  4. The economic critique. Naoroji, Dutt and Ranade turned poverty from a fact of nature into a political charge.
  5. Racial humiliation. The Ilbert Bill, the Arms Act, separate railway carriages, exclusion from clubs, and the disparity in punishment for European crimes against Indians. Nothing radicalised the educated class faster.
  6. Cultural self-confidence. Orientalist scholarship (William Jones, Max Müller), the rediscovery of Ashoka and the Guptas, Vivekananda at Chicago, Tagore’s literature — these restored a sense that India had a past worth being sovereign for.
  7. Reform and revival movements, which built organisational habits — committees, subscriptions, journals, annual sessions — that transferred directly into politics.
  8. The failure of 1857, which demonstrated that armed regional revolt could not succeed and that a different method was needed.

The Indian National Congress

Founded on 28 December 1885 at the Gokuldas Tejpal Sanskrit College, Bombay, with 72 delegates and W.C. Bonnerjee presiding. Allan Octavian Hume, a retired ICS officer, was the moving spirit and first General Secretary. It was intended as an annual all-India forum; it became, over sixty years, the vehicle of Indian independence and then the governing party of a republic.

The “safety valve” question

Disputed The theory that Hume founded the Congress as a British-sponsored “safety valve” to drain off discontent rests mainly on William Wedderburn’s 1913 biography of Hume, which described Hume seeing secret reports of unrest, and on Lala Lajpat Rai’s subsequent use of the claim. Later nationalist and Marxist writers (including R.P. Dutt) adopted it.

Bipan Chandra’s influential rebuttal argues the reverse: that Indian leaders used Hume as a “lightning conductor” — an Englishman’s presence made it far harder for the government to suppress an infant organisation. He notes that the founders were experienced political organisers who had been building associations for a decade and hardly needed a retired official to give them the idea. Interpretation The safest formulation: “Hume’s motives were probably mixed — he genuinely believed a constitutional outlet was safer for the Raj than repression — but the Congress’s origins lie in fifteen years of Indian political organisation, not in a British plan.”

Phase one: the Moderates, 1885–1905

Who: Dadabhai Naoroji, Gopal Krishna Gokhale, Pherozeshah Mehta, Surendranath Banerjea, Badruddin Tyabji, Dinshaw Wacha, W.C. Bonnerjee, R.C. Dutt, Anandamohan Bose.

Method: the “3 P’s” — Prayer, Petition and Protest; constitutional agitation; annual sessions; memorials to Parliament; a British Committee of the Congress in London (1889) and its journal India; cultivating sympathetic MPs.

Demands: expansion of legislative councils and Indian representation; simultaneous ICS examinations in India and England and a higher age limit; separation of judiciary from executive; reduction of military expenditure; reduction of land revenue and salt tax; development of Indian industry; repeal of the Arms Act; freedom of speech and press.

What the Moderates achieved

  • Created the first all-India political organisation — an extraordinary feat of coordination across a subcontinent.
  • Built the economic critique that destroyed the moral case for the Raj.
  • Trained a generation in political method, finance, public speaking and committee work.
  • Established the principle that Indians had a right to a voice — and won the 1892 and, partly, the 1909 councils reforms.
  • Kept the movement legal and thus alive; an early armed movement would have been crushed in a season.

Their limits

  • Narrow social base: English-educated professionals, lawyers, journalists, teachers. Tilak’s jibe — that they were “political mendicants” — stuck.
  • Faith in British “sense of justice” proved misplaced; thirty years of petitions produced modest results.
  • No programme of mass mobilisation, and therefore no leverage.
  • Little engagement with peasants, workers, or the caste question.

Interpretation Judge them by context: in 1885 mass politics was neither possible nor safe. They did the necessary preliminary work. Gokhale — whom Gandhi called his political guru — represents the school at its best.

Facts worth carrying: Badruddin Tyabji was Congress President in 1887 — the third session — and George Yule the first European president in 1888. Annie Besant became the first woman president in 1917, and Sarojini Naidu the first Indian woman president in 1925. The Congress was never in principle a Hindu body, and its early Muslim participation is a useful corrective to later communal narratives.

Bengal, Swadeshi and the Surat Split

The years 1905–08 transformed Indian politics. A single administrative order produced the first mass agitation, the first economic boycott, the first systematic attempt at parallel institutions, and the first split in the Congress.

The Partition of Bengal

  • Announced 19 July 1905; effective 16 October 1905. Bengal Presidency — with Bihar and Orissa attached, about 78 million people — was divided into Eastern Bengal and Assam (capital Dacca, Muslim-majority) and the rest of Bengal (Bengali-speakers now a minority in their own province, outnumbered by Hindi and Odia speakers).
  • Official justification: administrative efficiency; the province was genuinely too large to govern.
  • Nationalist reading: Interpretation a deliberate blow at the centre of nationalist politics, splitting Bengali-speakers and setting Hindus against Muslims. The nationalist case is strengthened by the Home Secretary H.H. Risley’s minute of 6 December 1904: “Bengal united is a power; Bengal divided will pull in several different ways… one of our main objects is to split up and thereby weaken a solid body of opponents to our rule.” That is documentary evidence of political motive, and it is worth knowing verbatim.
  • Annulled at the Delhi Durbar, 12 December 1911, simultaneously with the announcement that the capital would move from Calcutta to Delhi. Bihar and Orissa were separated instead. Muslim opinion in eastern Bengal felt betrayed — a grievance the League used for decades.

The Swadeshi and Boycott Movement, 1905–08

  • 16 October 1905 observed as a day of mourning: hartal, fasting, bathing in the Ganga, and the tying of rakhi on each other’s wrists as a symbol of unity — Rabindranath Tagore’s idea. He also composed “Amar Sonar Bangla” for the movement; it is today the national anthem of Bangladesh.
  • Boycott of British goods: Manchester cloth, Liverpool salt, British-made sugar; bonfires of foreign cloth; picketing of shops; refusal by priests to officiate at ceremonies using foreign goods; washermen refusing to wash foreign clothes.
  • Swadeshi as construction, not just refusal: Bengal Chemicals (P.C. Ray, 1901/1893), Bengal National Bank, soap, match, tannery and textile ventures, Swadeshi Steam Navigation Company (V.O. Chidambaram Pillai, Tuticorin, 1906).
  • National education: the National Council of Education (15 August 1906) and Bengal National College with Aurobindo Ghosh as principal; the Bengal Technical Institute, which became Jadavpur University.
  • Culture as mobilisation: Vande Mataram became the movement’s anthem (and was banned); folk songs, jatra theatre, and the swadeshi song tradition carried politics to the illiterate.
  • Spread beyond Bengal: Tilak in Maharashtra, Lala Lajpat Rai and Ajit Singh in Punjab, Chidambaram Pillai and Subramania Siva in Madras, Bipin Chandra Pal everywhere as the great orator.
  • Repression: ban on public meetings, prosecution of editors, expulsion of students, the Barisal Conference dispersed by police (1906), deportation of Lajpat Rai and Ajit Singh (1907), and Tilak’s six-year transportation to Mandalay (1908).
Why Swadeshi matters more than its immediate result

The Partition was annulled, so the movement “won” — but the deeper significance is methodological. Swadeshi introduced the four techniques that Gandhi would later systematise: boycott of foreign goods, boycott of government institutions, construction of parallel national institutions, and mobilisation through cultural symbols. It also revealed the limits the Congress would struggle with for decades — the movement barely reached the Muslim peasantry of eastern Bengal, whose economic grievances were against Hindu zamindars, and it faded when repression rose and the middle-class students had to return to their examinations.

The Surat Split, December 1907

The Congress session at Surat broke up in disorder over the presidency (Rash Behari Ghosh, the Moderate candidate) and over whether the Calcutta 1906 resolutions on swaraj, boycott, swadeshi and national education would be retained. Chairs were thrown; the session was abandoned. The Moderates retained the organisation and amended the constitution to exclude the Extremists, who stayed out until the Lucknow session of 1916.

Interpretation The split was a gift to the government, which promptly offered the Moderates the Morley–Minto reforms of 1909 and simultaneously jailed the Extremists. The lesson — that a divided movement is easier to manage than a radical one — is the most quotable strategic point in this section, and it is directly transferable to any discussion of organisational unity.

The Muslim League and separate electorates

  • 1 October 1906 — the Simla Deputation, 35 Muslim notables led by the Aga Khan, ask Viceroy Minto for separate representation. Minto is sympathetic; his wife’s journal records the day as “nothing less than the pulling back of sixty-two millions of people from joining the ranks of the seditious opposition.”
  • 30 December 1906, Dacca — the All-India Muslim League is founded at the Muhammadan Educational Conference, with Nawab Salimullah of Dacca prominent. Its early aims were loyalty to the Crown, protection of Muslim political rights and prevention of hostility toward other communities.
  • 1909 — the Morley–Minto reforms grant separate electorates: Muslim voters electing Muslim members in Muslim-only constituencies, with weightage above population share in provinces where they were a minority.
  • Interpretation Separate electorates are the pivot of the communal question. Their defenders argued that a permanent minority needed guaranteed representation in a first-past-the-post system. Their critics — including the Congress and later most historians — argue that they made religion the basis of political identity, gave politicians an incentive to appeal only to their own community, and thereby created the constituency for Partition. Both points have force; the second is the stronger, and the structure was extended in 1919 and 1935 rather than dismantled.

The revolutionary movements

Running parallel to the Congress from 1897 to 1934 was a tradition of armed revolutionary nationalism. It never came close to overthrowing British rule, and it was ruthlessly suppressed. But it shaped the atmosphere, it produced the movement’s most enduring martyrs, and — in its later phase — it developed a sharper social and economic critique than the Congress possessed.

Second phase, 1922–1934

The suspension of Non-Cooperation in 1922 pushed a generation of disillusioned young activists back toward armed action — but with a new ideological content: socialism, republicanism, and an explicit critique of both British rule and Indian capitalism.

  • 9 Aug 1925Kakori train action — government treasury money looted from the 8-Down train near Lucknow. Ram Prasad Bismil, Ashfaqulla Khan, Roshan Singh and Rajendra Lahiri hanged (December 1927). What Bhagat Singh actually believed

He is the most quoted and least read figure of the movement. His writings, especially Why I am an Atheist (1930), the jail notebooks, and the 1929 statements, show a systematic thinker:

  • Revolution meant social transformation, not bombs. “By revolution we mean that the present order of things, which is based on manifest injustice, must change… the peasants have to liberate themselves not only from foreign yoke but also from the yoke of landlords and capitalists.”
  • He explicitly repudiated terrorism as a strategy in his later writings, arguing that individual action could only awaken, never liberate; mass organisation of workers and peasants was the only route.
  • He was an atheist and a socialist, deeply read in Marx, Lenin, Bakunin and the Irish and Russian revolutionary traditions, and he warned against communalism in explicit terms in his 1928 essay on the communal problem — arguing that religion in politics was the elite’s tool against class unity.
  • He was a student of strategy. Choosing an empty bench for the bomb, choosing arrest, choosing the courtroom as a stage — every element was designed for political communication, not physical damage.
Interview edge — “Gandhi or Bhagat Singh?”

This is a trap question, often asked to see if you can be provoked. The mature answer refuses the choice: “Sir, they had different theories of how power works. Gandhi believed British rule depended on Indian cooperation, so withdrawing cooperation would end it — which is essentially what happened. Bhagat Singh believed a subject people needed to prove it would fight, and that independence without social and economic change would only transfer power from white rulers to brown ones — a warning that turned out to be partly justified. Both critiques were correct about something. What I take from Bhagat Singh is that he chose to die for an idea at 23 and used his trial to teach; what I take from Gandhi is that he built an organisation that could act for thirty years without needing him in any one place.” Also worth knowing: Gandhi did raise Bhagat Singh's case with Irwin and sought commutation, while refusing to make it a condition of the pact — a fact that complicates both the accusation that he did nothing and the claim that he tried hard enough.

Assessment of the revolutionary tradition

Contribution

  • Destroyed the myth of British invincibility and the idea that Indians lacked courage.
  • Created a pantheon of martyrs whose emotional power sustained the movement, especially among the young.
  • Forced the pace: repression provoked by revolutionaries radicalised moderate opinion.
  • Introduced socialism and class analysis into Indian nationalism well before the Congress Socialist Party (1934).
  • Demonstrated genuine Hindu–Muslim and cross-caste solidarity in action — Bismil and Ashfaqulla, and the strongly mixed composition of the Ghadar movement.

Limits

  • Small, conspiratorial cells were easy to penetrate with informers, and every major action was followed by the destruction of the network.
  • No possibility of matching the military power of the state; no mass base to sustain losses.
  • Provoked repressive legislation that hurt the wider movement — the Rowlatt Act and the Bengal Ordinances were justified by reference to revolutionary violence.
  • Bhagat Singh himself concluded that the method could not deliver, which is the most authoritative criticism available.

World War I, the Lucknow Pact and Home Rule

The First World War is the hinge of the whole story. India entered it as a loyal contributor expecting reward and emerged radicalised, economically strained, politically united across communities for a brief moment, and furious at the reward it actually received.

India’s war

  • Established Roughly 1.3–1.5 million Indians served — about 800,000 combatants and the remainder in labour and support corps. Around 74,000 died and some 67,000 were wounded. Names are recorded on the India Gate memorial in Delhi (originally the All India War Memorial, completed 1931) and at Neuve-Chapelle.
  • Theatres: France and Flanders (the Indian Corps held the line at Neuve Chapelle and Ypres in 1914–15 — the first Indian troops to fight in Europe), Mesopotamia (the largest deployment; the disastrous siege of Kut-al-Amara 1915–16), Gallipoli, Egypt, Palestine (Haifa was taken by the Jodhpur and Mysore Lancers in 1918), East Africa.
  • Khudadad Khan, 129th Baluchis, became the first Indian awarded the Victoria Cross (Hollebeke, 31 October 1914). Eleven Indians won the VC in the war.
  • Financial contribution: India made an outright “gift” of £100 million to the British war effort in 1917 and continued to fund its own expanded army. Taxes rose, prices rose sharply, and the 1918–19 influenza pandemic then killed an estimated 12–18 million people in India — the heaviest toll of any country.

Why Indians supported the war

The Congress, including Gandhi, supported recruitment — Gandhi actively recruited in Kheda and was called the “recruiting sergeant.” The logic was explicit: loyalty in a crisis would make self-government undeniable afterwards. The model was the Dominions — Canada, Australia, South Africa — which had fought and gained autonomy. India was even a separate signatory to the Treaty of Versailles and a founder member of the League of Nations in 1919, despite not being self-governing — an anomaly nationalists used constantly.

The Lucknow Pact, December 1916

  • Two reunions in one session: the Moderates and Extremists reunited in the Congress, and the Congress and the Muslim League concluded a joint scheme of reform. Jinnah, then a Congress member and League president, was the chief architect — earning Sarojini Naidu’s description of him as “the ambassador of Hindu–Muslim unity.”
  • Joint demands: self-government; elected majorities in legislatures; half the Executive Council to be Indian; separation of executive and judiciary.
  • The price: the Congress accepted separate electorates and weightage for Muslims in minority provinces (in exchange for reduced representation in Bengal and Punjab, where Muslims were a majority).
  • Interpretation This is the most consequential trade in the whole story. The Congress bought unity in 1916 by conceding the principle of communal representation it had fought since 1909. Once conceded, the principle could not be withdrawn — it was reinforced in 1919 and 1935, and it structured the politics that ended in 1947. Whether a better bargain was available in 1916 is genuinely arguable; that the concession had long consequences is not.

The Home Rule Leagues, 1916

  • Tilak’s Indian Home Rule League — April 1916, Belgaum; active in Maharashtra (except Bombay city), Karnataka, Central Provinces and Berar. Six branches; demands included linguistic states and education in the vernacular.
  • Annie Besant’s All-India Home Rule League — September 1916, Madras; about 200 branches, loosely organised; papers New India and Commonweal. Her lieutenants included George Arundale, B.P. Wadia, C.P. Ramaswamy Aiyar, and among the young recruits, Jawaharlal Nehru.
  • Impact: the first sustained agitation between Swadeshi and Non-Cooperation; it built a permanent organisational network of branches, reading rooms and propaganda that Gandhi inherited. Besant’s internment in June 1917 produced a nationwide protest and made her Congress President in December 1917.
  • The agitation directly provoked the Montagu Declaration of 20 August 1917.

The Montagu Declaration and the 1919 Act

Secretary of State Edwin Montagu told the Commons on 20 August 1917 that British policy was “the increasing association of Indians in every branch of the administration and the gradual development of self-governing institutions with a view to the progressive realisation of responsible government in India as an integral part of the British Empire.” This was the first official use of “responsible government” — a large concession in principle, hedged by “gradual” and “progressive” in practice.

The sentence that explains 1919–20

India was asked to give men and money for a war fought, Britain said, for the rights of small nations and the principle of self-determination — a principle President Wilson proclaimed to the world in January 1918. India gave 1.3 million soldiers, £100 million and 74,000 dead. What it received in 1919 was dyarchy, and, in the same season, the Rowlatt Act extending wartime emergency detention into peacetime, and then Jallianwala Bagh. The gap between the promise and the delivery is the entire explanation for why a loyalist Congress became a mass resistance movement within eighteen months.

Part V

The Gandhian era, 1915–1939

Gandhi: the method and the apprenticeship

Gandhi’s significance is not that he was morally admirable — many were — but that he solved a political problem nobody else had solved: how to mobilise hundreds of millions of poor, largely illiterate, deeply divided people into sustained political action against a state that monopolised organised force. His answer was a technique, and it is worth studying as a technique.

South Africa, 1893–1914: the laboratory

  • Arrived in Natal in 1893 on a one-year legal contract; stayed 21 years. The ejection from a first-class carriage at Pietermaritzburg (June 1893) is the founding story he himself told.
  • 1894 — founded the Natal Indian Congress. 1904 — Phoenix Settlement; 1910 — Tolstoy Farm; the newspaper Indian Opinion (1903).
  • 1906 — the first satyagraha, against the Transvaal Asiatic Registration Act (“Black Act”): mass refusal to register and public burning of registration certificates. The word satyagraha — “holding firmly to truth” — was coined in a competition in Indian Opinion because he rejected “passive resistance” as suggesting weakness.
  • 1913 — the great march of miners and the campaign against the £3 tax and the Searle judgment invalidating non-Christian marriages; the Smuts–Gandhi agreement (1914) conceded significant points.
  • What he learned: that suffering publicly and without retaliation shifts opinion; that organisation, funds, a newspaper and discipline matter more than rhetoric; that mass action must have a single, concrete, morally unambiguous demand; and that he could work with Hindus, Muslims, Christians and Parsis in one campaign. He also formed his views on race and caste in this period — views that changed significantly over his life, and which are legitimately criticised today (see the Controversies section).

Return and reconnaissance, 1915–17

Gandhi landed in Bombay on 9 January 1915 (now observed as Pravasi Bharatiya Divas). Gokhale, his self-declared political guru, advised him to spend a year travelling India with his “ears open and his mouth shut.” He did exactly that, third class, and founded the Sabarmati (initially Kochrab) Ashram at Ahmedabad in May 1915.

The technique, analysed

  1. Choose an issue that is concrete, local, economic and morally unambiguous. Not “freedom” but indigo, revenue, salt. A peasant who will not march for an abstraction will march for a specific injustice he experiences.
  2. Investigate first. The 8,000 Champaran statements were a weapon — they made the facts undeniable and framed the campaign as law-abiding truth-telling rather than agitation.
  3. Accept the penalty openly. Breaking a law and then submitting to arrest distinguishes civil disobedience from criminality, and makes repression look like what it is.
  4. Insist on non-violence as discipline, not only ethics. Violence gave the state the justification it wanted and, crucially, excluded women, the old and the poor from participating. Non-violence is the only method in which a 60-year-old woman is as effective as a young man — which is why it produced mass participation on an unprecedented scale.
  5. Use symbols the illiterate can read. The spinning wheel, khadi, salt, the bonfire of foreign cloth, the fast. Each is a physical act that carries an argument.
  6. Constructive programme. Between campaigns: khadi, village industry, sanitation, basic education, removal of untouchability, Hindu–Muslim unity, prohibition. This kept the organisation alive and in contact with villages during periods when agitation was impossible — a point most candidates miss entirely and a very strong one to make.
  7. Know when to stop. The most criticised and most strategically serious element: he suspended movements (1922, 1934) when discipline broke, on the view that an uncontrolled movement would become a riot, would be crushed, and would destroy the organisation.
Why an officer should study Gandhi

Set aside the saintliness. Gandhi is a case study in leadership of a mass organisation under repression: clarity of aim, economy of means, discipline as the centre of gravity, symbol as communication, personal example as the only durable authority, and the willingness to take an unpopular decision — calling off a movement at its peak — because the long-term integrity of the force mattered more than the immediate objective. Whether you admire him or not, those are command problems, and he solved most of them.

Rowlatt, Jallianwala Bagh and Non-Cooperation

Between March 1919 and February 1922 the Indian national movement was transformed from a body of a few thousand educated men into a mass organisation with millions of members. Three events did it: a repressive law, a massacre, and an alliance.

The Rowlatt Act, March 1919

  • Formally the Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act 1919, based on the Rowlatt (Sedition) Committee report. It authorised trial of political offences by a three-judge panel without jury and without appeal, in-camera proceedings, acceptance of evidence not admissible under the Evidence Act, and detention without trial for up to two years.
  • Popularly summarised as “na dalil, na vakil, na appeal” — no argument, no lawyer, no appeal.
  • Every single elected Indian member of the Imperial Legislative Council voted against it and it was passed anyway by the official majority. Jinnah resigned his seat in protest, writing that a government that passed such a law “forfeits its claim to be called a civilised government.” That fact — total Indian opposition, passage regardless — destroyed whatever credibility the 1919 reforms had.
  • Gandhi called for a nationwide hartal on 6 April 1919, with fasting and prayer. It was his first all-India call, and the response was enormous.

Jallianwala Bagh, 13 April 1919

  • Context: Punjab was tense. On 9 April the popular leaders Dr Saifuddin Kitchlew and Dr Satyapal were arrested and deported from Amritsar; protests on 10 April were fired on; a mob then killed several Europeans and assaulted a missionary, Miss Sherwood. Brigadier-General R.E.H. Dyer took charge on 11 April; a proclamation banning gatherings was issued on 13 April but was not widely known.
  • 13 April was Baisakhi. Thousands — many pilgrims and villagers visiting the Golden Temple for the festival, unaware of the ban — gathered in the Jallianwala Bagh, a walled enclosure of about six or seven acres with a few narrow exits.
  • Dyer entered with 50 riflemen (25 Gurkhas, 25 Baluchi and Pathan troops) and two armoured cars, which he could not bring in because the entrance was too narrow. He gave no warning and no order to disperse. Firing continued for about ten minutes; roughly 1,650 rounds were expended, aimed where the crowd was densest and at the exits.
  • Disputed Casualties: the official Hunter Committee figure was 379 dead and about 1,200 wounded; the Indian National Congress inquiry (with Gandhi, Motilal Nehru, C.R. Das) estimated over 1,000 dead. The true figure is unknowable; give the range.
  • Afterwards: martial law in Punjab; the “crawling order” requiring Indians to crawl on their bellies along the street where Miss Sherwood was assaulted; public floggings; aerial machine-gunning of a crowd at Gujranwala.
  • The Hunter Committee (1920) censured Dyer; he was relieved of command and resigned. But the House of Lords passed a motion in his favour, and the Morning Post raised a public subscription of about £26,000 for him — a sum larger than many Indians could conceive. Established It was the British reaction, more than the massacre itself, that destroyed Indian faith in British justice. Dyer told the Hunter Committee, under questioning, that he fired to “produce a sufficient moral effect… throughout the Punjab,” and that he would have used the machine guns had he been able to bring them in.
  • Responses: Rabindranath Tagore renounced his knighthood on 30 May 1919 in a letter of extraordinary dignity. Gandhi returned his Kaiser-i-Hind medal. Sir Sankaran Nair resigned from the Viceroy’s Executive Council — the only member to do so. In 1940, Udham Singh, who had been present at the Bagh, shot dead Sir Michael O’Dwyer — the Lieutenant-Governor of Punjab who had approved Dyer’s action — at Caxton Hall, London; he was hanged on 31 July 1940.

Precision matters here. Dyer commanded at the Bagh; O’Dwyer was the Lieutenant-Governor who endorsed him. Candidates confuse the two names constantly, and getting it right signals that you have actually read about the event rather than absorbed it from a film.

The Khilafat movement

  • The issue: the Ottoman Sultan was also the Khalifa, spiritual head for many Sunni Muslims and custodian of the holy places. Britain had promised generous treatment of Turkey; the Treaty of Sèvres (August 1920) dismembered the Ottoman Empire instead.
  • Leaders: the Ali brothers — Maulana Mohammad Ali and Shaukat Ali — Maulana Abul Kalam Azad, Hakim Ajmal Khan, Hasrat Mohani, Dr M.A. Ansari. All-India Khilafat Committee (1919); Khilafat Conference, Delhi, November 1919.
  • Gandhi’s decision to make the Khilafat a Congress cause was the boldest political judgement of his career. It brought the Muslim masses into the national movement for the first and, as it turned out, the last time on that scale.
  • Interpretation The great debate. Supporters argue it produced the most genuine Hindu–Muslim unity of the entire struggle and that the opportunity had to be seized. Critics — including Jinnah at the time, and later historians — argue that (a) it legitimised the use of religious grievance as a mobilising principle, which could not afterwards be confined to one community; (b) it attached Indian politics to a pan-Islamic question of no Indian relevance; and (c) it collapsed humiliatingly when Mustafa Kemal Atatürk abolished the Caliphate himself in March 1924, leaving its Indian supporters stranded. Jinnah opposed Gandhi's programme at Nagpur in 1920 and left the Congress. Both readings have force; say so.

The Non-Cooperation Movement, 1920–22

  • Calcutta special session, September 1920 — the programme adopted, against resistance from C.R. Das, Annie Besant, Jinnah and Bipin Chandra Pal. Nagpur session, December 1920 — confirmed decisively, and the Congress constitution was overhauled.
  • The Nagpur reorganisation is under-appreciated and worth knowing. The Congress acquired: a Working Committee of 15 as an executive; the All India Congress Committee of 350; Provincial Congress Committees reorganised on linguistic lines — a decision with immense long-term consequences; village and district committees; and a membership fee of four annas to make it a mass organisation. It stopped being an annual conference and became a permanent political machine.
  • The programme — negative (non-cooperation): surrender of titles and honorary offices; boycott of law courts by lawyers, of government schools and colleges by students, of the 1920 council elections, of foreign cloth; refusal to serve in Mesopotamia; eventually refusal of taxes.
  • The programme — positive (constructive): national schools and colleges (Jamia Millia Islamia, Kashi Vidyapith, Gujarat Vidyapith, Bihar Vidyapith — all founded in this period), panchayats for arbitration, hand-spinning and khadi, removal of untouchability, Hindu–Muslim unity, temperance.
  • Gandhi’s promise: “Swaraj within one year.” A serious tactical error that he later acknowledged, because it set an unmeetable deadline.
  • Scale: Students left government institutions in tens of thousands; C.R. Das, Motilal Nehru, Rajendra Prasad, Vallabhbhai Patel, Asaf Ali and Saifuddin Kitchlew abandoned large legal practices; the Prince of Wales’s visit in November 1921 was met with a complete hartal in Bombay; foreign cloth imports fell by roughly half in value between 1920–21 and 1921–22. About 30,000 people were in jail by the end of 1921.
  • Local movements merged in: the Akali agitation in Punjab; the Eka movement and Baba Ramchandra’s Awadh Kisan Sabha in UP; the Alluri Sitarama Raju tribal rising in Andhra (1922–24); the Assam tea garden labourers’ exodus and the Chandpur firing (1921).

Chauri Chaura and withdrawal

  • 5 February 1922 — at Chauri Chaura in Gorakhpur district, a procession was fired on by police; the crowd turned on the police station, set it alight and 22 policemen (commonly given as 22, sometimes 23) were killed.
  • Gandhi undertook a five-day fast and, at the Bardoli meeting of the Congress Working Committee on 11–12 February 1922, had the movement suspended. He said he would not build a movement on “a foundation of violence.”
  • Reaction inside the Congress was furious. Motilal Nehru, C.R. Das and Lajpat Rai were in jail and deeply angry; Subhas Bose called it “a national calamity.” Jawaharlal Nehru wrote of his bewilderment at the movement being stopped “just when it seemed at its height.”
  • Gandhi was arrested on 10 March 1922, tried for sedition before Judge Broomfield at Ahmedabad on 18 March, pleaded guilty and invited the maximum penalty in a statement often called the Great Trial speech. Sentenced to six years; released in February 1924 after an appendix operation.

Was withdrawal a mistake?

  • Yes: the movement was at its peak; momentum was irrecoverable; it demoralised a generation and pushed many into revolutionary politics; a single incident in one village should not have halted an all-India movement; it gave the government breathing space to reorganise.

Or a sound judgement?

  • No: non-violence was the source of the movement’s moral advantage and the reason for its breadth; once violence began, the state could and would use overwhelming force, and the Congress had no answer to it; the organisation would have been destroyed and its leaders imprisoned for a decade; the movement’s real achievement was permanent organisational and psychological, not immediate, and that achievement survived because the Congress was not crushed.

Interpretation Judith Brown and Bipan Chandra broadly defend the decision as strategically sound within Gandhi’s theory of struggle — he explicitly worked on a “struggle–truce–struggle” model, in which periods of consolidation between campaigns are not retreats but phases. Shahid Amin’s Event, Metaphor, Memory examines Chauri Chaura from the villagers’ side and shows how differently the same event looked to the participants, who were then abandoned to the courts — 19 men were hanged. That is a genuinely uncomfortable point and worth acknowledging.

The 1920s: Swarajists, Simon and the road to Lahore

The years between Non-Cooperation and Civil Disobedience are usually skipped. They should not be: this is when the Congress learned council politics, when communal relations deteriorated sharply, when the demand shifted from dominion status to complete independence, and when the next generation — Nehru, Bose, Patel — took over.

Swarajists and No-changers

  • Swaraj Party, 1 January 1923 — C.R. Das (President) and Motilal Nehru (Secretary) argued for entering the councils to obstruct them from within — “to wreck the reforms from inside.”
  • No-changers — Patel, Rajendra Prasad, Rajagopalachari, Ansari — held that council entry would corrupt the movement and that constructive work in villages should continue.
  • The 1923 elections gave the Swarajists 42 of 101 elected seats in the Central Legislative Assembly and a majority in the Central Provinces; with independents they repeatedly defeated the government, throwing out budget demands and passing a resolution demanding a Round Table Conference and self-government.
  • Achievements: Vithalbhai Patel became the first Indian President (Speaker) of the Central Legislative Assembly in 1925; the Swarajists forced the appointment of the Skeen Committee (1925) on Indianisation of the officer corps and the Muddiman Committee (1924) on the working of dyarchy; they exposed the scandal of the Lee Commission; and they kept nationalism visible during a demoralised period.
  • Limits: obstruction has no positive programme; the party split over “responsive cooperation”; C.R. Das died in 1925 and Motilal’s leadership could not hold it together.

Communal deterioration

The mid-1920s saw a sharp rise in communal riots — Kohat (1924), Calcutta, Dacca, Lucknow. Shuddhi and sangathan movements on one side, tabligh and tanzim on the other. The Hindu Mahasabha was revitalised (Lajpat Rai, Madan Mohan Malaviya) and the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh was founded by K.B. Hedgewar at Nagpur in 1925. Gandhi undertook a 21-day fast for communal unity at Mohammad Ali’s house in Delhi in September 1924. Interpretation Most historians link the deterioration to the collapse of the Khilafat alliance, the competitive politics created by separate electorates, and the economic competition for scarce jobs and council seats — a combination of institutional design and material grievance rather than ancient hatred.

Simon, Nehru Report and Lahore

  • 8 Nov 1927Simon Commission announced — seven members, all British, no Indian, to review the 1919 Act. The insult was gratuitous and deliberate. Both Congress factions, the Muslim League majority under Jinnah, the Hindu Mahasabha and the Liberals boycotted it.
  • 3 Feb 1928The Commission lands at Bombay. Nationwide hartal; black flags; the slogan “Simon Go Back” — coined by Yusuf Meherally, who later also coined “Quit India.”
  • 30 Oct 1928At Lahore, Lala Lajpat Rai leads the demonstration against Simon and is beaten by police under Superintendent Scott. He says — in words that became a rallying cry — “every blow aimed at me is a nail in the coffin of the British Empire.” He dies on 17 November 1928. Govind Ballabh Pant and Jawaharlal Nehru were also injured in lathi-charges elsewhere.
  • Aug 1928Nehru Report — the all-parties committee under Motilal Nehru produced the first Indian-drafted constitution. Proposed dominion status, a federal structure with a strong centre, joint electorates with reservation of seats only for minorities in provinces where they were less than 10 per cent, a declaration of fundamental rights (19 of them), universal adult suffrage, responsible government, and the separation of religion and state. It was rejected by the League, the Sikh League and the Hindu Mahasabha — each for different reasons, which is the point: no formula satisfied everyone.
  • Mar 1929Jinnah’s Fourteen Points — his counter-proposal: a federal constitution with residuary powers to provinces, one-third Muslim representation at the centre, separate electorates, no territorial redistribution affecting Muslim majorities in Punjab, Bengal and NWFP, and a requirement that no communal bill pass if three-quarters of the affected community opposed it. Interpretation This is the point at which Jinnah’s path and the Congress’s diverge permanently; he left for London in 1930 and practised law there for several years.
  • Dec 1928Calcutta Congress — an ultimatum: accept dominion status within one year or the Congress will demand complete independence and launch civil disobedience. The younger leaders, Nehru and Bose, had pressed for immediate independence and accepted this compromise reluctantly.
  • 31 Oct 1929Irwin Declaration — the Viceroy states that dominion status is the “natural issue” of India’s constitutional progress and announces a Round Table Conference. It was immediately repudiated by the Conservative opposition in Britain, and Irwin could give no timetable. The Delhi Manifesto by Indian leaders sought guarantees; Irwin refused.
  • 19 Dec 1929Lahore Session, presided over by Jawaharlal Nehru (at 40, and largely on Gandhi’s insistence). The Congress adopts Purna Swaraj — complete independence — as its goal, authorises civil disobedience including non-payment of taxes, and withdraws from the legislatures. On the night of 31 December 1929 the tricolour is hoisted on the banks of the Ravi. 26 January 1930 is observed across India as the first Independence Day, with a public pledge read at meetings everywhere. That date was later chosen for the commencement of the Constitution in 1950 — which is why Republic Day is 26 January.

Civil Disobedience and the Round Table Conferences

The Salt March is the most studied political action of the twentieth century, and rightly. It is a case study in the selection of an objective: Gandhi found the one law that was simultaneously trivial, universal, economically real and morally indefensible.

Why salt

  • The salt tax gave the government a monopoly on manufacture and sale; making salt from seawater was illegal even for one’s own use. The tax was small in revenue terms (about 4 per cent) but fell on every single person in India, rich and poor, Hindu and Muslim, in every province, and hardest on the poorest, for whom it could be a significant share of income. Salt is also physiologically essential in a hot country.
  • It was therefore the perfect symbol: nobody needed it explained; defiance was cheap, safe to begin and infinitely replicable on any coastline; and no British official could argue with a straight face that taxing a man for boiling seawater was just.
  • Nehru’s initial reaction was puzzlement; the Congress Working Committee was unenthusiastic; the British press treated it as absurd. All of them misread it, and Gandhi’s judgement was vindicated within weeks.

The Dandi March

  • Eleven demands sent to Irwin on 30 January 1930 — including prohibition, halving land revenue, abolishing the salt tax and the CID, reducing military spending and the highest civil service salaries by half, releasing political prisoners, and a protective tariff on foreign cloth. Irwin refused to engage.
  • 12 March 1930 — Gandhi sets out from Sabarmati Ashram with 78 chosen satyagrahis for Dandi on the Gujarat coast — about 385 km (241 miles) in 24 days, through some 48 villages, speaking at every stop. The world’s press followed; it was one of the first global media events.
  • 6 April 1930, 6:30 a.m. — Gandhi picks up a lump of natural salt from the shore at Dandi and says, in effect, that with this he is shaking the foundations of the British Empire. Civil disobedience begins across India within days.

The movement, 1930–34

  • Geography: salt manufacture on the coasts of Gujarat, Bombay, Madras, Bengal, Orissa and Malabar (C. Rajagopalachari’s Vedaranyam march; K. Kelappan in Malabar); no-tax campaigns in Bardoli and Gujarat; no-revenue and no-chaukidari-tax campaigns in Bihar and Bengal; refusal to pay forest laws in Maharashtra, Karnataka and Central Provinces; the Kisan movement in UP refusing rent.
  • NWFP — the most remarkable theatre. Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan, the “Frontier Gandhi,” and his Khudai Khidmatgars (Servants of God), the “Red Shirts”, ran a disciplined non-violent movement among the Pathans. At Qissa Khwani Bazaar, Peshawar, on 23 April 1930 troops fired on an unarmed crowd, killing a large number. Two platoons of the Garhwal Rifles refused to fire on the crowd — a moment of extraordinary significance, punished with heavy sentences and remembered as the first serious sign of the army’s unreliability against non-violent Indians.
  • Sholapur — textile workers effectively took over the town for several days in May 1930; martial law followed.
  • Dharasana Salt Works, 21 May 1930 — Sarojini Naidu and Imam Sahib led about 2,500 volunteers who advanced in columns and were clubbed down without raising a hand. Webb Miller’s United Press dispatch was published in hundreds of newspapers worldwide and did more damage to Britain’s moral standing than any speech.
  • Women’s participation was unprecedented — Sarojini Naidu, Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay, Kasturba, Avantikabai Gokhale, Hansa Mehta, and tens of thousands of ordinary women picketing liquor and cloth shops. This is the campaign in which women entered Indian public life in numbers.
  • Repression: roughly 90,000 arrests; the Congress declared illegal; press ordinances; lathi charges and firings.

Gandhi–Irwin Pact and the Round Tables

  • Nov 1930 – Jan 1931First Round Table Conference, London. Congress absent (its leaders were in jail), so it had no representative weight. Significant outcome: the princes provisionally agreed to join an all-India federation, and Ambedkar and Sir Tej Bahadur Sapru made an impression. Ambedkar demanded separate electorates for the Depressed Classes.
  • 5 Mar 1931Gandhi–Irwin Pact (the “Delhi Pact”). Government: release political prisoners not convicted of violence; withdraw ordinances; restore confiscated property; permit peaceful picketing; allow salt manufacture for personal use in coastal villages. Congress: suspend civil disobedience and attend the Second Round Table. Interpretation Criticised then and since — Bose and the younger radicals thought Gandhi had conceded much for little, and the failure to secure commutation for Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev and Rajguru, hanged 23 March 1931, caused lasting bitterness. Defenders argue that the pact was the first time the British government negotiated with the Congress as an equal party rather than as a petitioner, which was itself a political victory of the first order.
  • Sep–Dec 1931Second Round Table Conference — Gandhi attends as the Congress’s sole representative, claiming to speak for all India. He stayed in the East End of London, visited Lancashire mill workers made unemployed by his own boycott (and was cheered by them), and met Charlie Chaplin. The conference deadlocked entirely on the communal question — the representation of Muslims, Sikhs, Christians, Anglo-Indians, Europeans and the Depressed Classes. Ramsay MacDonald announced he would decide the matter himself if Indians could not agree.
  • Jan 1932Gandhi returns; Willingdon has already begun a far harsher repression under the Emergency Powers Ordinances. Civil disobedience resumes and is crushed; about 120,000 arrests. Gandhi is arrested on 4 January 1932.
  • Nov–Dec 1932Third Round Table Conference — Congress absent, the Labour opposition absent; largely a drafting exercise that fed into the 1935 Act.
  • Apr–May 1934Civil disobedience formally withdrawn; Gandhi retires from active Congress membership (October 1934) to concentrate on the constructive programme, especially the All India Village Industries Association and untouchability.
What Civil Disobedience achieved — the honest balance

It did not win independence, and its formal gains were slight. What it did achieve: it made the Congress the only body that could credibly claim to speak for India; it internationalised the Indian question decisively; it drew women, peasants and the NWFP into the movement; it demonstrated that the government could jail a hundred thousand people and still not govern with consent; and it produced the negotiating framework — Congress as a party to be dealt with — that led directly to 1935, 1937 and eventually 1947. Movements are not judged only by whether the immediate demand is granted. They are judged by what capability they leave behind.

The Communal Award, the Poona Pact and the 1935 Act

The Communal Award and Poona Pact, 1932

  • 16 August 1932 — Ramsay MacDonald announces the Communal Award: separate electorates continued for Muslims, Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians and Europeans, and extended to the Depressed Classes, who would have both a separate electorate and the right to vote in the general constituencies — a “double vote.”
  • Gandhi began a fast unto death in Yeravda jail on 20 September 1932. His objection was not to representation for the Depressed Classes but to separating them electorally from the Hindu community, which he believed would make the removal of untouchability impossible and would permanently divide Hindu society. Ambedkar’s position was that only a separate electorate could give Dalits representatives genuinely accountable to them rather than chosen by caste-Hindu majorities.
  • Poona Pact, 24 September 1932 — the compromise. Separate electorates abandoned; instead reserved seats in joint electorates, raised from the Award’s 71 to 148 in provincial legislatures, plus 18 per cent of seats in the central legislature, with a system of primary election by Depressed Class voters of a panel of candidates (the panel provision lapsed after a few years).
  • Interpretation This is one of the hardest moral questions in modern Indian history and you should not pretend it is simple. Ambedkar signed under the pressure of Gandhi’s possible death, and later wrote with great bitterness that he had been coerced and that the Dalits had lost their political independence. Gandhi’s defenders argue that separate electorates would have converted an oppressed section of Hindu society into a permanent separate political category, as had happened with Muslims, with predictable results; and that the Pact delivered more than twice the reserved seats. Both men were acting on a genuine principle. The reservation system in the Indian Constitution descends directly from this pact.
  • Gandhi then launched a sustained anti-untouchability campaign: the Harijan Sevak Sangh (1932), the weekly Harijan (1933) and a nationwide Harijan tour (1933–34) of some 20,000 km. Ambedkar rejected the term “Harijan” as patronising. The Temple Entry Proclamation of Travancore (12 November 1936) was the most significant single breakthrough.

The Government of India Act, 1935

The longest Act ever passed by the British Parliament to that date — 321 sections and 10 schedules. It is the single most important constitutional document for independent India, because roughly 70 per cent of the Constitution of 1950 derives from it in structure and language.

The 1937 elections and the Congress ministries

  • Congress won outright majorities in 5 of 11 provinces — Madras, United Provinces, Central Provinces, Bihar and Orissa — and was the largest party in Bombay, Bengal, Assam and NWFP. It formed ministries in 8 provinces (adding Bombay, Assam and NWFP; later Sind briefly).
  • The Muslim League did badly: it won only about 4.8 per cent of the total Muslim vote and 109 of the 482 Muslim seats; it failed to form a government in any Muslim-majority province. Punjab went to the Unionist Party (Sikandar Hyat Khan), Bengal to the Krishak Praja Party (Fazlul Huq), Sind to local parties. Established In 1937 the League was not the voice of Indian Muslims. That it became so by 1946 is the central puzzle of the final decade.
  • Congress record in office (1937–39): release of political prisoners; repeal of some repressive laws; tenancy legislation in UP, Bihar and Bombay reducing rents and securing occupancy rights; the Wardha scheme of basic education; prohibition in some areas; encouragement of khadi and village industry; the National Planning Committee (1938) under Nehru — the origin of Indian economic planning. Also, some strikes were handled repressively and civil liberties were not always respected, which the Left criticised at the time.
  • The fatal miscalculation. In UP, the League expected a coalition; the Congress, having won a majority, offered ministerial places only on condition the League group dissolve itself into the Congress. Interpretation Many historians — including some sympathetic to the Congress — regard this as a serious error. It gave Jinnah his argument: that in a majority-rule system the Congress would take everything, and that Muslims needed not representation but parity or separation. The League’s Pirpur Report (1938) and Fazlul Huq’s pamphlet catalogued alleged Congress misrule — the charges were largely exaggerated and some were fabricated, but they were politically effective.
  • 22 December 1939 — after the Congress ministries resigned over the war declaration, Jinnah called for a “Day of Deliverance.”

Part VI

The final decade, 1939–1947

Communal politics and the idea of Partition

Partition was not inevitable and it was not ancient. As late as 1937 the Muslim League could not win a Muslim-majority province; as late as 1946 the Congress and the League agreed on a united India under the Cabinet Mission Plan. Understanding how the demand emerged and hardened in nine years is the most analytically demanding part of this subject — and the one where balance matters most.

The long-term institutional causes

  1. Separate electorates (1909, extended 1919 and 1935). The structural cause. Once a politician’s electorate is defined by religion, his incentive is to represent that religion against others; cross-communal coalition-building is electorally punished, not rewarded. This is a design problem, not a cultural one.
  2. The Census and the arithmetic of majority. From 1871 the colonial census enumerated and fixed religious categories. Once representation became numerical, demography became destiny, and communities began to see each other as competing blocs.
  3. Divide and rule. Interpretation The evidence for deliberate British encouragement is real but uneven — Risley’s 1904 minute, Minto’s reception of the Simla Deputation, and Olaf Caroe’s later manoeuvres are documented. But the claim that the British simply “created” communalism is too strong; they exploited, institutionalised and amplified divisions that had their own Indian political dynamics. Say “exploited and institutionalised,” not “invented.”
  4. Uneven modernisation. In Bengal and the UP, Hindus entered Western education and the professions earlier; in Punjab, Hindu moneylenders and Muslim peasant debtors; in Malabar, Hindu landlords and Muslim tenants. Economic conflict acquired a religious label.
  5. Religious idiom in nationalism. Tilak’s Ganapati festival, the cow-protection movement of the 1890s, Vande Mataram’s imagery drawn from a novel with an explicitly anti-Muslim frame, the Hindu revivalist tone of parts of the Extremist movement. And symmetrically, the Khilafat’s mobilisation of Islamic grievance. Interpretation Each made the other more plausible.
  6. Competing historical memory. Colonial historiography — James Mill’s division of Indian history into “Hindu”, “Muslim” and “British” periods — taught both communities to see the medieval past as one of conquest and subjugation rather than of layered coexistence.

The short-term political causes, 1937–46

  • 1937: the Congress’s refusal of coalition in UP; the League’s humiliation at the polls and Jinnah’s conclusion that constitutional bargaining within a united India was useless without an alternative threat.
  • Jinnah’s reinvention. The constitutionalist of 1916 became the mass leader of 1940. He built the League into a mass party in three years using precisely the techniques he had once disdained — the Muslim Students Federation, the National Guard, mass processions, and the slogan of Islam in danger. Interpretation Ayesha Jalal’s influential and contested thesis in The Sole Spokesman (1985) is that Jinnah used the Pakistan demand primarily as a bargaining counter to obtain parity for Muslims in a loose all-India federation, and that he did not want, and did not expect, the “moth-eaten” Pakistan he received. Critics respond that he pursued it with an intensity, and with consequences, that make the “bluff” reading hard to sustain after 1946.
  • The war gave the League leverage. With the Congress out of office from 1939 and in jail from 1942, the League cooperated with the war effort, took office in provinces, and grew unopposed. Established The three years the Congress leadership spent in prison after Quit India were the three years in which the League became a mass party. This is the most important single causal link between Quit India and Partition, and it is not much discussed.
  • British calculation. By 1942 the Cripps offer already contained the seed: the right of any province not to accede to the new Union. Once non-accession was conceded in principle, Pakistan existed as a constitutional possibility.

The Lahore Resolution, 23 March 1940

  • Moved by Fazlul Huq at the League’s Lahore session and drafted with Sikandar Hyat Khan’s involvement. Its key language: “the areas in which the Muslims are numerically in a majority as in the North-Western and Eastern zones of India should be grouped to constitute ‘Independent States’ in which the constituent units shall be autonomous and sovereign.”
  • Established The word “Pakistan” does not appear in the resolution, and the plural “States” left crucial ambiguity — one state or two? Sovereign, or autonomous within a federation? This ambiguity was politically useful and is the textual basis for the Jalal thesis. It was amended to the singular only at the Delhi convention of April 1946. The press immediately called it the “Pakistan Resolution”, and the name stuck.
  • Origins of the name and idea: the poet Muhammad Iqbal’s 1930 Allahabad address proposed a consolidated North-West Indian Muslim state within the Indian federation; Choudhry Rahmat Ali coined PAKSTAN in the Cambridge pamphlet Now or Never (1933) as an acronym of Punjab, Afghania, Kashmir, Sindh and Baluchistan — and was dismissed by League leaders at the time as a student crank.
  • The Two-Nation Theory as Jinnah stated it in his 1940 presidential address: Hindus and Muslims are “not religions in the strict sense of the word, but are, in fact, different and distinct social orders”, with different histories, heroes, laws and calendars, and “to yoke together two such nations under a single state… must lead to growing discontent.”

The opposition to Partition — from within Muslim politics

A point almost no candidate makes, and an extremely strong one, because it destroys the lazy equation of “Muslim” with “pro-Partition”:

  • Maulana Abul Kalam Azad — Congress President 1940–46, an Islamic scholar who argued from the Quran and Islamic history that composite nationalism was religiously legitimate and that Partition would leave Muslims weaker, divided into three fragments. His 1946 warnings about what Partition would do proved accurate.
  • Jamiat Ulema-i-Hind and Maulana Hussain Ahmad Madani of Deoband — argued for muttahida qaumiyat (composite nationalism), that nationhood is constituted by territory and not by religion. Iqbal attacked Madani in verse; Madani replied in detail. The theological argument against the Two-Nation Theory was made by orthodox Islamic scholars.
  • Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan and the Khudai Khidmatgars, who carried the NWFP for the Congress and who, when the 1947 referendum was imposed, boycotted it demanding the option of an independent “Pakhtunistan.” His words to the Congress leaders on accepting Partition — “you have thrown us to the wolves” — are among the saddest in the record.
  • The Momin Conference (representing Muslim weavers and artisans), the Ahrars, the Khaksars, and the Shia political conference all opposed the League at various points. The League’s claim to be the “sole spokesman” was a political achievement of the 1940s, not a fact of 1940.

World War II, Cripps and Quit India

The declaration and the resignations

  • 3 September 1939 — Viceroy Linlithgow declared India at war with Germany without consulting a single Indian leader or the Central Legislative Assembly. Technically constitutional; politically catastrophic. The eight Congress provincial ministries resigned in October–November 1939.
  • The Congress position was principled and awkward: it opposed fascism and sympathised with the Allies (it had sent a medical mission to China under Dr Dwarkanath Kotnis in 1938 and condemned the invasion of Abyssinia and Spain), but refused to fight for a democracy that would not grant democracy to India. Its offer: cooperation in exchange for a declaration of war aims including Indian independence, and a national government at the centre now.
  • August Offer, 8 August 1940 — Dominion status as the objective; expansion of the Viceroy’s Executive Council; a War Advisory Council; and, critically, an assurance that no future constitution would be adopted without the consent of minorities — effectively a veto handed to the League. Rejected by the Congress; Jinnah welcomed the veto.
  • Individual Satyagraha, from 17 October 1940 — a deliberately limited, symbolic anti-war protest. Selected individuals publicly spoke against the war and courted arrest. Vinoba Bhave was the first satyagrahi, Jawaharlal Nehru the second, Brahma Datt the third. About 25,000 were arrested. Gandhi’s aim was to register moral dissent without embarrassing Britain at a moment of extreme peril — an important point about his conduct that critics of Quit India often ignore.

The Cripps Mission, March 1942

  • Why it came: Japan had taken Malaya, Singapore (15 February 1942 — the largest capitulation in British military history, with about 85,000 troops surrendered, a large proportion Indian) and Rangoon. Japanese forces were on India’s border; Colombo and Indian coastal towns were bombed in April 1942. Britain needed Indian cooperation, and Roosevelt and Chiang Kai-shek were pressing Churchill hard to make a political offer.
  • The offer: Dominion status after the war; a constitution-making body elected by provincial legislatures plus princely nominees; a treaty to cover minorities; and the right of any province to decline to accede and to negotiate a separate constitution. In the meantime Britain retained full control of defence.
  • Why it failed: the Congress wanted an immediate national government and a real share in defence; Gandhi called it “a post-dated cheque on a crashing bank” (the phrase is usually rendered that way, though the exact wording is debated). The League rejected it because it did not concede Pakistan outright. Churchill had appointed Cripps partly to demonstrate to Washington that an offer had been made and refused — his instructions to Cripps left almost no negotiating room.
  • Established The non-accession clause is the point at which Pakistan became a British constitutional option. This single provision matters more than anything else in the mission.

The Quit India Movement, 1942

  • 8 August 1942 — the AICC at Gowalia Tank Maidan, Bombay, passes the Quit India Resolution. Gandhi’s speech that night contains the instruction: “Here is a mantra, a short one, that I give you. You may imprint it on your hearts… Do or Die. We shall either free India or die in the attempt.” He also said he was placing “an open rebellion” before them and asked every Indian to “consider himself a free man from this moment.”
  • 9 August 1942, before dawn — Operation Zero Hour: Gandhi, Nehru, Patel, Azad and the entire Working Committee were arrested and the Congress declared illegal. The movement was decapitated before it began — which is precisely why it became spontaneous and violent.
  • The three phases:
    • Urban, August 1942: hartals, strikes, student demonstrations, clashes with police, particularly in Bombay, Delhi, Ahmedabad, Patna, Banaras.
    • Rural, September–October: the most serious phase. Attacks on “the symbols of authority”: about 550 post offices, 250 railway stations, 150 police stations destroyed or damaged; telegraph wires cut on a huge scale; rail lines torn up; over 200 railway stations damaged. Parallel governments were established at Ballia (Chittu Pandey, August 1942), Tamluk in Midnapore (the Tamralipta Jatiya Sarkar, December 1942 to August 1944 — it ran relief during the 1943 famine and had a women’s wing, the Vidyut Vahini), and Satara (the Prati Sarkar of Y.B. Chavan and Nana Patil, which lasted until 1945 and ran courts and village administration).
    • Underground, 1943–44: Jayaprakash Narayan (who escaped from Hazaribagh jail on Diwali night 1942), Ram Manohar Lohia, Aruna Asaf Ali (who had hoisted the flag at Gowalia Tank on 9 August and remained underground for years), Sucheta Kripalani, Achyut Patwardhan, Biju Patnaik. Usha Mehta, aged 22, ran the Congress Radio on 42.34 metres from shifting locations in Bombay from 14 August until she was arrested on 12 November 1942 — she served four years.
  • Repression: firing on crowds, machine-gunning from aircraft in some districts, collective fines, public floggings. Disputed Official figures: about 1,060 killed and 3,200 wounded; Congress estimates put deaths at 7,000–10,000. Over 90,000 arrested. Kasturba Gandhi died in detention at the Aga Khan Palace on 22 February 1944, as did Gandhi’s secretary Mahadev Desai in August 1942.
  • Who stayed out: the Muslim League, the Hindu Mahasabha, the Communist Party of India (which after the German invasion of the USSR adopted a “People’s War” line and supported the war effort — a position that cost it heavily in nationalist credibility), the Unionists, the princes, and Ambedkar, who was in the Viceroy’s Executive Council.

The case that Quit India failed

  • Militarily and administratively suppressed within about six weeks in the main.
  • The British did not leave; the war effort was barely affected.
  • The entire leadership was imprisoned until 1945, ceding the political field to the League at the decisive moment.
  • It abandoned non-violence in practice, undermining the Congress’s central claim.
  • Launching it while a Japanese invasion threatened was, in the view of critics then and now, a serious misjudgement of timing.

The case that it succeeded

  • It demonstrated that the British could hold India only by force, and that the machinery of consent had gone. Linlithgow called it “by far the most serious rebellion since 1857.”
  • It required roughly 57 battalions of troops to suppress, at a moment when every battalion was needed elsewhere — a real strategic cost.
  • It settled the question of whether Britain would leave; only when and how remained. Every post-war British calculation assumed India could not be governed against the Congress.
  • It created the mass legitimacy and the cadre — Jayaprakash Narayan, Lohia, Aruna Asaf Ali — that shaped post-independence politics.
  • It internationalised the issue at the moment the Atlantic Charter had proclaimed self-determination, forcing Churchill into the embarrassing position of publicly exempting India from it.

Subhas Chandra Bose and the Indian National Army

For a defence aspirant this is the most important section in the handbook — and the one where you must be most careful to separate documented fact from legend. Bose’s military campaign failed; his political effect on the British decision to leave was arguably decisive. Both statements are true, and holding them together is the mark of a serious answer.

Before the INA

  • Born Cuttack, 23 January 1897. Placed fourth in the Indian Civil Service examination in London in 1920 — and resigned in April 1921 before confirmation, writing that he could not serve a government he intended to overthrow. Very few people in history have walked away from that.
  • Apprenticed under C.R. Das in Bengal; CEO of the Calcutta Corporation; imprisoned repeatedly; deported to Mandalay 1924–27.
  • General Secretary of the Congress; with Nehru led the Independence for India League (1928) pressing for complete independence against the dominion-status compromise.
  • Congress President at Haripura, 1938 — where he set up the National Planning Committee under Nehru. Re-elected at Tripuri, January 1939, defeating Gandhi’s candidate Pattabhi Sitaramayya by 1,580 votes to 1,377. Gandhi called the result “my defeat.” The Working Committee, all Gandhians, resigned; the Pant Resolution required the President to nominate the Working Committee in accordance with Gandhi’s wishes. Bose resigned the presidency on 29 April 1939 and founded the Forward Bloc on 3 May 1939. He was expelled from Congress office for three years in August 1939.

The escape and the German years

  • Arrested in July 1940 for the Holwell Monument agitation; began a hunger strike; released to house arrest at 38/2 Elgin Road, Calcutta, under surveillance.
  • Night of 16–17 January 1941 — escapes in disguise, driven by his nephew Sisir Bose, travels as “Mohammed Ziauddin” to Gomoh (now Netaji Subhas Chandra Bose Gomoh Junction), then via Peshawar and Kabul — helped by Bhagat Ram Talwar, code-named “Rahmat Khan” — as an Italian, “Orlando Mazzotta,” to Moscow and on to Berlin, April 1941.
  • In Germany: the Free India Centre, the Azad Hind Radio (from which he broadcast, and where the salutation “Jai Hind” and the title “Netaji” were adopted), and the Indian Legion (Legion Freies Indien, 3,000 men) recruited from Indian POWs captured in North Africa. He met Hitler in May 1942 and came away unimpressed and uneasy; he had publicly objected to passages about India in Mein Kampf.
  • February–May 1943 — transferred from Kiel to Sumatra by German U-boat U-180 and Japanese submarine I-29, with a mid-ocean transfer off Madagascar — one of only two recorded civilian transfers between submarines of two navies during the war.

The Indian National Army

  • First INA, 1942. Conceived by Major Mohan Singh of the 1/14th Punjab Regiment, captured in Malaya, with the encouragement of the Japanese intelligence officer Major Iwaichi Fujiwara (the F Kikan). Rash Behari Bose — the 1912 Delhi conspirator, long exiled in Japan — headed the Indian Independence League. By September 1942 the INA had about 40,000 men. Mohan Singh dissolved it in December 1942 after disputes with the Japanese over whether the INA was an allied national army or an auxiliary corps — an important and creditable stand.
  • Bose takes command, 4 July 1943, Singapore. He revives and reorganises the force; about 45,000–50,000 in total, drawn from Indian POWs and from the civilian Indian population of Malaya, Burma and Thailand, who also funded it substantially.
  • 21 October 1943 — proclamation of the Provisional Government of Free India (Arzi Hukumat-e-Azad Hind) in Singapore, with Bose as Head of State, Prime Minister and Minister for War. Recognised by nine governments: Germany, Japan, Italy (RSI), Croatia, Manchukuo, Nanjing China, Thailand, Burma and the Philippines. It declared war on Britain and the United States, issued its own currency and stamps, and administered the Andaman and Nicobar Islands (handed over by Japan in December 1943 and renamed Shaheed and Swaraj) — though in practice Japanese military administration continued there, which Bose protested.
  • Composition and ethos. Explicitly non-communal: mixed units, common messing across caste and religion, Jai Hind as the greeting, “Ittehad, Etmad, Qurbani” (Unity, Faith, Sacrifice) as the motto, and Hindustani in Roman script as the common language. Shah Nawaz Khan, Prem Sahgal, Gurbaksh Singh Dhillon, Habib ur Rahman, M.Z. Kiani, A.C. Chatterji — the leadership was deliberately mixed. “Subh Sukh Chain”, an Urdu-Hindustani adaptation of Tagore’s song, was its anthem.
  • The Rani of Jhansi Regiment — an all-woman combat unit under Captain (later Colonel) Lakshmi Swaminathan (Sahgal), formed in July 1943 with about 1,000 volunteers, mostly Tamil women from Malayan rubber estates. It was the first women’s combat regiment in Asia and one of the first in the world. Interpretation Its actual combat role was limited — it was mainly deployed in nursing and rearguard duties in Burma — but its existence was a radical social statement that mattered enormously.
  • Bose’s call: “Give me blood, and I will give you freedom” (Burma, 1944) and “Dilli Chalo.”

The campaign, 1944

  • Operation U-Go — the Japanese Fifteenth Army under Lt Gen Mutaguchi, with INA divisions attached, advanced into Manipur and Nagaland in March 1944. On 14 April 1944 Colonel Shaukat Malik’s unit raised the tricolour at Moirang in Manipur — the first Indian soil on which an Indian national army raised the flag. The INA also entered Kohima district.
  • Imphal and Kohima (March–July 1944) were catastrophic. The Japanese plan depended on capturing British supply dumps; Slim’s Fourteenth Army held the boxes, was supplied by air, and let the monsoon and Japanese logistics do the rest. Established Japanese losses were around 53,000 casualties including some 30,000 dead, mostly from starvation and disease; the INA lost heavily too. Kohima–Imphal was voted Britain’s greatest battle by the National Army Museum in 2013 — a useful fact, because it establishes the scale of what the INA was part of.
  • Why the INA failed militarily: no independent logistics, artillery or air support; total dependence on a Japanese command that treated it as auxiliary and often withheld supplies; troops ill-equipped and under-fed; monsoon; and the hard fact that the Indian Army units facing them — the very same regiments, sometimes the same battalions — fought extremely well for the British. This last point is uncomfortable and essential.
  • Retreat and surrender through Burma; Rangoon fell in May 1945; INA units surrendered with the Japanese.

The disappearance

  • Disputed The official account, supported by the Shah Nawaz Committee (1956) and the Khosla Commission (1970) and by the Japanese and British records, is that Bose died on 18 August 1945 of third-degree burns after a Japanese bomber crashed on take-off at Taihoku (Taipei), Formosa, and that his ashes are at the Renkoji temple in Tokyo.
  • The Mukherjee Commission (1999–2005) concluded that he did not die in the crash, partly because Taiwanese authorities stated no air crash occurred at Taipei on that date; the Government of India did not accept its findings.
  • Alternative theories — survival in the Soviet Union, or the “Gumnami Baba” of Faizabad — have no confirmed documentary basis. Files have been progressively declassified since 2015 without resolving the question to everyone’s satisfaction.
  • How to handle this in an interview: state the official finding, note that a later commission dissented and was not accepted, note that declassification has not settled it, and do not advocate a theory. If pressed for an opinion: “Sir, on the available evidence the air crash is the best-supported account, but I would not claim certainty where the government itself has had three inquiries. What is certain is what he did between 1943 and 1945, and that is what I would rather discuss.”

The INA trials — where the real victory was won

  • November 1945 — the British decided to hold public courts-martial at the Red Fort, of all places, beginning with a deliberately chosen trio: Colonel Prem Sahgal (Hindu), Colonel Gurbaksh Singh Dhillon (Sikh) and Major General Shah Nawaz Khan (Muslim), charged with waging war against the King-Emperor, murder and abetment of murder. The symbolism they intended — traitors tried at the seat of Mughal power — backfired completely.
  • The INA Defence Committee was assembled by the Congress: Bhulabhai Desai leading, with Jawaharlal Nehru (who donned his barrister’s gown for the first time in twenty-five years), Tej Bahadur Sapru, Asaf Ali, K.N. Katju, Kailash Nath Katju and others. The defence argued that the INA men were soldiers of a recognised provisional government entitled to belligerent status under international law, and that allegiance to a state one has repudiated cannot be treason.
  • The public response was unlike anything before it. Demonstrations, strikes and riots across India in November 1945 and February 1946 — Calcutta, Bombay, Madras, Karachi, Patna, Allahabad. The slogan on walls everywhere: “Lal Qile se aayi awaz — Sahgal, Dhillon, Shah Nawaz.” Crucially, the demonstrations were joined by Muslim League students alongside Congress and Communist ones — a rare and, for the British, alarming display of unity.
  • Outcome: all three were convicted and sentenced to transportation for life. Commander-in-Chief Claude Auchinleck remitted the sentences on 3 January 1946 — a decision he explained privately in a remarkable letter to his senior officers: that confirming the sentences would produce “a violent reaction” in the Indian Army, whose officers and men, he judged, were nearly all sympathetic to the INA men whatever they thought of their actions. Established This is documented British testimony that the loyalty of the Indian Army could no longer be relied upon against nationalist sentiment. It is the single strongest piece of evidence for the military-pressure explanation of British withdrawal.

The Royal Indian Navy mutiny, February 1946

  • 18 February 1946 — ratings of HMIS Talwar in Bombay strike over food, racial abuse and slow demobilisation, with B.C. Dutt (who had been arrested for painting “Quit India” on the ship) among the instigators. Within two days the strike spread to 78 ships and 20 shore establishments and about 20,000 ratings from Karachi to Calcutta, Madras, Cochin, Vizag and the Andamans.
  • The ratings hoisted the Congress, League and Communist flags together on the masts. There were sympathy strikes in the RIAF at Bombay, Poona, Calcutta and elsewhere, and among some army units at Jabalpur.
  • Massive civilian support in Bombay — a general strike, barricades, and roughly 200–300 civilians killed in firing over 21–22 February.
  • It was called off on 23 February on the advice of Sardar Patel and Jinnah, with an assurance against victimisation that was only partly honoured. Aruna Asaf Ali was among the few nationalist leaders who supported the ratings; the Congress leadership was deeply uneasy about an armed mutiny it did not control.
  • Interpretation The Congress’s decision is criticised as a lost opportunity and defended as a recognition that an uncontrolled armed revolt would have produced a bloodbath and might have wrecked the negotiations then under way. What is not disputed is the effect on London: the armed forces, the one instrument that made British rule possible, had visibly become unreliable in all three services within three months.
Interview edge — “Did Bose or Gandhi win independence?”

A very common question. The best answer is causal, not partisan: “Sir, I would say the Congress movement made India ungovernable by consent, and the INA episode made it ungovernable by force. Gandhi’s movements meant Britain could not rule with Indian cooperation; the INA trials and the naval mutiny meant it could not be sure of ruling with Indian bayonets either. Attlee’s government faced a bankrupt treasury, a demobilising army and an unreliable Indian one. Neither alone would have been enough. And there is one specific fact I find compelling — Auchinleck, the Commander-in-Chief, commuted the INA sentences precisely because he feared the reaction inside the Indian Army. That is the British side saying it themselves.”

One caution: the widely circulated “Attlee interview” with Justice P.B. Chakrabarty, in which Attlee allegedly rated Gandhi’s influence as “mi-ni-mal” and credited Bose, rests on a single second-hand account published decades later and is not corroborated by any contemporary record. Disputed Do not quote it as evidence. You do not need it — Auchinleck’s documented letter makes the same point far more reliably, and citing the better source is itself a mark of judgement.

1945–47: the endgame

Twenty-four months from the end of the war to the end of the Raj. The pace is the story: what had been debated for fifty years was settled in two years, under pressure of time that nobody could control and that made a negotiated, orderly settlement impossible.

Why Britain’s position collapsed

  • Bankruptcy. Britain ended the war with debts of about £3 billion abroad, of which roughly £1.3 billion was owed to India. It needed a US loan (the Anglo-American Loan of 1946, on hard terms) to survive. An empire that must borrow to eat cannot police a subcontinent.
  • A new government. The Labour landslide of July 1945 brought Clement Attlee to power. Labour had long been committed in principle to Indian self-government, and Attlee had served on the Simon Commission and knew the subject.
  • Military exhaustion and demobilisation. Britain could not find the troops. Wavell estimated in 1946 that holding India against a mass movement would need four or five divisions of British troops that simply did not exist, and that British soldiers who had fought a six-year war wanted to go home, not to Bihar.
  • Unreliable Indian forces — the INA trials, the RIN mutiny, the RIAF strikes, the Jabalpur signals unrest, and the police strike in Bihar and Delhi in 1946.
  • The international climate. The United States, the new United Nations (with its Charter language on self-determination), the Soviet Union and the Asian nationalist movements all made continued colonial rule costly. Roosevelt had pressed Churchill on India repeatedly.
  • The administrative machine was running down. ICS recruitment had virtually stopped during the war; British officers were ageing and leaving; the service was increasingly Indian and increasingly nationalist in sympathy.

The sequence

  • Jun–Jul 1945Wavell Plan and Simla Conference. Proposed a reconstructed Executive Council, entirely Indian except the Viceroy and C-in-C, with parity between caste Hindus and Muslims. Broke down when Jinnah insisted the League alone could nominate all Muslim members — including in place of Congress Muslims such as Azad. Wavell gave Jinnah an effective veto by not proceeding without him. Interpretation Many historians regard this as the moment the League’s claim to be the “sole spokesman” was conceded in practice.
  • Dec 1945 – Jan 1946Central and provincial elections. On a restricted franchise, the Congress won 91 per cent of the non-Muslim vote and 57 of 102 central seats; the League won 86.6 per cent of the Muslim vote and all 30 Muslim seats at the centre and 442 of 509 Muslim seats in the provinces. Established The 1946 election transformed the League’s claim from an assertion into an electoral fact. It is the decisive data point for anyone arguing that Partition reflected a real constituency. The counter-point, equally factual: the franchise covered only about 10–14 per cent of the population, weighted toward property.
  • Mar–Jun 1946Cabinet Mission — Pethick-Lawrence (Secretary of State), Stafford Cripps and A.V. Alexander. It explicitly rejected Pakistan, on the reasoning that a sovereign Pakistan containing large non-Muslim populations solved nothing, and that a “truncated” Pakistan without the non-Muslim-majority districts of Punjab and Bengal would not be viable. Its 16 May plan: a three-tier structure — a weak Union centre handling only defence, foreign affairs and communications; provinces grouped into Group A (Hindu-majority provinces), Group B (Punjab, NWFP, Sind) and Group C (Bengal and Assam); and full residuary powers with the provinces, with the right to reconsider grouping after ten years.
  • Jun–Jul 1946Both parties accepted the long-term plan — the closest India came to a united settlement. It unravelled over the interpretation of grouping: was it compulsory (the League’s reading, and the Mission’s) or optional (the Congress’s reading, since Assam and NWFP had Congress majorities and objected to being grouped)? On 10 July 1946 Nehru said at a Bombay press conference that the Congress would enter the Constituent Assembly “completely unfettered by agreements” and free to modify the plan. Disputed Whether this single remark destroyed the plan is one of the great arguments of Indian history. Azad wrote in India Wins Freedom that it was a catastrophic blunder. Others argue the plan was already unworkable and Jinnah was looking for an exit. Both views are held by serious historians. On 29 July 1946 the League withdrew its acceptance and called for Direct Action.
  • 16 Aug 1946Direct Action Day. In Calcutta, where the League ran the Bengal government under H.S. Suhrawardy and declared a public holiday, communal killing began and continued for four days — the Great Calcutta Killings. Disputed About 4,000 dead and 100,000 homeless, by most estimates. Violence then travelled: Noakhali (October 1946), Bihar (October–November 1946), Garhmukteshwar in UP, and Rawalpindi and the Punjab in March 1947. Gandhi walked barefoot through the villages of Noakhali for four months, and Mountbatten later called him a “one-man boundary force” for his effect in Calcutta in August 1947.
  • 2 Sep 1946Interim Government sworn in under Nehru as Vice-President of the Executive Council. The League joined only on 26 October 1946 — and explicitly to obstruct from within. Liaquat Ali Khan took Finance and used the portfolio to block Congress ministers’ proposals with a famously punitive budget. Patel said the League had entered “not to cooperate but to fight from within”, and the experience convinced him and Nehru that a united India with the League as a permanent veto-holder would be unworkable — a crucial step toward accepting Partition.
  • 20 Feb 1947Attlee’s statement: Britain would transfer power “by a date not later than June 1948”, and Mountbatten would replace Wavell. Interpretation Announcing a deadline without an agreed plan was intended to force the parties to settle. It also told every armed faction exactly how long it had to create facts on the ground — and the Punjab violence escalated from March 1947.
  • 24 Mar 1947Lord Louis Mountbatten sworn in as the last Viceroy (arrived 22 March), with a personal instruction to achieve a settlement and unprecedented plenary authority.
  • May–Jun 1947The “Plan Balkan” (Ismay Plan), which would have let provinces choose independence separately, was shown privately to Nehru at Simla; his furious reaction killed it. V.P. Menon redrafted it as the dominion-status plan.
  • 3 Jun 1947The Mountbatten Plan. Partition of India into two dominions; partition of Punjab and Bengal if their legislators so voted, by a procedure in which members from Muslim-majority and non-Muslim-majority districts voted separately; a referendum in NWFP and in Sylhet; a decision by the Sind Assembly and by the Baluchistan Shahi Jirga; a Boundary Commission; and the date advanced to 15 August 1947. Accepted by the Congress (with Gandhi deeply unhappy), the League and the Sikh leadership. Dominion status was the key device — it allowed immediate transfer under the existing constitutional machinery rather than waiting for a treaty.
  • 18 Jul 1947Indian Independence Act 1947 receives royal assent: two independent dominions from 15 August; the Constituent Assemblies become sovereign legislatures; British paramountcy over the princely states lapses; the office of Secretary of State for India abolished; the Governor-General’s powers to reserve bills ended; the Government of India Act 1935 to serve as the interim constitution of each dominion.
  • 14–15 Aug 1947Pakistan’s independence observed on 14 August (Mountbatten was in Karachi that day for Jinnah’s swearing-in, which is the practical reason for the different dates). At midnight on 14/15 August the Constituent Assembly of India meets; Nehru delivers the “Tryst with Destiny” speech; India becomes independent. Gandhi was not in Delhi — he was in Calcutta, fasting against communal violence.
Why the date moved from June 1948 to August 1947

Mountbatten advanced the date by ten months. His justification was that the situation was deteriorating faster than the timetable, that an announced early date would force decisions, and that a long lame-duck period would be exploited by every faction. Interpretation The counter-argument, made forcefully by Patel’s biographers, by Azad, and by historians including Stanley Wolpert (Shameful Flight), is that the acceleration made adequate preparation impossible: the boundary was not published until after independence, the Punjab Boundary Force of 50,000 was hopelessly inadequate for a province of 30 million, no population transfer was planned, and army division was carried out simultaneously with the crisis it was supposed to manage. This is the strongest single criticism of the British transfer of power, and it is one you can make confidently because it is a criticism of execution, not of intent.

Partition

Handle this section with care in any interview. It is emotionally charged, it involves communities represented in every unit of the armed forces, and the quality a board is looking for is not passion but sobriety. Give facts, give both sides’ suffering, and draw a forward-looking conclusion.

The Radcliffe Line

  • Sir Cyril Radcliffe, a distinguished English barrister who had never been to India and knew nothing of it — chosen precisely for that reason, as a guarantee of impartiality. He arrived on 8 July 1947 and had about five weeks.
  • Two Boundary Commissions (Punjab and Bengal), each with four Indian judges — two nominated by the Congress, two by the League — who deadlocked on every question, leaving Radcliffe as sole arbiter in practice.
  • Terms of reference: divide on the basis of “contiguous majority areas of Muslims and non-Muslims”, while also taking into account “other factors” — an undefined phrase that permitted, and required, discretion.
  • He worked from out-of-date maps and the 1941 census, without any survey on the ground. The award was completed on 12 August but not published until 17 August 1947 — two days after independence. Interpretation Mountbatten’s decision to delay publication is usually explained as an attempt to keep the celebrations untainted and to avoid Britain being held responsible for the violence. The cost was that millions did not know which country they were in on the day their country changed, and that administration and police could not be pre-positioned.
  • Radcliffe destroyed his papers, refused his fee of 40,000 rupees, and never spoke publicly about the work. Auden’s poem Partition is about him.
  • The most contested decisions: the award of the Gurdaspur district (Muslim-majority overall, but with a Hindu/Sikh-majority tehsil structure that made it arguable), which gave India its only road access to Jammu and Kashmir; the award of the Chittagong Hill Tracts (over 97 per cent non-Muslim, Buddhist and tribal) to Pakistan; and Ferozepur, where an earlier draft appears to have been changed. Disputed Allegations of Mountbatten’s influence on Gurdaspur have been made since 1947 and remain unproven; the district’s composition was genuinely ambiguous. State the controversy; do not assert the conspiracy.

The human catastrophe

  • Disputed Deaths: estimates range from 200,000 to 2 million. The commonly cited middle figure is around one million. There was no count; the range reflects genuinely different methods.
  • Disputed Displacement: commonly estimated at 12–18 million across the two new borders — the largest forced migration in recorded history. The 1951 censuses of India and Pakistan recorded roughly 7.2 million Muslims moving to Pakistan and 7.2 million Hindus and Sikhs moving to India in the west alone.
  • Abduction of women: the Inter-Dominion Treaty (1947) and the Abducted Persons (Recovery and Restoration) Act 1949 dealt with an estimated 50,000 Muslim women in India and 33,000 Hindu and Sikh women in Pakistan. The “recovery” operations often returned women, against their will and years later, to families that would not accept them. Urvashi Butalia’s The Other Side of Silence and Ritu Menon and Kamla Bhasin’s Borders and Boundaries recovered this history from oral testimony; it is the most disturbing and least-known dimension.
  • Punjab suffered worst — a province with the most intermixed population and the largest concentration of demobilised soldiers with military training on all three sides. Bengal saw less immediate slaughter but a longer, larger and continuing displacement over decades.
  • The Punjab Boundary Force — about 50,000 men under Major General T.W. Rees, itself communally mixed and therefore internally strained — was disbanded on 1 September 1947 as unworkable. It was never remotely adequate.
  • Delhi itself came close to collapse in September 1947; Nehru and Patel personally intervened in the streets, and the Emergency Committee effectively governed the capital for weeks.

Partition of the assets and the armed forces

  • Assets divided roughly 82.5 : 17.5 between India and Pakistan. Cash balances: India Rs 75 crore of the sterling balances held, with Pakistan to receive Rs 75 crore, of which Rs 20 crore was paid initially. The remaining Rs 55 crore was withheld during the Kashmir fighting and released in January 1948 after Gandhi’s fast — a decision that contributed to the hatred that led to his assassination on 30 January 1948.
  • The armed forces were divided on a rough 2:1 basis. The Armed Forces Reconstitution Committee under Auchinleck (as Supreme Commander) supervised it. Muslim personnel were given an option; units were split by company and squadron, equipment by inventory. Regiments with centuries of shared history were cut in half within weeks. The Punjab Regiment, the Baluch Regiment, the Frontier Force — officers who had trained together at Dehradun and Sandhurst and fought together in Africa and Italy found themselves on opposite sides within ten weeks, and in combat in Kashmir within eleven.
  • India received about 2 of every 3 troops, most of the ordnance factories (Pakistan received almost none) and most of the training establishments — the IMA at Dehradun stayed in India; Pakistan built Kakul.
  • Officer shortage: at independence India had very few senior Indian officers. General K.M. Cariappa became the first Indian Commander-in-Chief on 15 January 1949 (now Army Day). The Navy and Air Force had British chiefs until 1958 and 1954 respectively.
How to discuss Partition in an SSB

Three rules. First, never blame a community — blame the politics of the period and be precise about which decisions by which actors (British, Congress and League alike) contributed. Second, acknowledge suffering on both sides without competitive accounting. Third, end forward. Something like: “What I take from Partition professionally is that the armed forces of independent India were built deliberately as a non-communal institution — mixed units, common messing, regimental loyalty above identity — precisely because the generation that had seen 1947 knew what happens when an army divides along community lines. Keeping the services apolitical and secular is not just a value; it is a lesson learned at enormous cost.” That answer demonstrates historical knowledge, emotional maturity and professional understanding in one move.

Integration of the princely states

Often treated as a footnote; it was in fact the most dangerous problem of 1947 and the greatest administrative achievement of the founding generation. On 15 August 1947, roughly two-fifths of the subcontinent’s territory was legally free to choose independence.

The legal position

The Indian Independence Act provided that paramountcy lapsed and did not devolve on either dominion. Legally, 565 states (the conventional figure; the exact count varies by how minor estates are treated) could accede to India, accede to Pakistan, or remain independent. Mountbatten, at Patel’s urging, told the Chamber of Princes on 25 July 1947 that independence was an illusion — geography, economics and defence made it impossible — and urged accession before 15 August.

The instrument and the men

  • States Department created 5 July 1947, with Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel as Minister and V.P. Menon as Secretary. Patel’s appeal combined appeal to patriotism with an unmistakable warning, and he offered generous terms.
  • The Instrument of Accession ceded only three subjects — defence, external affairs and communications. Rulers retained internal autonomy initially. The Standstill Agreement continued existing arrangements while details were settled.
  • The privy purse, tax-free, pegged to the state’s former revenue, plus retention of titles, palaces and privileges. (Abolished by the 26th Amendment in 1971.)
  • Established All but three states acceded before or around 15 August 1947. Patel and Menon accomplished in six weeks what most observers expected to take years or a war.

Popular agitation and a provisional government (Arzi Hukumat) under Samaldas Gandhi; the Nawab fled to Karachi on 25 October; the Dewan (Sir Shah Nawaz Bhutto) invited India in; Indian forces entered on 9 November 1947. A plebiscite in February 1948 gave an overwhelming majority for India. The precedent India set here — that the wishes of the people should decide — is the exact principle it would later have to argue about in Kashmir, and vice versa.
Hyderabad The largest and richest state, area comparable to Italy, ruled by the Nizam Osman Ali Khan, Muslim, over a roughly 85 per cent Hindu population; landlocked in the centre of India. Sought independence; signed a Standstill Agreement (29 November 1947); meanwhile the Razakar militia under Kasim Razvi terrorised the countryside, and a Communist-led peasant insurgency in Telangana was already under way against the feudal order. Operation Polo, 13–18 September 1948 — a five-day “police action” under Major General J.N. Chaudhuri; the Hyderabad State Forces surrendered; the Nizam became Rajpramukh. Disputed The Sunderlal Committee report (1949, not published until 2013) estimated communal killings during and after the operation at between 27,000 and 40,000, with other estimates higher. This is a genuinely difficult part of the record and an honest candidate acknowledges it.
Jammu & Kashmir Hindu Maharaja Hari Singh, Muslim-majority population, bordering both dominions. He delayed, seeking independence, and signed a Standstill Agreement with Pakistan. On 22 October 1947, Pashtun tribal lashkars supported by Pakistani officers and logistics invaded via Muzaffarabad; Baramulla was sacked, which delayed their advance on Srinagar. Hari Singh signed the Instrument of Accession on 26 October 1947; V.P. Menon flew it back; Indian troops were airlifted to Srinagar on 27 October 1947 — 1 Sikh under Lt Col Dewan Ranjit Rai, who was killed within days. Major Somnath Sharma was killed at Badgam on 3 November 1947 and became independent India’s first Param Vir Chakra. The First Kashmir War ran to the ceasefire of 1 January 1949 after India referred the matter to the UN Security Council on 1 January 1948. The Ceasefire Line of the Karachi Agreement (July 1949) became, after 1972, the Line of Control.

Also worth knowing: Travancore’s Dewan Sir C.P. Ramaswami Aiyar declared independence in June 1947 and reversed course after an assassination attempt and Patel’s pressure. Bhopal’s Nawab held out until 1949. Sikkim became a protectorate in 1950 and joined the Union only in 1975. The French enclaves — Chandernagore (1950–52), Pondicherry, Karaikal, Mahe, Yanam (de facto 1954, treaty ratified 1962) — and the Portuguese possessions — Goa, Daman and Diu, taken in Operation Vijay, 17–19 December 1961 — completed the territorial consolidation. Dadra and Nagar Haveli was liberated by volunteers in 1954 and merged in 1961.

Patel and Menon — a leadership case study

Worth having ready as an example of leadership under time pressure. They faced 565 counterparties, no legal authority to compel any of them, a six-week deadline, and no force available. Their method: a minimal ask (three subjects only, not full merger), generous terms to the individual (privy purse, titles), a clear framing of the alternative (Mountbatten’s address to the princes), speed (settle the easy 90 per cent first and isolate the hard cases), and willingness to use force in the last resort but only after political options were exhausted. That is a textbook sequence — and the later merger into full union was achieved afterwards, once accession had removed the leverage. Note the sequencing: get the essential thing first, negotiate the rest later.

Part VII

Analysis, interpretation and argument

Why did the British leave in 1947?

This is the highest-value question in the whole subject, because there is no single answer and the board can see immediately whether you have thought about it. Six explanations compete. The right answer weighs them; it does not choose one.

Six explanations, with evidence for and against
ExplanationStrongest evidence forStrongest objection
1. The national movement made India ungovernable Three mass movements; 1946 elections; an administration that could no longer function without Congress cooperation; Linlithgow calling 1942 the most serious rebellion since 1857. The Raj survived and suppressed every one of those movements. In 1943 British power in India looked, on paper, secure.
2. The armed forces became unreliable INA trials and Auchinleck’s documented commutation reasoning; the RIN mutiny across 78 ships; RIAF strikes; the Garhwal Rifles at Peshawar in 1930; police strikes in Bihar and Delhi 1946. The Indian Army fought loyally and superbly through 1939–45, and units carried out Partition duties and the Kashmir operations without breaking. Unreliability was potential, not actual.
3. Britain was bankrupt and exhausted £1.3 billion owed to India; the 1946 American loan; demobilisation; rationing at home; the simultaneous withdrawal from Palestine and Greece; the decision to hand Greece and Turkey to the Americans in February 1947. Britain held on to other colonies for another two decades and fought expensive campaigns in Malaya and Kenya. Poverty alone does not explain a decision of this scale.
4. Labour’s ideological commitment Attlee’s personal knowledge and long-standing position; Cripps’s and Pethick-Lawrence’s records; the speed of the 20 February 1947 statement. Labour also fought colonial wars elsewhere. And Attlee’s government partly acted because it had no alternative, not purely from conviction.
5. International pressure and the new order American anti-colonial pressure from Roosevelt onward; the UN Charter; the Atlantic Charter embarrassment; Soviet propaganda advantage; the Asian precedent of Japanese victories in 1942 destroying the myth of white invincibility. The United States prioritised the Cold War over decolonisation from 1947 and did not push Britain hard in the final phase.
6. The “imperial calculus” / Cambridge School view Britain left when India ceased to be profitable and became a net liability; the empire was always a balance sheet, and by 1946 the balance had reversed. Treats Indians as objects of British calculation rather than agents — and the reason India became unprofitable and unmanageable was precisely the national movement. The explanation collapses back into explanation 1.
The synthesis worth memorising

“Sir, no single factor explains it. I would put it as a chain. The national movement over sixty years destroyed the legitimacy of British rule and made governing by consent impossible — that is the necessary condition. The Second World War then removed Britain’s capacity: it bankrupted the treasury, exhausted the army and brought in a government sympathetic to Indian self-government. And the INA trials and the naval mutiny raised the one question the Raj could never afford to have answered — whether Indian troops would fire on Indians in a nationalist cause. When a government cannot rule by consent, cannot afford to rule by force, and cannot be certain of its own instrument of force, it leaves. The movement created the first condition; the war created the second; 1945–46 created the third.”

The military dimension

The thread most relevant to a defence aspirant, and the one most often missed. The Indian Army is simultaneously the instrument of conquest, the agent of the 1857 rupture, the empire’s strategic reserve for a century, a school of Indian professionalism, and — in 1946 — the reason the British left.

The Indian Army as imperial strategic reserve

  • Established India was Britain’s barracks in the East. Between 1838 and 1920 Indian troops fought in China, Persia, Afghanistan, Abyssinia, Egypt, Sudan, Burma, Malaya, Tibet (Younghusband 1904), East and West Africa, Mesopotamia, Gallipoli, France and Palestine.
  • India paid. The cost of the Indian Army — routinely a quarter to a half of total Indian revenue — was borne by Indian taxpayers even when it was used for purely imperial purposes. This was one of the most effective nationalist grievances precisely because it was arithmetically undeniable.
  • Lord Salisbury’s remark that India was “an English barrack in the Oriental Seas from which we may draw any number of troops without paying for them” is the classic quotation. It is worth knowing because it comes from the imperial side.

Indianisation of the officer corps

  • Pre-1914No Indian could hold a King’s Commission. The highest rank available was Subedar-Major — a Viceroy’s Commissioned Officer, subordinate to the most junior British subaltern.
  • 1917Ten King’s Commissions granted to Indians as a war measure — the first, tiny breach.
  • 1918–19Vacancies reserved for Indians at Sandhurst — ten a year. Attrition was high; the social and financial barriers were severe.
  • 1922Prince of Wales Royal Indian Military College, Dehradun (now RIMC) opens as a feeder to Sandhurst.
  • 1923Eight-Unit Scheme — Indianisation confined to eight units so that British officers would not have to serve under Indians elsewhere. Deeply resented and deliberately segregating.
  • 1925–26Skeen Committee (the “Indian Sandhurst Committee”), chaired by General Andrew Skeen with Motilal Nehru, Jinnah and Sardar Jogendra Singh among its members, recommended an Indian military academy and a twenty-five-year programme of full Indianisation. The government accepted only part of it.
  • 1932Indian Military Academy, Dehradun opens on 1 October 1932 with 40 cadets. The “Pioneers” — the first course — included Smith Dun (later C-in-C of Burma), Mohammad Musa (later C-in-C of Pakistan) and Sam Manekshaw (later India’s first Field Marshal). Three future chiefs of three armies in one course of forty.
  • 1934The Indian Air Force is raised — No. 1 Squadron, with six officers including Subroto Mukerjee, later the first Indian Chief of Air Staff.
  • 1939–45Wartime expansion forces mass commissioning: from a few hundred Indian officers to roughly 8,000 by 1945 (Emergency Commissions). This is the generation that officered the Indian Army after 1947.
  • 1947–49General K.M. Cariappa becomes the first Indian Commander-in-Chief on 15 January 1949.

India in the Second World War

  • Established About 2.5 million Indians served — the largest volunteer army in history. There was no conscription. Roughly 87,000 died, and about 34,000 were wounded, with around 67,000 taken prisoner.
  • 31 Indians (and members of the pre-1947 Indian Army) were awarded the Victoria Cross in the Second World War.
  • Theatres: North Africa (4th Indian Division at Sidi Barrani, Keren and El Alamein — one of the outstanding formations of the war), East Africa, Italy (Monte Cassino — 4th and 8th Indian Divisions; the 10th Indian Division), Iraq, Iran, Syria, Malaya and Singapore, and above all Burma, where the Fourteenth Army under Slim was predominantly Indian and fought the longest campaign of the war.
  • Industrial and logistical contribution: India supplied ordnance, textiles, food, timber and steel to the entire Middle East and South-East Asia theatre, and hosted the massive logistical base for the Burma campaign and the China airlift.
  • Field Marshal Auchinleck’s judgement: that Britain “could not have come through both wars if they had not had the Indian Army.” A first-rate quotation, from the top of the British military establishment.

Military lessons for a candidate

  1. Logistics decides campaigns. Imphal–Kohima was lost by the Japanese because their plan required capturing enemy supplies. The INA failed for the same reason. Bose had soldiers and a cause and no supply chain.
  2. Loyalty is the foundation of military power, and it is political. 1857 and 1946 are the same lesson from opposite ends: when soldiers stop believing in the legitimacy of the authority they serve, no amount of equipment compensates.
  3. Organisation beats numbers. Plassey, Buxar, Assaye, and the whole conquest.
  4. Intelligence and subversion are cheaper than battle. Clive at Plassey; the informers who broke every revolutionary network; the betrayal of the Ghadar rising.
  5. Moral advantage is an operational factor. Dharasana in 1930 achieved more with 2,500 unarmed men than any armed action of the period, because the reporting of it changed opinion in Britain and America.
  6. An apolitical, non-communal army is a strategic asset, not a nicety. The founders of independent India built it that way because they had watched 1857’s caste-based Bengal Army, the 1930s communal recruitment, and 1947’s communal division of regiments.

Geopolitics and the wider world

India’s place in imperial strategy

  • India was the strategic centre of the British Empire, not a peripheral possession. Curzon: “As long as we rule India we are the greatest power in the world. If we lose it we shall drop straightaway to a third-rate power.”
  • The Great Game. Fear of Russian expansion toward Central Asia drove the Anglo-Afghan Wars (1839–42, 1878–80, 1919), the forward policy on the North-West Frontier, the Younghusband expedition to Tibet (1904), the Durand Line (1893), and the creation of the NWFP (1901). The security of India shaped British policy from the Bosphorus to the Bering Sea.
  • The sea lanes. The Cape route, then the Suez Canal from 1869, made Egypt, Aden, Ceylon, Singapore and the Persian Gulf strategically necessary because of India. Britain’s presence in the Gulf, and the Trucial States system, existed to protect the route to India — and was administered, for decades, from Bombay by the Government of India.
  • Burma, Malaya, Aden and the Gulf residencies were run as extensions of the Indian empire; Indian rupees circulated in the Gulf until 1959 (and the Gulf Rupee until 1966).

External influences on Indian nationalism

  • Italian unification — Mazzini and Garibaldi were the models for Tilak, Savarkar and the Bengal revolutionaries; Savarkar translated Mazzini’s autobiography.
  • The Irish struggle — Home Rule (hence the name of the 1916 Leagues), the Easter Rising (1916), Sinn Féin’s abstention from Westminster (a model for council boycott), and the Irish Free State’s dominion status (1921) as a constitutional precedent that Indian leaders studied closely. Annie Besant and Éamon de Valera were explicit reference points.
  • Japan’s defeat of Russia, 1905 — an enormous psychological event across Asia. Nehru wrote of the excitement it caused him as a schoolboy: an Asian power had defeated a European one.
  • The Russian Revolution, 1917 — brought socialism and anti-imperialism into Indian politics; M.N. Roy co-founded the Communist Party of Mexico and debated Lenin at the Second Comintern Congress (1920) on the colonial question; the CPI was founded at Tashkent in 1920 and in India in 1925.
  • The American Revolution and the French Revolution — the vocabulary of rights and the legitimacy of resistance.
  • Pan-Asianism and the Bandung tradition — the Brussels Congress of Oppressed Nationalities (1927), where Nehru represented the Congress and met anti-colonial leaders from Africa, Asia and Latin America; the League Against Imperialism; and the Asian Relations Conference, Delhi, March 1947, convened before independence.
  • The Japanese conquests of 1941–42 — the fall of Singapore destroyed the psychological premise of European invincibility in Asia permanently. Interpretation Many historians treat this as one of the underrated causes of rapid post-war decolonisation across the region.

India’s influence on the world

  • The method travelled. Satyagraha influenced Martin Luther King Jr (who visited India in 1959), Nelson Mandela and the ANC in its early phase, Kwame Nkrumah’s “positive action” in Ghana, and the anti-apartheid, Polish Solidarity and American civil rights movements.
  • India’s independence was the hinge of decolonisation. Ceylon and Burma followed in 1948; Britain’s African withdrawal began in earnest with Ghana in 1957. Once the largest and most valuable colony went, the logic of empire unravelled.
  • Indians abroad as nationalists: the Ghadar movement in North America, India House in London, the Berlin Committee, the Indian Independence League in South-East Asia, and Gandhi’s own South African apprenticeship. This was a globally distributed movement, not a purely domestic one.
  • Non-alignment grew directly out of the anti-colonial experience and became India’s principal contribution to Cold War international politics.

Women, Dalits, workers, peasants and capital

The freedom struggle is usually told as a story of leaders. It was in fact a coalition of social groups with different and sometimes conflicting interests, held together with considerable skill. Knowing the composition is what separates a textbook answer from an analytical one.

Women

  • Before 1920: largely individual — Rani Lakshmibai, Begum Hazrat Mahal, Bhikaji Cama, Annie Besant, the Bengali revolutionary women.
  • 1930 is the turning point. Civil Disobedience brought women into the streets in tens of thousands: picketing liquor and foreign-cloth shops, making salt, courting arrest. Gandhi’s method — which required endurance rather than physical force, and which treated the household as a site of politics through khadi and boycott — made mass female participation possible in a way no armed movement could.
  • Organisations: Women’s Indian Association (1917, Margaret Cousins, Annie Besant, Dorothy Jinarajadasa), National Council of Women in India (1925), All India Women’s Conference (1927, with Margaret Cousins, Rani Lakshmibai Rajwade, Muthulakshmi Reddy), Rashtriya Stree Sangha.
  • Names to know: Sarojini Naidu, Kasturba Gandhi, Kamala Nehru, Vijaya Lakshmi Pandit (later the first woman President of the UN General Assembly), Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay (who insisted women be included in the Dandi campaign, against Gandhi’s initial reluctance), Aruna Asaf Ali, Sucheta Kripalani, Usha Mehta, Durgabai Deshmukh, Hansa Mehta (who argued successfully for “all human beings” rather than “all men” in the Universal Declaration of Human Rights), Rajkumari Amrit Kaur, Begum Hazrat Mahal, Bi Amma (Abadi Bano Begum, the Ali brothers’ mother, who addressed political meetings in purdah), Matangini Hazra (shot carrying the flag at Tamluk in 1942, aged 73), Rani Gaidinliu (Naga leader imprisoned from age 16 to 32), Lakshmi Sahgal of the INA.
  • Suffrage: Indian women received the vote on the same property terms as men province by province from 1921 (Madras and Bombay first), and universal adult franchise came with the Constitution in 1950 — without the decades-long separate battle fought in Britain and America, because it arrived with independence.

Dalits and the caste question

  • Ambedkar’s position: the British were not the only, and not the worst, oppressor for a Dalit. Swaraj without the annihilation of caste would mean rule by the same caste elite. His demands were for separate political identity, representation, education and legal rights, not deferred social reform.
  • Milestones: Mahad Satyagraha (March 1927) asserting the right to drink from the Chavdar tank, and the public burning of the Manusmriti (December 1927); Kalaram Temple entry, Nashik (1930); Round Table Conferences; Communal Award and Poona Pact (1932); Annihilation of Caste (1936 — the undelivered address); Independent Labour Party (1936) and the Scheduled Castes Federation (1942); Labour Member of the Viceroy’s Executive Council (1942–46).
  • Other streams: the Self-Respect Movement in Tamil Nadu; the Adi-Dharm movement in Punjab; the Namashudra movement in Bengal; Sree Narayana Guru’s SNDP in Kerala; the Satnami movement in Chhattisgarh; the Mahar and Nadar mobilisations.
  • The honest assessment: the Congress under Gandhi made untouchability a central concern and campaigned against it seriously from 1932 — but as a reform to be granted by upper-caste conscience, not a right to be claimed by political power. Ambedkar’s critique that this was paternalism has force. The Constitution of 1950, which he chiefly drafted, settled the argument in his favour: Article 17 abolishes untouchability outright.

Workers and peasants

  • Trade unionism: the Bombay Millhands Association (1890, N.M. Lokhande); the All India Trade Union Congress (1920), whose first president was Lala Lajpat Rai; the great Bombay textile strikes (1928–29 — the Girni Kamgar Union); the Meerut Conspiracy Case (1929–33) in which 33 trade unionists and communists were tried for four years, giving the Left a national platform.
  • Peasant movements: the Awadh Kisan Sabha and the Eka movement (1920–22); the Bardoli Satyagraha (1928) against a 22 per cent revenue increase, which won and made Patel the Sardar; the All India Kisan Sabha (1936, Swami Sahajanand Saraswati, N.G. Ranga); the Moplah rising (1921); the Tebhaga movement (1946–47) in Bengal for two-thirds of the crop to the sharecropper; the Telangana armed struggle (1946–51) against the Nizam’s feudal order; the Warli revolt in Maharashtra (1945).
  • Interpretation The Congress leadership under Gandhi consistently sought to keep class conflict subordinate to national unity, urging trusteeship rather than expropriation. The Left argued that this blunted the movement’s radicalism and left agrarian structures intact. The post-1947 record on land reform — uneven, and largely unsuccessful outside Kerala, West Bengal and parts of Jammu and Kashmir — gives that critique retrospective weight.

Indian business

  • Indian capital had a material interest in swadeshi (protection from British competition), in tariff autonomy, and in a government that would not privilege Lancashire. G.D. Birla, Jamnalal Bajaj (Gandhi’s “fifth son” and Congress treasurer), Ambalal Sarabhai, Walchand Hirachand and the Tatas funded the Congress substantially.
  • FICCI (1927) became the organised voice of Indian business and broadly supported the national movement.
  • The Bombay Plan (1944) — a development blueprint signed by eight leading industrialists including J.R.D. Tata, G.D. Birla, Lala Shri Ram and Kasturbhai Lalbhai — called for a state-led, planned, heavily industrialising economy. That India’s biggest capitalists themselves proposed state planning is a fact that surprises most people and is excellent material for a group discussion on economic policy.
  • Interpretation Marxist historians (R.P. Dutt, and in a different register Sumit Sarkar) argue that business funding gave capital a moderating influence over Congress strategy — visible, they say, in the hurried settlements of 1922 and 1931. Others argue the causation is overstated and that business followed the movement rather than steering it.

Competing interpretations

Knowing that historians disagree, and why, is the single most impressive thing you can demonstrate on this topic. You do not need to have read these books. You need to know the five positions and be able to say which parts of each you find persuasive.

How to deploy this without showing off

Never list the schools. Use one, once, at the right moment: “There is a view — the Cambridge School — that nationalism was mainly elite competition for office. I think that explains some provincial politics but it cannot explain why a peasant in Midnapore who had no prospect of office went to jail in 1942. So I would say elite interest was one motor, but not the only one.” One sentence of historiography, immediately grounded in a concrete counter-example. That is the right dose.

Controversies: handling the hard questions

These questions get asked, sometimes deliberately to provoke. In each case the formula is the same: state the charge fairly, state the defence fairly, give your view with a reason, and keep your tone level. A board is assessing your temperament as much as your knowledge.

1. Did Gandhi fail Bhagat Singh?

The charge: Gandhi could have made commutation a condition of the Gandhi–Irwin Pact and did not.
The record: Gandhi raised the case with Irwin on several occasions, including on 19 March 1931, and asked for commutation on grounds of public feeling. He declined to make it a precondition of the pact, on the stated ground that he would not use a political negotiation to bargain over a judicial sentence, and because he disagreed with the killing of Saunders. Your line: “He tried, but not to the point of risking the pact. Whether that was principled restraint or a failure of nerve is a fair argument. What is unfair is the claim that he did nothing, or that he wanted Bhagat Singh dead — there is no evidence for that.”

2. Was Gandhi responsible for Partition?

The charge: the Khilafat alliance legitimised religion in politics; he failed to meet Jinnah’s demand for parity in the 1920s and 1930s; he accepted Partition in the end.
The record: He opposed Partition until the last and proposed, in April 1947, that Jinnah be invited to head the government of a united India — a proposal the Congress leadership rejected as unworkable. He was not present at the independence celebrations. He fasted in Calcutta and walked Noakhali. Your line: “Partition had institutional causes going back to 1909 and political causes in the 1937–46 period. Gandhi made mistakes — the Khilafat alliance is genuinely arguable — but by 1947 the decision was made by the Congress leadership and the League, and he opposed it.”

3. Gandhi and caste — and his early views on race

The charge: he defended varnashrama for much of his life, opposed separate electorates for Dalits, used the patronising term “Harijan”, and his South African writings of the 1890s and 1900s contain remarks about Africans that are plainly racist by any standard.
The record: All of this is documented; his collected works are public. His views changed substantially over fifty years — by the 1940s he was arguing against caste restrictions on marriage and dining and refusing to attend weddings that were not inter-caste. Your line: “I think the honest position is that he was a man who changed enormously over his life, that his early views on race in South Africa were indefensible, and that Ambedkar’s criticism on caste had real force. Treating him as a saint makes it impossible to learn anything from him. His method, and his willingness to be wrong in public and change, are what I take from him.” This kind of answer — admitting the flaw without abandoning the assessment — is exactly what a board wants to see.

4. Was Bose a fascist collaborator?

The charge: he allied with Nazi Germany and Imperial Japan, regimes responsible for enormous atrocities, and he admired authoritarian methods.
The record: He did ally with them, and he wrote approvingly of the efficiency of authoritarian states in the 1930s. He also objected publicly to racist passages in Mein Kampf, insisted the INA be non-communal and egalitarian, created a women’s combat regiment, and consistently described his aim as an independent, socialist, democratic India — his Azad Hind proclamation committed to a permanent constitution to be framed by the Indian people. Your line: “He made a realpolitik calculation that Britain’s enemies were India’s temporary allies — the same logic as the Irish in 1916. I think the alliance with the Axis is a legitimate criticism and one we should not dodge. But calling him a fascist misreads him: the INA’s own practice — mixed units, common messing, a women’s regiment — was more egalitarian than the army it fought.”

5. Nehru versus Patel

The charge, in its usual form: Patel should have been Prime Minister; Gandhi imposed Nehru; Kashmir and China would have gone differently.
The record: In 1946, 12 of 15 Provincial Congress Committees nominated Patel and none nominated Nehru; Gandhi asked Patel to withdraw and he did, without public complaint. They worked together closely from 1947 to Patel’s death in December 1950, and their correspondence shows both serious disagreement and deep mutual respect. Your line: “The counterfactual is unknowable. What is knowable is that they were complementary — Patel built the territorial state and the administrative machine, Nehru built the democratic and institutional framework and India’s foreign policy. The habit of setting them against each other is a later political construction; in 1947 they were a partnership, and Gandhi's judgement was that both were needed.”

6. Savarkar and the mercy petitions

The facts: Savarkar was a genuine revolutionary who organised abroad, wrote the first nationalist history of 1857, and served about ten years in the Cellular Jail under two life sentences in brutal conditions. He also submitted several clemency petitions between 1911 and 1920, some in submissive language, and after release accepted conditions restricting political activity. He later articulated Hindutva (1923), a political doctrine distinct from the secular nationalism of the Congress, and was tried and acquitted in the Gandhi assassination case. Your line, if pressed: “Sir, this is a politically contested figure and I would rather give facts than take a side. He was undoubtedly a revolutionary who suffered severely, and he also wrote petitions that his critics find hard to reconcile with that. His later political philosophy is a separate question from his record as a revolutionary, and people who agree about one often disagree about the other.” Then stop. Do not be drawn into current politics.

The rule for every controversy

The board is not testing your politics and you should never reveal a partisan alignment. They are testing whether you can hold a difficult subject calmly, acknowledge evidence that cuts against your instinct, and reach a view without contempt for people who reach a different one. That capacity — not the historical content — is what is actually being assessed. If you ever feel provoked, slow down, say “there are two views on this,” and give both before giving yours.

Lesser-known facts worth carrying

Used sparingly — one or two, at the right moment — these signal genuine reading rather than exam preparation. Used in bulk, they signal showing off. Choose three that you find genuinely interesting and let the rest go.

  • The East India Company was, for a time, a bigger military power than most states. By 1800 its army was roughly twice the size of the British Army. A private company with a private army of a quarter of a million men is the extreme case of state functions in corporate hands — an excellent GD point on regulating corporate power.
  • Britain bailed out the Company in 1773 with the Tea Act, which let it dump surplus tea in America duty-free. The result was the Boston Tea Party. The American Revolution was triggered, in part, by a bailout of the company that was conquering India.
  • India’s flag went through several designs. The 1906 Calcutta flag, Cama’s Stuttgart flag (1907), the Home Rule flag (1917), Pingali Venkayya’s design (1921) with the charkha, the 1931 tricolour, and finally the Constituent Assembly’s adoption on 22 July 1947, with the Ashoka Chakra replacing the charkha at the suggestion that a wheel of law was a better emblem of a state.
  • “Jana Gana Mana” was written in 1911 and first sung at the Calcutta Congress on 27 December 1911. The persistent claim that it was written in praise of George V was denied by Tagore himself in a 1937 letter in terms of some asperity. Adopted as the national anthem on 24 January 1950; Vande Mataram was given equal status as the national song.
  • The Cellular Jail at Port Blair had 693 solitary cells arranged in seven wings around a central tower — the panopticon design — and was built by the prisoners themselves. Only three of the seven wings survive.
  • Vinayak Damodar Savarkar’s escape attempt at Marseilles in 1910 — he squeezed through a porthole and swam ashore — produced a case before the Permanent Court of Arbitration at The Hague on whether France should have returned him. France lost.
  • Gandhi was nominated for the Nobel Peace Prize five times and never received it. The Nobel Committee itself published a regretful note about this in 1989 and again in 2006.
  • The Congress Radio of 1942 broadcast on 42.34 metres and moved location almost every day. Usha Mehta was 22; she served four years and later became a professor of political science.
  • The 1946 Indian elections were fought on a franchise of roughly 10–14 per cent of the population. India’s first general election in 1951–52, by contrast, enfranchised about 173 million adults, most of them illiterate — the largest exercise in democratic faith ever attempted anywhere.
  • The INA’s treasure and the Rani of Jhansi Regiment’s survivors largely returned to civilian life in Malaya and India; Lakshmi Sahgal practised medicine in Kanpur for fifty years and, at the age of 87, stood for President of India in 2002.
  • Three future army chiefs of three different countries — India, Pakistan and Burma — were in the first course at the IMA in 1932. Manekshaw, Musa and Smith Dun.
  • The first Indian to be awarded the Victoria Cross was Sepoy Khudadad Khan of the 129th Baluchis, at Hollebeke in Belgium on 31 October 1914, for continuing to fire a machine gun alone after his entire crew was killed.
  • Sixty-seven Indian soldiers of the pre-1947 army are buried in Europe under Commonwealth headstones, and the memorial at Neuve-Chapelle commemorates 4,742 Indian soldiers with no known grave. The Haifa Day commemoration on 23 September each year marks the Indian cavalry charge that liberated Haifa in 1918 — one of the last successful cavalry charges in military history.
  • Netaji’s INA had a secret service, a currency, a national bank (the Azad Hind Bank, Rangoon) and a civil administration. It was a state-building project, not only a military one.
  • Sardar Patel’s integration of the princely states cost the Indian exchequer the privy purses — about Rs 4.66 crore a year at the outset. Critics called it expensive. It was, by any measure, the cheapest territorial consolidation in modern history.

Legacy and unfinished business

What India inherited on 15 August 1947

The liabilities

  • Literacy about 16–18 per cent; life expectancy about 32 years.
  • Per-capita income roughly stagnant for half a century; almost no heavy industry outside Jamshedpur and a handful of centres.
  • A partitioned economy: the jute mills in India, the jute fields in East Pakistan; the cotton mills in India, much of the long-staple cotton in Sind and West Punjab; the canal headworks on one side of a new border and the irrigated land on the other.
  • 10–18 million refugees to resettle and a communal wound still open.
  • 565 princely states to integrate, a war in Kashmir within ten weeks, and a divided army.
  • No universal franchise experience, no national language consensus, and predictions from serious observers that the country would break up within a decade.

The assets

  • A political leadership with sixty years of organisational experience and enormous legitimacy.
  • An administrative service, a legal system, a railway network and a functioning tax machinery.
  • A professional, apolitical, non-communal army — deliberately kept out of politics, a decision whose value becomes obvious when you compare India’s post-colonial trajectory with almost any comparable country’s.
  • A drafted vision: the Constituent Assembly had been sitting since 9 December 1946 and would produce the Constitution on 26 November 1949, in force 26 January 1950.
  • Universal adult franchise from day one — no property, literacy, gender or income qualification. No other newly independent country of comparable poverty attempted it.

The questions 1947 left open

  1. The boundary and Kashmir — the unfinished business of Partition, and the source of four wars.
  2. Communal harmony — Partition settled a territorial question and did not settle a social one, in either country.
  3. Caste — Ambedkar’s warning in his closing speech to the Constituent Assembly on 25 November 1949 remains the sharpest statement of it: that India was entering “a life of contradictions,” with political equality of one-man-one-vote and social and economic inequality, and that if the contradiction was not removed “those who suffer from inequality will blow up the structure of political democracy.”
  4. Economic transformation — political freedom was won; the freedom from poverty Naoroji had defined was not, and much of the post-1947 argument about planning, licensing and liberalisation is a continuation of the argument the nationalist economists began.
  5. Federalism and language — the linguistic reorganisation of states (States Reorganisation Act 1956) settled one part of a question the Congress had itself raised at Nagpur in 1920.
The closing thought for any lecturette on this subject

“We did not only win a country in 1947. We won the right to make our own mistakes and to correct them ourselves. Every generation since has had to decide what to do with that. For us the question is not what the freedom fighters achieved — it is whether we are worth what they paid.” A line like this works because it is short, it is not chest-thumping, and it turns history into responsibility. Adapt it in your own words; never deliver it as a recitation.

Part VIII

SSB toolkit

SSB relevance and technique

History is assessed differently in an SSB than in an examination. Nobody is checking your dates. They are checking reasoning, balance, emotional stability under provocation, and whether you can connect the past to a professional present. Here is how the subject appears in each stage.

Personal Interview

  • Expect these: “Who is your favourite freedom fighter and why?” · “Was 1857 the first war of independence?” · “Could Partition have been avoided?” · “Gandhi or Bose?” · “What did the British give India?” · “Why did we take so long to become free?” · “What is the relevance of the freedom struggle today?”
  • Structure every answer in three parts: a one-line position, two or three specific supporting facts, and a concession or nuance. The concession is what makes it credible.
  • Never bluff a date. “Sir, I think it was in the early 1930s but I am not certain of the exact year” is a good answer. A confident wrong date is a bad one, and the board will often check.
  • Favourite freedom fighter: choose someone you can actually discuss for three minutes, name one specific incident, and say what quality you are trying to build in yourself from them. Avoid the reflexive answer; if you say Bhagat Singh, be ready for “what did he write?”
  • Watch the tone. Anti-British rhetoric is unnecessary and slightly immature — Britain is a partner country and an ally today. Analytical criticism of colonialism is entirely appropriate; contempt is not.

Group Discussion

  • Likely topics: “Was Partition inevitable?” · “Did the British do more harm than good?” · “Violent vs non-violent struggle” · “Should we demand reparations?” · “Have we fulfilled the vision of our freedom fighters?” · “Nation-building: 1947 vs today.”
  • Your value in a GD is direction, not volume. Try to be the person who frames: “Can we agree to look at this in three parts — the economic, the political and the social?” A framing contribution in the first ninety seconds is worth more than five later interventions.
  • Bring one specific fact nobody else has — the 1946 election result, Auchinleck’s commutation, the Risley minute, the countervailing excise of 1896. Specificity earns you the floor.
  • Bring others in. “I think what he was saying about separate electorates connects to…” Group testing officers are watching cooperation as much as content.
  • Never let it become communal. If the discussion drifts that way, redirect to institutions and decisions: “I think the more useful question is what the electoral system did, rather than what communities did.”

Psychological tests

  • WAT — words like Freedom, Sacrifice, Nation, Struggle, Unity, Leader, Duty, History, Courage, Revolution come up constantly. Respond with short, positive, action-oriented sentences, not slogans: “Freedom — freedom demands daily responsibility.” Avoid empty patriotic declamation; it reads as performance.
  • SRT — situations about communal tension, mob violence, a rumour spreading, protecting a person from a crowd. The freedom struggle gives you the right instinct: de-escalate, protect the vulnerable, inform authority, do not take sides on identity.
  • TAT / Self-description — drawing on a historical figure is fine if it is specific and genuine. “I admire Patel’s habit of taking the decision and owning it” is usable. “I want to be like Gandhi” is not.
The single most common mistake

Candidates treat this subject as devotional. They speak in exclamation marks, use phrases like “great martyrs sacrificed everything,” and say nothing specific. The board hears this fifty times a week and it registers as absence of thought. Reverence is assumed; you do not have to perform it. Lower the temperature, raise the specificity, and you will sound like an officer rather than a school assembly.

Debate points: both sides, prepared

For each proposition, the two strongest arguments each way. Prepare both sides of everything — in a GD you may be pushed to defend either, and in an interview the ability to state the opposing case fairly is itself the test.

“Partition was inevitable”

For: Separate electorates from 1909 institutionalised religion as the unit of politics for four decades; by 1946 the League had won 86.6 per cent of the Muslim vote and all 30 central Muslim seats, which is a democratic mandate that could not simply be overridden; the Interim Government of 1946–47 showed that a forced partnership would produce paralysis, not unity.

Against: Both parties accepted the Cabinet Mission Plan in June 1946 — a united India was agreed and then lost over the interpretation of grouping; the League had no Muslim-majority province in 1937; Punjab and Bengal were governed by non-League coalitions until the 1940s; and a federal formula with strong provinces was available and was nearly taken.

“British rule did more harm than good”

For: A quarter of world output to four per cent; repeated famines with millions of deaths under a policy of non-intervention; literacy at 16 per cent after 190 years; systematic de-industrialisation and a tariff policy designed for Lancashire; political subordination and racial humiliation as daily practice.

Against: Unified legal and administrative institutions, a railway and telegraph network, universities, the survey and census apparatus, and the English link language — all retained by independent India; some social legislation (sati, widow remarriage, the Sarda Act); and some of India’s stagnation had ecological and demographic causes that would have operated under any government.

The decisive framing: the test is not “did anything good happen?” but opportunity cost — what would India have done with its own revenue over 190 years? Japan is the comparison.

“Non-violence won independence”

For: It made mass participation possible across class, gender and age; it denied the state the justification it needed for overwhelming force; it won the international argument; and it left India with democratic rather than militarised political habits — compare the post-colonial trajectory of states that won independence by armed struggle.

Against: Every non-violent movement was suppressed; the British left when they were bankrupt, militarily exhausted and unsure of their own Indian troops; the INA trials and the RIN mutiny were the immediate triggers of 1946; and the revolutionary tradition sustained a spirit of defiance that constitutional politics alone could not.

“Should Britain pay reparations?”

For: The extraction was real and quantifiable in part (Home Charges, the 1857 suppression costs, the Koh-i-Noor and looted artefacts); acknowledgement matters morally; and some restitution of cultural property is already accepted practice internationally.

Against: The counterfactual is unknowable and the compounding assumptions are arbitrary; the present generation of neither country is the relevant party; and India’s interest today is a forward-looking partnership — trade, technology, diaspora — rather than a legal claim it cannot win. A good middle position: “I would prefer acknowledgement and the return of specific looted objects to a monetary claim. Reparations are an argument about the past; India’s advantage lies in arguments about the future.”

“The freedom struggle was an elite movement”

For: Its leadership was overwhelmingly English-educated, upper-caste and professional; the franchise under which it contested elections covered 10–14 per cent of the population; the social questions of caste and land were consistently deferred.

Against: Ninety thousand arrests in 1930 and 1932 each, and over ninety thousand in 1942, cannot be an elite; parallel governments in Ballia, Tamluk and Satara were run by villagers; the Khudai Khidmatgars, the Kisan Sabhas, the Tebhaga and Telangana movements and the tribal risings were mass movements with their own leadership.

“Quit India was a mistake”

For: Launched at the worst strategic moment, with Japan on the border; suppressed in six weeks; abandoned non-violence in practice; and it removed the Congress leadership from politics for three years, during which the League became a mass party — arguably making Partition far more likely.

Against: It proved that British rule survived only by force, which settled the post-war question; it tied down troops Britain could not spare; and a movement that had been promising action since 1940 would have lost all credibility had it done nothing after Cripps failed.

Glossary

Diwani
The right to collect land revenue and administer civil justice. Granted to the Company for Bengal, Bihar and Orissa in 1765 — the legal basis of colonial rule.
Nizamat
The executive and criminal justice function, nominally retained by the Nawab under the Dual Government of 1765–72.
Dastak
A duty-free trade pass obtained under the 1717 farman, systematically abused by private English traders — the proximate cause of conflict with Siraj-ud-Daulah and Mir Qasim.
Subsidiary Alliance
Wellesley’s system by which a ruler paid for a British garrison, accepted a Resident, and surrendered his foreign policy — sovereignty lost without formal annexation.
Doctrine of Lapse
Dalhousie’s rule that a dependent state without a natural heir lapsed to the paramount power, adopted heirs not being recognised.
Paramountcy
The Crown’s overriding authority over the princely states after 1858 — defence, external relations and communications. It lapsed in 1947 rather than passing to either dominion.
Home Charges
The annual remittance from India to Britain covering debt service, India Office costs, pensions, guaranteed railway returns and overseas military expenditure.
Drain of wealth
Naoroji’s term for the unrequited transfer of Indian resources to Britain — exports for which no payment returned.
Dyarchy
“Double rule” — the 1919 division of provincial subjects into transferred (ministerial) and reserved (gubernatorial) categories.
Separate electorates
Constituencies reserved for one religious community, voting only for candidates of that community. Introduced 1909, extended 1919 and 1935, abolished 1950.
Satyagraha
“Holding firmly to truth.” Gandhi’s term for non-violent resistance, deliberately chosen in place of “passive resistance.”
Ahimsa
Non-violence in thought, word and deed — the ethical foundation of satyagraha.
Swadeshi / Swaraj
“Of one’s own country” — the use and promotion of indigenous goods. / “Self-rule” — from dominion status in early usage to complete independence after 1929.
Purna Swaraj
Complete independence, adopted as the Congress goal at Lahore in December 1929.
Khadi
Hand-spun, hand-woven cloth — the movement’s central symbol, a means of rural employment, and a wearable act of political affiliation.
Hartal
A complete cessation of work and business as a mode of protest.
Hijrat / Kala pani
Religiously motivated migration, notably by Khilafat supporters to Afghanistan in 1920. / “Black water” — the sea, whose crossing meant loss of caste, and by extension transportation to the Andamans.
Tinkathia
The Champaran obligation to plant indigo on three kathas of every twenty of holding.
Ryot / Zamindar / Mahal

Twelve dates you must not get wrong

The arc in eight sentences

  1. Europeans came for spices and stayed for textiles; the English won because of finance, sea power and Indian political fragmentation.
  2. Plassey (1757) gave the Company Bengal’s politics and Buxar (1764) gave it legal sovereignty; the Diwani of 1765 let it buy Indian goods with Indian taxes.
  3. Conquest ended in 1849 with Punjab; the system rested on land revenue, an Indian army and Indian intermediaries.
  4. The Revolt of 1857 destroyed Company rule, produced the Crown Raj, and pushed the British into alliance with princes and landlords.
  5. Western education, the press, the economic critique and racial humiliation produced the Congress in 1885; Bengal’s partition in 1905 produced the first mass movement.
  6. The First World War’s broken promises, the Rowlatt Act and Jallianwala Bagh turned a petitioning body into a mass movement under Gandhi from 1920.
  7. Three mass movements (1920–22, 1930–34, 1942) destroyed the legitimacy of British rule; separate electorates and the politics of 1937–46 built the constituency for Partition.
  8. The Second World War bankrupted Britain; the INA trials and the RIN mutiny made the Indian armed forces unreliable; power was transferred in ten hurried weeks, and the country was divided.

Five facts that win arguments

  • Roughly 240,000 of the Company’s 300,000 troops in 1857 were Indian — India was conquered with Indian manpower.
  • Risley’s 1904 minute states the political purpose of Bengal’s partition in the government’s own words.
  • Auchinleck commuted the INA sentences in January 1946 because he feared the reaction in the Indian Army — the British saying it themselves.
  • The League won 4.8 per cent of the Muslim vote in 1937 and 86.6 per cent in 1946 — Partition’s constituency was made in nine years, not nine centuries.
  • Literacy at independence was about 16–18 per cent and life expectancy about 32 years — the single most damning statistic of 190 years of rule.

Three answers to have ready, word for word in your own phrasing

  • Why did the British leave? The movement removed consent; the war removed capacity; 1946 removed certainty about the army.
  • Gandhi or Bose? One made India ungovernable by consent, the other made it ungovernable by force. Neither alone would have sufficed.
  • Was Partition inevitable? Not in 1937, and not even in June 1946 when both parties accepted the Cabinet Mission Plan. It became inevitable between July 1946 and March 1947.
