What are India’s core issues?

This handbook follows a mains-style policy-brief format for each issue: context, arguments in favour, arguments against/risks, the state’s current approach, and a way-forward. Use it to write structured answers rather than one-sided opinions.

At home, the priority issues are growth with inclusion, inflation and household stress, governance capacity, and infrastructure/energy resilience. Abroad, the priority issues are the China border and strategic rivalry, the Pakistan-terrorism problem, the US relationship, Indo-Pacific maritime security, technology/supply-chain dependence, defence modernisation, and climate-linked security risk.

These issues are connected, not separate silos: a domestic growth gap weakens military preparedness; a trade disruption reshapes jobs and inflation; a diplomatic misstep widens strategic risk. A good mains answer links the domestic and external dimension of any issue rather than treating them in isolation.

Answer-writing template

Context (1-2 lines) → Arguments for → Arguments against/risks → Government measures → Way forward → one-line conclusion linking domestic and strategic impact.

Growth, jobs and inequality

Context: India’s long-run advantage is scale and demographic size. Its core challenge is conversion: turning that scale into productive, skilled, formal employment rather than growth that concentrates in a few sectors and regions.

Arguments in favour (optimism)

  • Large consumer market and a maturing services and digital base.
  • Expanding manufacturing ambition and PLI-led capacity build-up.
  • Young, mobile labour force and a growing entrepreneurial ecosystem.

Arguments against / risks

  • Sharp inequality and uneven job quality across states and sectors.
  • Persistent rural and farm distress despite aggregate growth.
  • Large informal workforce with low productivity and weak social security.

Way forward

  • Scale skilling and apprenticeship programmes tied to actual industry demand.
  • Accelerate formalisation of MSMEs through credit access and simplified compliance.
  • Prioritise labour-intensive manufacturing and logistics to absorb the workforce.
Exam angle

India can become high-productivity and high-income only if scale is matched with skilling, formalisation and logistics; failure costs more than GDP points — it weakens social cohesion and strategic capacity.

Part I · India at home

Domestic pressures and policy trade-offs

Governance, federalism and state capacity

Context: India’s governance challenge is not whether the Centre is strong enough; it is whether state capacity is consistent across regions and levels of administration. Public trust depends on delivery — roads, justice, electricity, ports and welfare systems functioning in practice.

Arguments for central coordination

  • Scale and standardisation needed in infrastructure, security and industrial policy.
  • Faster, uniform execution of national missions across states.

Arguments against over-centralisation

  • States differ sharply in capacity, political culture and social demands.
  • Excessive centralisation can blunt local accountability and slow correction.

Way forward

  • Strengthen state-level implementation capacity through fiscal devolution and technical support.
  • Use cooperative federalism forums (GST Council-style bodies) for execution-heavy reforms.
  • Build third-party monitoring of delivery outcomes, not just policy announcements.
Exam angle

If federal governance improves, India competes more effectively in manufacturing and strategic planning; if it does not, Centre-state friction slows reform exactly when speed is needed.

Energy, food and infrastructure stress

Context: Energy security, food supply resilience, logistics bottlenecks and urban-rural infrastructure gaps are not side issues; they feed directly into inflation, industrial performance and strategic preparedness.

Arguments in favour

  • Renewables push, rail upgrades and port expansion can lower cost pressures over time.
  • Improves resilience against commodity-price and climate shocks.

Arguments against / risks

  • Transition needs large capital, consistent policy, and reliable grid upgrades.
  • Coordination across ministries and states is slow and inconsistent.

Way forward

  • Diversify energy imports while scaling domestic renewable and storage capacity.
  • Invest in multimodal logistics (rail-road-port) to cut transit cost and time.
  • Build buffer stocks and price-stabilisation mechanisms for key food items.
Exam angle

The real challenge is not “green” policy in rhetoric but keeping the economy stable while shifting energy mix, infrastructure model and industrial strategy together.

Part II · India abroad

Border security, diplomacy and strategic balance

China, border security and strategic rivalry

Context: The China challenge is the single most important strategic issue for India. The unresolved border, the broader strategic rivalry, and the trade relationship cannot be treated as separate tracks — the same relationship that creates trade opportunity also creates strategic suspicion and supply-chain dependence.

Arguments for engagement

  • India needs market access, inputs and a stable relationship with a major power.
  • Managed engagement lowers the risk of miscalculation through dialogue channels.

Arguments against complacency

  • The border dispute remains unresolved; PLA posture and infrastructure build-up have changed.
  • Strategic rivalry extends to surveillance, logistics and political signalling, not just the LAC.

Way forward

  • Continue border-infrastructure hardening and forward deployment under Border Area Development.
  • Diversify trade and critical-mineral/electronics supply chains away from single-source dependence.
  • Keep military and diplomatic channels (WMCC, Corps Commander talks) active even amid tension.
Exam angle

India’s posture is best framed as competitive coexistence: defensive deterrence and supply diversification alongside continued diplomatic engagement — not a binary of confrontation or trust.

Pakistan, terrorism and the border question

Context: India’s Pakistan problem combines history, strategic geography and cross-border violence. It is not just military confrontation; it is about trust deficits, proxy conflict, the military balance, and the political cost of instability near the border.

Arguments for a firm posture

  • Deterrence and security require visible readiness and counter-terror capacity.
  • A disciplined response to infiltration and proxy tactics signals resolve to adversaries and allies alike.

Arguments against escalation

  • Unchecked escalation can narrow diplomatic space and raise miscalculation risk.
  • Domestic nationalism can push policy toward symbolism rather than sustainable strategy.

Way forward

  • Maintain calibrated deterrence: hard security at the border, counter-infiltration grid, surgical response options.
  • Build sustained international pressure against cross-border terror financing and safe havens.
  • Keep a narrow diplomatic channel open for crisis management even without full normalisation.
Exam angle

India’s best long-term strategy is calibrated deterrence, not endless escalation — persistent friction managed with strategic discipline rather than emotional response.

US and Western relations

Context: India’s relationship with the United States has broadened into technology, defence, maritime security and supply chains, driven by shared interest in managing China’s rise.

Arguments in favour

  • Access to advanced technology, strategic capital, defence systems and diplomatic backing.
  • Deeper interoperability through agreements such as LEMOA, COMCASA and BECA.

Arguments against / risks

  • Risk of strategic entanglement or being seen as aligned with one bloc.
  • Dependence on a single partner for critical defence or technology inputs.

Way forward

  • Deepen cooperation issue-by-issue (tech, defence, trade) rather than through a formal alliance.
  • Preserve independent channels with Russia, the Gulf and the Global South in parallel.
  • Use co-production and technology-transfer clauses to build domestic capacity, not just imports.
Exam angle

The likely trajectory is not a formal alliance but a deepening web of issue-based cooperation, with India’s independence treated as a strategic asset rather than a liability.

Indo-Pacific, QUAD and maritime security

Context: India’s maritime outlook is shaped by the Indo-Pacific — key sea lanes, critical islands, disputed waters, and the choices of countries that will determine the future security architecture.

Arguments for QUAD/maritime cooperation

  • Capability-building, information-sharing and logistics access in a contested environment.
  • Improves surveillance, humanitarian response and interoperability with partners.

Arguments against over-commitment

  • Coalition membership can create political friction and raise partner expectations.
  • Risks complicating India’s traditional non-aligned balancing behaviour.

Way forward

  • Expand maritime domain awareness and logistics-sharing with regional partners.
  • Keep QUAD functional and non-military in public framing while deepening practical cooperation.
  • Invest in blue-water capability to secure SLOCs (sea lines of communication) independently.
Exam angle

The practical goal is not joining a single bloc but shaping an open, stable Indo-Pacific where India remains central — maritime deterrence plus coalition flexibility.

Technology, data and supply chains

Context: Technology is central to jobs, defence, digital governance and national security. India’s challenge is capturing the gains of digital innovation while reducing strategic dependence on a narrow set of external suppliers and platforms.

Arguments in favour

  • High-value manufacturing, stronger digital public infrastructure (DPI), and a skilled workforce.
  • Greater strategic autonomy through semiconductor and electronics localisation.

Arguments against / risks

  • Supply-chain vulnerability in critical minerals, chips and components.
  • Cybersecurity risk, uneven regulation, and political pressure from major economies.

Way forward

  • Scale semiconductor mission incentives and critical-mineral sourcing diversification.
  • Build a data-protection and AI-governance framework that does not stifle innovation.
  • Deepen technology-sharing pacts (iCET-style) while retaining domestic IP and manufacturing.
Exam angle

India’s likely path is strategic balancing: build domestic capacity in semiconductors and digital infrastructure while engaging globally without over-dependence on any single country or platform.

Defence, security and strategic autonomy

Context: India’s defence policy sits at the intersection of deterrence, industrial development and fiscal realism. The country needs modern systems, credible logistics and cross-service coordination, without overextending the budget.

Arguments for indigenous capability

  • Greater independence and lower strategic vulnerability through Atmanirbhar defence production.
  • Domestic jobs and a more resilient industrial base (DRDO, DPSUs, private defence startups).

Arguments against / risks

  • Procurement and platform delays, uneven technology absorption.
  • Maintenance burden and cost overruns can undercut operational readiness.

Way forward

  • Fast-track theatre commands and joint logistics for genuine tri-service integration.
  • Blend indigenous production with select strategic imports for capability gaps.
  • Tie defence budgeting to clear threat-prioritisation rather than platform prestige.
Exam angle

The goal is a defence posture that is credible, affordable and strategically coherent — not maximum spending or maximal confrontation.

Climate, migration and geopolitics

Context: Climate and migration are increasingly national-security issues. Floods, heat stress, crop volatility and water stress all influence domestic stability, migration patterns and regional politics.

Arguments in favour of acting now

  • Adaptation is a development priority, not just an environmental one.
  • India’s scale gives it leverage to shape global climate-finance and technology terms.

Arguments against / risks

  • Without resilience planning, climate stress fuels urban pressure and rural distress.
  • Competing demands for energy, water and adaptation funding sharpen regional friction.

Way forward

  • Mainstream climate-risk assessment into infrastructure and urban planning.
  • Use platforms like ISA and CDRI to shape global rules rather than just follow them.
  • Build early-warning and water-management systems in the most climate-exposed states.
Exam angle

Countries that adapt faster gain political stability and economic resilience; the real test is making climate security a core part of national planning, not a rhetorical add-on.

Why diplomacy matters

Context: Diplomacy is underrated because it is less dramatic than conflict or election rhetoric, yet it shapes most strategic outcomes through negotiation, crisis management and issue-specific cooperation.

Arguments in favour

  • Creates space for de-escalation, trade management and crisis communication.
  • Allows India to shape rules and coalitions rather than merely react to them.

Arguments against / limits

  • Diplomatic processes can be slow, vague, or captured by short-term politics.
  • Over-reliance on dialogue can be read as weakness by adversarial states.

Way forward

  • Keep red lines firm and publicly clear while remaining flexible on issue-specific cooperation.
  • Invest in diplomatic capacity (missions, trade negotiators, strategic-studies cadre).
  • Use multilateral platforms (G20, SCO, BRICS, QUAD) to diversify leverage, not just symbolism.
Exam angle

India needs a confident but less noisy diplomatic style: firm red lines, flexible issue management, and a focus on shaping the terms of cooperation rather than reacting after events occur.

Future story: India by 2035

Context: India by 2035 is unlikely to be a simple story of triumph or decline. The more realistic path is strategic acceleration with uneven domestic delivery — the outcome depends on whether scale converts into capability.

Favourable scenario drivers

  • Sustained growth, governance reform and manufacturing expansion.
  • Stronger diplomatic balancing across the US, Russia, the Gulf and the Indo-Pacific.

Unfavourable scenario drivers

  • Regional inequality, weak state capacity and strategic overextension.
  • Diplomatic drift or over-commitment to a single bloc under external pressure.

Way forward

  • Sequence reform: state capacity and jobs first, strategic signalling second.
  • Treat diplomacy and domestic delivery as one connected policy track, not two.
  • Build resilience buffers (fiscal, energy, supply-chain) ahead of the next global shock.
Exam angle

Without internal strength, diplomacy weakens; without external balance, growth becomes more vulnerable. India’s best future rests on disciplined implementation, not symbolism.

Perspectives and controversies

Established India needs stronger state capacity, industrial policy and defence readiness to sustain its growth and strategic position. Interpretation Analysts disagree on sequencing: some prioritise domestic reform first, others prioritise external balancing first. Disputed How far India should deepen US/Western defence-tech ties without risking strategic entanglement remains actively contested. Re-verify specific trade, defence-deal and diplomatic-statement details against current official sources before citing them in an answer.

The real issue is not whether one perspective is “right” and another is “wrong.” It is which trade-offs are acceptable under today’s conditions. India’s future depends on balancing growth with inclusion, deterrence with diplomacy, and autonomy with partnership. Those are real and continuing choices.

Debate motions

  1. India’s growth will be limited more by governance and inclusion than by capital shortages.
  2. Strategic engagement with China is necessary but cannot be based on trust.
  3. India should deepen defence and technology ties with the US even if it reduces diplomatic flexibility.
  4. Border deterrence matters more than diplomatic public messaging in India’s security doctrine.
  5. India’s long-term strength depends more on domestic reform than on external alliances.
  6. Technology sovereignty is a strategic necessity for India’s future.
  7. Climate resilience should be treated as a core national security issue.
  8. India should pursue a more assertive diplomatic posture in the Indo-Pacific.

Master chronology

  • 1947Independence creates the strategic challenge of building a secure, inclusive and internationally relevant state.
  • 1948-71Wars, partition aftermath, and regional tensions shape India’s security and diplomatic posture.
  • 1980s-90sGlobalisation and liberalisation transform India’s economy and increase external strategic complexity.
  • 2000sDigital expansion, global trade and security competition reshape Indian policy choices.
  • 2014 onwardIndia’s military, diplomatic and economic posture becomes more assertive across multiple fronts.
  • 2019-2024Border tensions, pandemic disruption, and supply-chain stress sharpen strategic and domestic debates.
  • 2025-35India’s next decade will be shaped by growth, technology, resilience, and balancing major-power competition.

Glossary

Strategic autonomy
The ability to decide policy priorities and partnerships without being fully dependent on any one major power.
Deterrence
Measures designed to convince an adversary that aggression will be costly and likely unsuccessful.
Supply-chain resilience
The ability of a country or economy to keep critical goods and materials flowing despite shocks or geopolitical disruption.
Federalism
A system in which power and responsibility are shared between the centre and state governments.
Indo-Pacific
The wider maritime and strategic zone linking the Indian and Pacific Oceans.
Border hardening
Improving infrastructure, surveillance, logistics and force posture along sensitive borders.

Rapid revision sheet

One-page answer framework

  • State the context in one line: what is the issue and why does it matter now?
  • Give 2-3 arguments in favour, grounded in concrete policy or strategic logic.
  • Give 2-3 arguments against or risks, not just a token counterpoint.
  • Name the government’s current approach or measures where relevant.
  • Close with a way-forward and a one-line link to India’s broader strategic position.
  • Growth without inclusion creates political pressure.
  • Security without diplomacy can increase risk rather than reduce it.
  • Technology strategy is part of national security strategy.
  • Border problems and economic relations are linked, not separate.
  • Strategic autonomy means choosing partnerships without losing independence.
  • Climate and infrastructure resilience affect both growth and stability.
  • Good policy must weigh pros, cons, costs and likely future impact.
