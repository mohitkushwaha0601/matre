Using this handbook

This is the short version: enough to speak accurately on any part of the freedom struggle, built to be read in two to three hours.

  • First pass: read the snapshot and the revision sheet. That is your skeleton.
  • Second pass: read Parts I-IV in order. Each section gives the facts, then one idea worth saying aloud.
  • Third pass: answer the practice questions aloud in two minutes each.
How to answer any history question

Say what happened and when; explain why (separate the trigger from the deeper cause); say what changed because of it; give one other view; then say what it means today. Five moves, under two minutes.

Claims are tagged Established Interpretation Disputed. Where numbers are contested, quote a range.

The whole story in three lines

The British won India through finance, sea power and Indian division. They ruled it through land revenue, an Indian army and Indian collaborators. They lost it when a mass movement removed consent, a world war removed capacity, and 1946 removed their confidence in the Indian armed forces.

Part I

Traders to rulers

The Europeans arrive, 1498-1750

Europeans came for pepper and stayed for cloth. For two and a half centuries they were traders dependent on Indian rulers' permission.

  • 1498Vasco da Gama reaches Calicut, opening the sea route round Africa.
  • 1510The Portuguese take Goa under Albuquerque — their base for a century of naval control.
  • 1600The English East India Company receives its royal charter (31 December). The Dutch VOC follows in 1602.
  • 1613-15English factory at Surat; Sir Thomas Roe at Jahangir's court.
  • 1664The French company is founded; Pondicherry becomes its centre.
  • 1707Death of Aurangzeb; the Mughal empire fragments into successor states.
  • 1746-63The Carnatic Wars: Anglo-French rivalry in the south, decided at Wandiwash (1760).
Why the English won

Interpretation Not superior civilisation, but three practical advantages: a company backed by British credit and Parliament, command of the sea, and a divided subcontinent in which Indian rulers hired Company troops against each other.

Plassey to Punjab: the conquest

In a century the Company went from a trading firm to the ruler of most of South Asia.

Milestones of conquest
Year Event Why it mattered
1757 Plassey — Clive defeats Siraj-ud-Daulah, helped by Mir Jafar's betrayal Control of Bengal's politics.
1764 Buxar — defeat of Mir Qasim, the Nawab of Awadh and the Mughal emperor A real military victory; legal sovereignty followed.
1765 Diwani of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa (Treaty of Allahabad) The right to collect revenue — Indian taxes now paid for Indian exports.
1767-99 Four Anglo-Mysore Wars; Tipu Sultan killed at Seringapatam The strongest southern opponent removed.
1775-1818 Three Anglo-Maratha Wars The last pan-Indian power broken.
1845-49 Two Anglo-Sikh Wars; Punjab annexed Conquest essentially complete.

Tools of expansion: Wellesley's Subsidiary Alliance (1798) — a ruler paid for a British garrison and gave up his foreign policy; and Dalhousie's Doctrine of Lapse — states without a natural heir were annexed (Satara, Jhansi, Nagpur). Awadh was annexed in 1856 on grounds of misgovernment.

A fact that wins arguments. Established Roughly 240,000 of the Company's 300,000 troops in 1857 were Indian. India was conquered largely with Indian soldiers and Indian money.

Part II

The colonial state

Land, drain, famine and railways

The Raj rested on land revenue. How that revenue was raised — and where it went — is the core of the economic case against colonial rule.

Permanent Settlement, 1793

Cornwallis fixed revenue permanently with zamindars in Bengal, who became proprietors. Peasants lost security.

Ryotwari

Settled directly with the cultivator (ryot) in Madras and Bombay, associated with Thomas Munro. High, revisable assessments.

Mahalwari

Settled with the village or estate (mahal) as a unit, in the North-Western Provinces and Punjab.

The economic critique

  • Drain of wealth — Dadabhai Naoroji's Poverty and Un-British Rule in India (1901) argued that Home Charges and remittances sent Indian wealth to Britain without return. R. C. Dutt developed the case.
  • Deindustrialisation — Indian handloom textiles lost markets to Lancashire machine cloth.
  • Famines — the Bengal famine of 1770, the Great Famine of 1876-78 and the Bengal famine of 1943 each killed millions. Disputed Death tolls are estimates; quote ranges.
  • Railways — first passenger train Bombay-Thane, 16 April 1853. Built with guaranteed returns to British investors and designed mainly for trade and troop movement, but they also unified the country.
A balanced line

Interpretation Historians still debate how much colonial rule caused India's poverty versus failed to reduce it. The strongest single statistic: literacy at independence was about 16-18% and life expectancy about 32 years after nearly two centuries of rule.

Reform, education and a new middle class

The nineteenth century produced Indian reformers who questioned their own society — and an English-educated class that would soon question British rule.

  • 1828-29Raja Ram Mohan Roy founds the Brahmo Samaj; Sati abolished under Bentinck (1829).
  • 1835Macaulay's Minute — English becomes the medium of higher education.
  • 1854-57Wood's Despatch; universities at Calcutta, Bombay and Madras (1857).
  • 1856Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act, after Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar's campaign.
  • 1873-75Jyotiba Phule's Satyashodhak Samaj (1873); Dayananda's Arya Samaj and Sayyid Ahmad Khan's Aligarh college (1875).
  • 1897Swami Vivekananda founds the Ramakrishna Mission.

Interpretation English education was meant to create loyal intermediaries. It also gave Indians the vocabulary of liberty, representation and nationhood — and a shared language across regions in which to organise.

1857 and the Crown Raj

The largest armed challenge to British rule in the nineteenth century. It failed, but it ended Company rule.

Causes

  • Trigger: Enfield rifle cartridges rumoured to be greased with cow and pig fat
  • Political: annexations under the Doctrine of Lapse and of Awadh
  • Economic: heavy land revenue; dispossessed taluqdars
  • Military: pay, overseas service, racial discrimination

Course

  • Mangal Pandey at Barrackpore (29 March 1857); rising at Meerut, 10 May 1857
  • Bahadur Shah Zafar proclaimed emperor at Delhi
  • Leaders: Nana Saheb and Tantia Tope (Kanpur), Rani Lakshmibai (Jhansi), Begum Hazrat Mahal (Lucknow), Kunwar Singh (Bihar)
  • Delhi retaken September 1857; resistance crushed by 1858-59

Why it failed: limited to north and central India; no unified leadership or programme; Punjab, Bengal and the south largely stayed out; British control of communications and resources.

Consequences: the Government of India Act 1858 transferred power to the Crown; the Queen's Proclamation (1 November 1858) promised no more annexations; the army was reorganised around “martial races”; the British allied with princes and landlords.

Mutiny or first war of independence? Interpretation V. D. Savarkar called it the First War of Independence (1909); many British writers called it a mutiny. A fair answer: it began as a military revolt and became a wider popular uprising in several regions, but it did not yet have a national programme.

Part III

The national movement

Early nationalism and the Congress

From petitions to a permanent national organisation.

  • Roots: the press, English education, the economic critique, and racial humiliation — the Ilbert Bill controversy (1883) showed that Europeans would not accept Indian judges trying them.
  • Indian National Congress founded in Bombay on 28 December 1885; 72 delegates; A. O. Hume a key organiser; W. C. Bonnerjee presided.
  • Moderates (Naoroji, Gokhale, Pherozeshah Mehta) believed in constitutional methods — petitions, speeches, reports.
  • Extremists (Tilak, Lajpat Rai, Bipin Chandra Pal — “Lal-Bal-Pal”) demanded self-rule and mass action. Tilak: “Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it.”
Were the Moderates a failure?

Interpretation They won few concessions, but they built the economic case against colonial rule and a national platform that every later leader used. Dismissing them misses how movements are built.

Bengal partition, Swadeshi and the revolutionaries

  • 1905Curzon partitions Bengal (effective 16 October). The Swadeshi movement — boycott of British goods, national education — is the first mass movement.
  • 1906All-India Muslim League founded at Dacca (30 December).
  • 1907Congress splits at Surat between Moderates and Extremists.
  • 1908Tilak sentenced to six years; Khudiram Bose executed.
  • 1909Morley-Minto reforms introduce separate electorates for Muslims.
  • 1911Partition of Bengal annulled; capital shifted from Calcutta to Delhi.
  • 1913Ghadar Party formed by Indians in North America.

Divide and rule in the government's own words. Established Home Secretary H. H. Risley's 1904 note stated that Bengal united was a power and Bengal divided would pull in different ways — the political purpose of partition, in an official record.

The First World War and Jallianwala Bagh

India gave men and money to the war and expected self-government in return. It got the Rowlatt Act.

  • Over a million Indians served overseas; tens of thousands died.
  • Lucknow Pact (1916) — Congress and the League agree on joint demands; the Congress reunites.
  • Home Rule Leagues (1916) of Tilak and Annie Besant.
  • Montagu Declaration (20 August 1917) promises “progressive realisation of responsible government”.
  • Rowlatt Act (March 1919) — detention without trial. Gandhi calls a nationwide hartal.
  • Jallianwala Bagh, Amritsar, 13 April 1919 — General Dyer's troops fire on an unarmed crowd. Disputed Official toll 379; Indian estimates around 1,000. Tagore renounced his knighthood.
  • Government of India Act 1919 (Montagu-Chelmsford) introduces dyarchy in the provinces.
The turning point

Jallianwala Bagh, and the British public's praise for Dyer afterwards, destroyed faith in British justice among moderate Indians. It turned a petitioning movement into a mass one.

Gandhi and Non-Cooperation

Gandhi returned from South Africa in 1915 with a method — satyagraha — and tested it before taking it national.

  • 1917-18Champaran (indigo peasants), Kheda (revenue relief) and Ahmedabad (mill workers).
  • 1920The Non-Cooperation Movement, joined with the Khilafat cause: boycott of councils, courts, schools and foreign cloth; khadi and national schools.
  • Feb 1922After a mob kills policemen at Chauri Chaura, Gandhi suspends the movement.
  • 1923C. R. Das and Motilal Nehru form the Swaraj Party to contest council elections.

What Non-Cooperation achieved

  • Turned Congress into a mass party with a village presence
  • Brought peasants, workers, women and Muslims into national politics
  • Removed the fear of British authority

What it did not

  • Swaraj “within one year” was not achieved
  • The suspension frustrated many younger leaders
  • The Khilafat alliance collapsed after 1924

Purna Swaraj, Dandi and the 1935 Act

  • 1928The all-white Simon Commission is boycotted; Lala Lajpat Rai dies after a police lathi charge. The Nehru Report proposes dominion status.
  • Dec 1929Lahore Congress under Jawaharlal Nehru declares Purna Swaraj; 26 January 1930 celebrated as Independence Day.
  • 1930Dandi March, 12 March - 6 April: Gandhi breaks the salt law. The Civil Disobedience Movement follows.
  • 1931Gandhi-Irwin Pact (5 March); Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev and Rajguru hanged (23 March); Gandhi attends the Second Round Table Conference.
  • 1932The Communal Award gives separate electorates to Depressed Classes; Gandhi fasts; the Poona Pact with Ambedkar substitutes reserved seats.
  • 1935Government of India Act: provincial autonomy and a proposed federation (never implemented). Much of it later shaped the Constitution.
  • 1937Provincial elections: Congress forms ministries in most provinces; the Muslim League does poorly.
Why salt?

Everyone used it, the tax fell on the poorest, and breaking the law needed only seawater. It made civil disobedience something every Indian could join — the genius of the choice was its simplicity.

Part IV

War, endgame, Partition

Quit India, Bose and the INA

The Second World War broke the old bargain. Britain needed India badly and could no longer afford to hold it.

  • Sep 1939Viceroy Linlithgow declares India at war without consulting Indian leaders. Congress ministries resign.
  • Mar 1940Muslim League's Lahore Resolution (23 March) demands independent states for Muslim-majority areas.
  • Mar 1942The Cripps Mission offers dominion status after the war, with a right for provinces to opt out. Rejected.
  • Aug 1942Quit India (8 August, Bombay): “Do or Die”. Leaders arrested overnight; an underground, often violent, rising follows and is suppressed.
  • 1943Bengal famine: about 2-3 million dead. Disputed Historians disagree on how far wartime policy caused it.
  • Oct 1943Subhas Chandra Bose proclaims the Azad Hind government in Singapore (21 October) and leads the INA.
  • 1944The INA fights alongside the Japanese at Imphal and Kohima and is defeated.
  • Aug 1945Bose reported killed in an air crash at Taipei (18 August). Disputed Some have long questioned this; official inquiries have largely accepted it.

The other Indian army. About 2.5 million Indians served in the British Indian Army — the largest volunteer army in history. That army, not the INA, fought at Kohima and in Burma, North Africa and Italy.

1945-47: the endgame

In eighteen months, Britain moved from planning a long transition to leaving early.

  • Nov 1945INA trials at the Red Fort spark nationwide protests; in January 1946 Auchinleck commutes the sentences.
  • Feb 1946Royal Indian Navy mutiny in Bombay (from 18 February), with sympathy strikes.
  • 1946Elections: Congress sweeps general seats; the League wins nearly all Muslim seats.
  • May 1946Cabinet Mission Plan (16 May): a loose three-tier federation. Both sides accept, then it unravels.
  • Aug 1946League's Direct Action Day (16 August): the Great Calcutta Killings. Violence spreads to Noakhali and Bihar.
  • Feb 1947Attlee announces (20 February) that Britain will leave by June 1948.
  • Jun 1947Mountbatten's 3 June Plan: Partition, with the transfer brought forward to August.
  • Jul-Aug 1947Indian Independence Act (18 July). Independence on 14-15 August. The Radcliffe boundaries are published on 17 August — after independence.
Why 1946 mattered

Interpretation The INA trials and the naval mutiny showed that the Indian armed forces — the final guarantee of British rule — could no longer be relied on. Combined with Britain's post-war bankruptcy, this turned “when” into “now”.

Partition and the princely states

The human cost

Disputed Estimates of deaths range from about 200,000 to 2 million; some 10-20 million people were displaced. Punjab and Bengal were divided. Quote ranges, not a single figure.

Integrating the states

About 565 princely states covered some two-fifths of the subcontinent. Paramountcy lapsed in 1947. Sardar Patel and V. P. Menon persuaded almost all to sign the Instrument of Accession (defence, foreign affairs, communications) before 15 August.

The three difficult cases
State Problem Outcome
Junagadh Muslim ruler, Hindu majority, acceded to Pakistan Indian action; plebiscite in February 1948 favoured India.
Hyderabad Nizam sought independence; Razakar violence Operation Polo, September 1948.
Jammu & Kashmir Hindu ruler, Muslim majority; tribal invasion from Pakistan Accession to India on 26 October 1947; the first India-Pakistan war.
Was Partition inevitable?

Interpretation Not in 1940, and arguably not until the Cabinet Mission Plan collapsed in mid-1946. Between July 1946 and March 1947, violence and mutual distrust made it very hard to avoid. Ayesha Jalal argues Jinnah used the Pakistan demand partly as a bargaining position.

Part V

Knowledge toolkit

Debates, controversies, lesser-known facts

Why did the British leave?

Give three reasons, not one: consent was gone after decades of mass movements; capacity was gone after a war that left Britain bankrupt; and the army, the last guarantee, was no longer certain. Interpretation Nationalist, Cambridge-school and Marxist historians weight these differently.

Main schools of history
School Main claim
Imperialist British rule brought order and modernisation; nationalism was an elite movement.
Nationalist A united people struggled against economic exploitation and won.
Cambridge Indian politics was driven by local elites competing for patronage.
Marxist Class interests shaped both colonial rule and the national movement.
Subaltern Peasants, tribals and workers acted on their own terms, not just as followers.

Controversies to handle carefully

  • Gandhi vs Bose: different methods, shared goal. Gandhi built the mass base; Bose's INA shook the army's loyalty in 1945-46. Both mattered.
  • Economic impact: exploitation versus failure to develop. Stick to agreed facts — low growth, famines and low literacy.
  • Partition responsibility: Jinnah, Congress and the British each carry some. Avoid assigning it to one actor alone.

Lesser-known facts

  • Only about 4.8% of Muslim votes went to the League in 1937; by 1946 it was 86.6%. The Pakistan demand gained mass support in less than a decade.
  • Ambedkar, Periyar and Phule challenged caste from outside the Congress mainstream — the freedom struggle was also a struggle inside Indian society.
  • Women led in all phases: Sarojini Naidu, Kasturba Gandhi, Aruna Asaf Ali (who hoisted the flag in August 1942), Captain Lakshmi Sahgal of the INA.

Counterfactuals and legacy

If the Cabinet Mission had held

A united but weak federation might have avoided Partition — or dissolved later in worse violence. Interpretation

If 1857 had succeeded

Likely a return to regional powers under a restored Mughal name, not a modern nation-state. Interpretation

What independent India inherited

  • Institutions: the civil service, the army, courts and a parliamentary system; the 1935 Act shaped much of the Constitution.
  • Ideas: non-violence, secularism, universal franchise and a civic, not religious, idea of the nation.
  • Problems: poverty, low literacy, Kashmir, refugees and the India-Pakistan rivalry.
The line for an interview

The freedom struggle's biggest achievement was not only independence but the method — a mass, largely non-violent, inclusive movement that trained Indians in democracy before they had a democracy.

Practice questions

Answer each aloud in two minutes, using the five-move method.

  1. How did a trading company become the ruler of India?
  2. Why was Plassey important, and why was Buxar more important?
  3. What was the drain theory, and is it still valid?
  4. Was 1857 a mutiny or a war of independence?
  5. Were the Moderates a failure?
  6. Why did Jallianwala Bagh change Indian politics?
  7. Why did Gandhi call off Non-Cooperation? Was he right?
  8. Why did Gandhi choose salt?
  9. Gandhi or Bose — whose method did more to win independence?
  10. Why did the British leave in 1947 and not earlier or later?
  11. Was Partition inevitable?
  12. How did Patel integrate the princely states?
  13. What did the British leave behind that still shapes India?
  14. Which lesser-known figure of the freedom struggle would you highlight, and why?

Debate motions

  1. Non-violence won India its freedom.
  2. The British Raj did more harm than good to India.
  3. Partition was the price of independence.
  4. The INA did more to end British rule than Quit India.
  5. History should be taught from many viewpoints, not one national story.
  6. Colonial institutions still help India more than they hold it back.
  7. The freedom struggle's ideals are still relevant to young Indians.

Glossary

Diwani
The right to collect revenue and administer civil justice; granted to the Company for Bengal, Bihar and Orissa in 1765.
Subsidiary Alliance
Wellesley's system: a ruler paid for a British garrison, accepted a Resident and surrendered foreign policy.
Doctrine of Lapse
Dalhousie's rule that a dependent state without a natural heir passed to the Company.
Paramountcy
The Crown's overriding authority over princely states; it lapsed in 1947.
Drain of wealth
Naoroji's term for the transfer of Indian resources to Britain without return.
Home Charges
India's annual payments to Britain for debt, pensions, administration and military costs.
Dyarchy
The 1919 split of provincial subjects between Indian ministers and British governors.
Separate electorates
Seats where only voters of one community voted; introduced 1909, abolished 1950.
Satyagraha
“Holding firmly to truth” — Gandhi's non-violent resistance.
Swadeshi / Swaraj
Use of Indian goods / self-rule.
Purna Swaraj
Complete independence; Congress goal from December 1929.
Hartal
A complete stoppage of work and business as protest.
Ryot / Zamindar
Cultivator / revenue-collecting landholder.
Instrument of Accession
The legal document by which a princely state joined India or Pakistan.
