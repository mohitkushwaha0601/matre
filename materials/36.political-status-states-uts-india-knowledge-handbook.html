Part I · The constitutional framework

A Union of States, and the Territories beside them

Snapshot and how to read

India is described by Article 1 as a "Union of States". In 2026 it has 28 states and 8 Union Territories (UTs). States have their own elected governments and a constitutionally protected share of powers. UTs are administered by the Centre through an administrator, with a few having elected assemblies. The line between the two has moved many times since 1947, and several disputes of today, from Jammu and Kashmir to Delhi and Ladakh, are about where that line should run.

A Union of States

Article 1 says: "India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States." The word "Union" was chosen deliberately. Dr B. R. Ambedkar explained in the Constituent Assembly that the Indian federation is not the result of an agreement between states, and so no state has the right to secede from it. The Union is indestructible, while states are not: Parliament may change their areas, boundaries and names. Established

Scholars have called this a "quasi-federal" system, a federation with a strong centre. The Supreme Court in S. R. Bommai v Union of India (1994) held that federalism is part of the Constitution's basic structure, which means it cannot be amended away. Established In practice the Union and states are expected to cooperate, and the balance has shifted between periods of strong central dominance and strong regional parties.

Exam wording

"Union of States" is not the same as "federation of states". The first stresses that the Union is permanent and states cannot leave; the second might suggest a compact between sovereigns. If asked why the framers chose the word, use Ambedkar's reasoning.

How states are made and changed

Articles 2-4 let Parliament admit or establish new states, form new states by separating territory, merge or divide states, and change names and boundaries. Under Article 4 these laws are not constitutional amendments, so a simple majority is enough. The President must refer the bill to the affected state legislature for its views, which Parliament need not accept. Established By contrast, ceding Indian territory to another country needs a constitutional amendment (the Berubari reference, 1960).

  • 1947-50Integration of princely states. At the 1950 Constitution, the units were grouped as Part A, B, C and D states.
  • 1953Andhra State carved from Madras after Potti Sriramulu's death in a fast for a Telugu-speaking state.
  • 1956States Reorganisation Act, following the Fazl Ali commission, creates 14 states and 6 UTs on largely linguistic lines.
  • 1960Bombay split into Maharashtra and Gujarat.
  • 1963-66Nagaland (1963); Haryana carved from Punjab (1966).
  • 1971-75Himachal Pradesh (1971); Manipur, Tripura and Meghalaya (1972); Sikkim joins (1975).
  • 1987Mizoram, Arunachal Pradesh and Goa become states.
  • 2000Chhattisgarh, Uttarakhand and Jharkhand created.
  • 2014Telangana separated from Andhra Pradesh (the 29th state at that time).
  • 2019Jammu and Kashmir state becomes two UTs.

Names have also changed: Madras to Tamil Nadu (1969), Mysore to Karnataka (1973), Uttaranchal to Uttarakhand (2007), Orissa to Odisha (2011). Re-verify state counts and ordinals

Union Territories: why and how

Union Territories are governed directly by the Union under Articles 239-241. The President acts through an administrator, called a Lieutenant Governor in Delhi, Puducherry, Jammu and Kashmir, and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, and an Administrator elsewhere. Article 240 lets the President make regulations for several UTs, and Article 241 lets Parliament set up a High Court for a UT or attach it to an existing one.

  • Strategic or border location: Ladakh, the Andaman and Nicobar Islands and Lakshadweep.
  • Distinct cultural history: Puducherry, a former French colony transferred to India in 1954 and formally in 1962. Re-verify dates
  • Capital function: Delhi and Chandigarh, the shared capital of Punjab and Haryana.
  • Small size or special needs: Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu, which were merged into one UT in 2020.

Delhi: a special case

Delhi is a UT with an elected government under Article 239AA, but police, public order and land remain with the Centre. The Lieutenant Governor and the elected government have clashed repeatedly. In 2018 a Constitution Bench held that the Lieutenant Governor must generally act on the advice of the council of ministers, and in 2023 another bench held that the elected government controls "services" apart from police, public order and land. Parliament then passed a 2023 law that restored central control over services through an authority, which has been challenged. Re-verify the status of the challenge

Why Chandigarh is awkward

Chandigarh is the capital of both Punjab and Haryana and a UT. Punjab claims it, and Haryana wants a separate capital. The long-pending 1985 transfer promise has never been executed, so any change to its administration becomes a political issue. Interpretation

Special provisions: Article 371

The Constitution gives certain states special arrangements under Articles 371 to 371J, usually to protect tribal customs, address regional imbalance or reflect a political settlement. They are often confused with Article 370, which applied only to Jammu and Kashmir and has been abrogated.

370 versus 371

Article 370 was a temporary provision giving Jammu and Kashmir its own constitution and wide autonomy. Article 371 and its sub-articles are permanent, narrower and targeted. The abrogation of 370 in 2019 did not affect 371.

Jammu and Kashmir: Article 370 and after

Jammu and Kashmir acceded to India in October 1947. Article 370 gave the state its own constitution, a flag and a limited list of Union subjects, and Article 35A (1954) reserved rights for "permanent residents". On 5 August 2019 the President issued an order under Article 370 itself that applied the whole Constitution to the state, and Parliament passed the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act 2019, which from 31 October 2019 created two UTs: Jammu and Kashmir (with a legislature) and Ladakh (without). Established The security background is in handbook 7.

  • Supreme Court, December 2023: a five-judge Constitution Bench upheld the abrogation. It held that Article 370 was a temporary provision, that Jammu and Kashmir had no internal sovereignty after accession, and directed that statehood be restored "as soon as possible" and elections be held. Re-verify details of the ruling
  • Elections in 2024: the first assembly election since 2014 was held in 2024, and a National Conference-led government took office. Re-verify
  • Present arrangement: as a UT, the Lieutenant Governor retains control over police, public order and certain services, and the elected government's powers are narrower than those of a state. Re-verify
  • Statehood: the Union government has said statehood will be restored at an appropriate time, but no date has been set as of my latest information. Disputed
Two readings

Supporters say the change ended a temporary arrangement, integrated the region and improved security and development. Critics say it downgraded a state to a UT without the assembly's consent and that autonomy was a basis of accession. The Court's answer on legality does not settle the political debate. The Northeast

The eight Northeast states (Arunachal Pradesh, Assam, Manipur, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Nagaland, Sikkim and Tripura) share a narrow land link to the rest of India and borders with China, Myanmar, Bangladesh, Bhutan and Nepal. Their political status is shaped by tribal identity, history of insurgency and a series of agreements that gave some of them special constitutional arrangements.

  • Inner Line Permit (ILP): a travel document under a colonial-era regulation that restricts entry of outsiders, now in force in Arunachal Pradesh, Mizoram, Nagaland and Manipur. Re-verify the list
  • AFSPA: the Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act has been withdrawn from some states, such as Tripura and Meghalaya, and reduced in parts of Assam, Manipur and Nagaland. It remains a point of dispute. Re-verify current coverage
  • Nagaland: a ceasefire with the NSCN (IM) dates from 1997, and a Framework Agreement was signed in 2015, but a final settlement has not been reached. Article 371A protects Naga customary law.
  • Mizoram: the 1986 Mizo Accord ended an insurgency and led to statehood in 1987, and is often cited as a model.
  • Manipur: ethnic violence between Meitei and Kuki-Zo communities from May 2023 led to a long crisis and, in 2025, President's Rule. Re-verify current status
  • Boundary disputes between Assam and its neighbours (Meghalaya, Mizoram, Arunachal Pradesh, Nagaland) have caused clashes and attempts at settlement.
  • Institutions: the North Eastern Council (1971) and a dedicated Ministry for the Development of the North Eastern Region coordinate development.
For SSB answers

The region combines geography (the Siliguri Corridor), identity politics and border security. Explain both the security and the political-settlement parts rather than treating the Northeast as only a security problem. Interpretation

Residuary powers rest with Parliament (Article 248). Parliament may legislate on a State List subject in the national interest if the Rajya Sabha passes a resolution by a two-thirds majority (Article 249), during an emergency (Article 250), by agreement of states (Article 252) or to implement a treaty (Article 253). Established Education was moved from the State to the Concurrent List by the 42nd Amendment in 1976.

  • Finance: the Finance Commission (Article 280), set up every five years, recommends how central taxes are shared. The 15th Commission recommended a 41% share for states, and a 16th Commission, chaired by Arvind Panagariya, covers the period from 2026-27. Re-verify its recommendations
  • GST Council (Article 279A): a joint body where the Centre holds one-third of the weight and states two-thirds, and decisions need a three-fourths majority, so neither side can act alone. Established
  • Other forums: the Inter-State Council (Article 263), zonal councils and NITI Aayog, which replaced the Planning Commission in 2015.
  • Grievances: some states argue that cesses and surcharges outside the shared pool and conditional schemes reduce their share, while others note that richer states contribute more than they receive. Disputed

Centre-state relations in practice

Law sets the framework, but practice depends on who governs where and on how Governors and central agencies behave.

  • The Governor: appointed by the President on the Centre's advice, the Governor is a constitutional head who acts mostly on ministers' advice, but has discretion in limited cases, such as inviting a leader to form a government in a hung assembly. Disputes between opposition-ruled states and Governors, for instance in Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Punjab and West Bengal, have been frequent.
  • Assent to Bills (Articles 200-201): in 2025 the Supreme Court in a Tamil Nadu case laid down time limits for Governors and the President to act on state Bills, and the President then sought the Court's opinion on the issue. The Court's later opinion is understood to have taken a more cautious line on timelines. Re-verify the exact holdings
  • President's Rule (Article 356): used many times in the first decades, often for political reasons. S. R. Bommai (1994) made proclamations subject to judicial review and required a floor test, which sharply reduced misuse. Established
  • Commissions: the Sarkaria Commission (1983-88) and the Punchhi Commission (2007-10) reviewed Centre-state relations and urged consultation and restraint.
  • Agencies and funds: disputes also arise over central investigating agencies, disaster relief, GST compensation and centrally sponsored schemes.
Competitive and cooperative federalism

"Cooperative federalism" describes joint forums such as the GST Council and NITI Aayog. "Competitive federalism" means states competing for investment and ranking. Critics say that when one party dominates both levels, the Centre tends to lead, and when opposition parties govern states, tension rises. Interpretation

Part III · Changing status and live issues

Statehood, delimitation, simultaneous elections and language

Statehood demands and how status changes

Under Articles 2 to 4, Parliament can admit or establish new states, form new states out of existing ones, alter boundaries or change the name of a state by ordinary law. The affected state's legislature is only consulted, and its views are not binding. This is why India is described as an "indestructible union of destructible states". Established Telangana became the 29th state in 2014 under the Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act, with Hyderabad as a shared capital for a transition period. Later, Jammu and Kashmir showed that a state can also be turned into UTs. Re-verify the transition end date

Census, delimitation and the seat freeze

Article 82 requires readjustment of seats after each census. The 42nd Amendment (1976) froze the allocation of seats among states to the 1971 census, and the 84th Amendment (2001) extended that until the first census after 2026. This was done so that states that reduced population growth would not lose seats. Established

  • The next census: a census with 1 March 2027 as the reference date was announced, and it is to include caste enumeration, the first such count since 1931 for all castes. Re-verify dates and phases
  • Women's reservation: the Constitution (106th Amendment) Act, 2023, the Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam, reserves one-third of Lok Sabha and assembly seats for women, but only after the next census and delimitation. Established
  • The southern concern: if seats are redistributed on current population, southern states with lower fertility could lose their share compared to northern states. Several southern leaders have demanded safeguards, and Tamil Nadu convened a meeting of parties on the subject in 2025. Re-verify
  • Capacity: the new Parliament building (2023) has seating for 888 members in the Lok Sabha and 384 in the Rajya Sabha, so a larger house is feasible. Re-verify
The core tension

Equal representation of citizens ("one person, one vote") conflicts with fairness to states that succeeded in population control. Options discussed include a proportional increase for all states, weighting, or a freeze for a further period. Interpretation

One Nation, One Election

The proposal is to hold Lok Sabha and state assembly elections together. A high-level committee chaired by former President Ram Nath Kovind (formed 2023) reported in March 2024 and recommended synchronised elections in stages, with local body elections following within 100 days. In December 2024 the Union government introduced the Constitution (129th Amendment) Bill and a connected Union Territories Laws Bill, which were referred to a Joint Parliamentary Committee. Re-verify the present status

Arguments in favour

  • Lower cost and fewer Model Code of Conduct periods.
  • Less disruption to governance and security deployments.
  • Policy continuity.

Concerns

  • Shortening the terms of state assemblies could weaken federal autonomy.
  • Handling of mid-term collapses of governments.
  • National issues may overshadow state issues.
  • Large logistics and constitutional changes may need state ratification, which is disputed.

Language and federal politics

India has no single "national language" in the Constitution. Article 343 makes Hindi in Devanagari script the official language of the Union, with English continuing as an associate official language under the Official Languages Act 1963. The Eighth Schedule lists 22 languages. States choose their own official languages, and some have used the status of a "classical language" (now given to a number of languages such as Tamil, Sanskrit, Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam, Odia, Marathi, Pali, Prakrit, Assamese and Bengali) as a cultural mark. Re-verify the classical list

  • History: anti-Hindi agitations in Madras in 1937-40 and 1965 shaped Tamil Nadu politics and the continued use of English.
  • Three-language formula: proposed in the 1960s and again in the National Education Policy 2020, it asks for the mother tongue or regional language, Hindi or another language, and English. Tamil Nadu follows a two-language policy and has opposed the formula.
  • Funding link: in 2025 the Centre and Tamil Nadu disputed release of education funds tied to adopting the policy. Re-verify the present state
  • States' reorganisation: after 1956, states were largely formed on linguistic lines, so language and state identity are closely linked.

Perspectives, controversies and facts

Interpretation Is India federal? Scholars call India "quasi-federal" because the Centre can create or reshape states, appoints Governors and holds residuary powers. Others point out that states have real power over many subjects, their own elected governments and a say in the GST Council, so the system is federal in practice. Disputed

Interpretation UT or state? A UT gives the Centre direct control, which suits strategic or small territories. Critics say that elected governments in Delhi, Puducherry and Jammu and Kashmir are denied full powers.

Interpretation Asymmetric federalism. Special arrangements in Articles 371 to 371J and the Sixth Schedule show that India accommodates diversity differently across regions, though some argue the pattern is inconsistent.

Interpretation Governors. Defenders see them as a constitutional link to the Centre, and critics see them as a tool of central politics.

Scenarios and strategic options

Presented neutrally, without recommending a political position:

Jammu and Kashmir regains statehood

Policing, public order and local control return to an elected government. Supporters see restored trust, while others worry about security coordination.

Delimitation on present population

Seats move toward more populous northern states. Southern states raise fears of reduced voice and resist.

Disputes over services and the Lieutenant Governor recur when the two sides are in different parties' hands. Court rulings and Acts of Parliament pull in different directions.

A Governor holds back a state Bill

Answer framework and worked example

For any question on political status, move through definition, location, evidence, contest, analysis, test, position, concession and close. One compressed example follows.

Worked example: "Is India truly a federal country?"

Define
A federation divides powers between levels of government, each with its own sphere, under a written constitution and independent courts.
Locate
Articles 1-4 (Union), the Seventh Schedule (division of powers), Articles 245-263 (relations) and Articles 268-293 (finance).
Establish
States have their own elected governments and exclusive subjects, courts police the boundary (Bommai, 1994), and the GST Council is shared by design.
Contest
The Centre can alter state boundaries and names by ordinary law, appoints Governors, can impose President's Rule, and holds residuary powers.
Analyse
The text is centralised to keep a diverse nation together after Partition, but practice has become more federal since the 1990s because of coalitions, regional parties and the courts.
Test
Look at how often Article 356 is used, how disputes over Governors and finances are resolved, and whether the GST Council works by consensus.
Position
India is a federation with a strong Centre, best described as "federal with unitary features", and recent trends show both strain and adaptation.
Concede
Judgements on practice depend on which party governs where, and recent court rulings are still being worked out. Re-verify
Close
Federalism in India is a negotiated balance, and its health shows in how disputes are settled, not only in the text.

Glossary

Administrator
The head of a UT without a Lieutenant Governor title.
Asymmetric federalism
Different states or regions having different constitutional arrangements.
Autonomous District Council
A body in a Sixth Schedule area with powers over local matters.
Concurrent List
Subjects on which both Parliament and states can legislate.
Delimitation
Redrawing constituencies and reallocating seats.
Divisible pool
Central tax revenue that is shared with states.
Governor
The constitutional head of a state, appointed by the President.
Inner Line Permit
A permit for outsiders to enter certain Northeast states.
Lieutenant Governor
The head of some UTs, appointed by the President.
PESA
Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996.
President's Rule
Central takeover of a state under Article 356.
Residuary power
Power over subjects not listed anywhere, held by Parliament.
Scheduled Area
A tribal area under the Fifth Schedule.
Special category status
A planning-era classification giving some states extra central aid; not constitutional.
Union Territory
A unit administered by the Union under Articles 239-241.
