Snapshot and how to read

Oceans cover about seven-tenths of the Earth, regulate climate, carry most of world trade and hold resources that states are only beginning to claim. India has a long coastline, two island territories, a monsoon driven by the Indian Ocean, a growing blue economy and a stated aim of being a "net security provider" in the region. This handbook covers the oceans in general and India's role in particular.

Part I · The world's oceans

Geography, science, law and resources

Ocean geography, currents and chokepoints

The oceans hold about 97% of Earth's water, with an average depth near 3,700 m. Seafloor features include continental shelves, mid-ocean ridges, abyssal plains and trenches. A global conveyor of surface and deep currents moves heat: the Gulf Stream and Kuroshio carry warm water poleward, the Humboldt carries cold water along South America, and the Antarctic Circumpolar Current circles the pole.

The Indian Ocean is unusual because it is closed to the north by the Asian landmass. Its winds reverse seasonally, blowing from the south-west in summer and the north-east in winter, and its surface currents (such as the Somali Current) reverse with them. This is the basis of the monsoon and of historic sail-powered trade. Established

Ocean science: monsoon, cyclones, tsunamis, climate

  • Monsoon link: the temperature contrast between land and the Indian Ocean drives the monsoon, which supplies most of India's annual rainfall.
  • El Niño and the Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD): El Niño (warm Pacific) generally weakens the Indian monsoon; a positive IOD (warm west, cool east Indian Ocean) can offset that effect. Forecasters watch both. Interpretation: the relationship is not perfectly reliable
  • Cyclones: the Bay of Bengal produces more cyclones than the Arabian Sea, and impacts are heavy on the Odisha, Andhra and Bengal coasts. The India Meteorological Department (IMD) is the regional specialised centre for the North Indian Ocean.
  • Tsunami: the 26 December 2004 Sumatra earthquake generated a tsunami that killed hundreds of thousands across the Indian Ocean. India then built the Indian Tsunami Early Warning Centre at INCOIS Hyderabad. Re-verify toll and dates
  • Climate: the oceans absorb most of the excess heat and a large share of the CO2 from human activity. The results are sea-level rise, acidification, marine heatwaves and coral bleaching. Re-verify magnitudes
Why it matters for India

Ocean science is not an academic extra. It feeds monsoon forecasts, cyclone warnings, fishing advisories and coastal planning, and it shapes decisions for low-lying coasts such as the Sundarbans and the Lakshadweep atolls.

The law of the sea: UNCLOS, EEZ, seabed and high seas

The United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS, 1982) came into force in 1994. India ratified it in 1995. It sets out zones measured from baselines:

  • India's law: the Territorial Waters, Continental Shelf, Exclusive Economic Zone and Other Maritime Zones Act, 1976.
  • Boundary cases: the 2014 arbitration between Bangladesh and India in the Bay of Bengal settled their maritime boundary, and the 2016 arbitration between the Philippines and China on the South China Sea rejected China's historic-rights claim inside the nine-dash line. China does not accept that ruling. Disputed
  • High seas treaty: the Agreement on Biodiversity Beyond National Jurisdiction (BBNJ, adopted 2023) provides for marine protected areas on the high seas. Re-verify entry into force and India's status
  • India's reading: India holds that other states should seek its consent for military exercises in its EEZ, which differs from the more permissive reading of some maritime powers. Interpretation Re-verify

Ocean resources, trade and undersea cables

  • Trade: about four-fifths of world trade by volume moves by sea. India carries most of its trade by volume and a large share by value on ships. Re-verify shares
  • Fisheries: a major source of protein and jobs, but many stocks are overfished. India is among the largest fish producers and exporters, with strong shrimp exports. Re-verify Subsidy rules for fisheries have been negotiated at the WTO. Re-verify status
  • Energy and minerals: offshore oil and gas (India's Bombay High and Krishna-Godavari basin), offshore wind, and seabed minerals such as polymetallic nodules, sulphides and cobalt-rich crusts. Gas hydrates are a longer-term prospect.
  • Undersea cables: they carry nearly all intercontinental data. Cable cuts and the security of landing stations have become strategic concerns. Re-verify share
  • Pressures: plastic and chemical pollution, oil spills, illegal and unreported fishing, and ship emissions, which the International Maritime Organization (IMO) regulates through MARPOL.
Resource rush, rule gap

Seabed mining of deep nodules is moving from exploration toward possible exploitation while rules at the ISA are still unsettled and some states press ahead nationally. This tests the "common heritage" principle. Interpretation

Part II · India and the oceans

Coast, heritage, economy, science and diplomacy

India's coast, islands and ports

India's coastline is officially about 7,500 km by older measurement and above 11,000 km when islands are included and a finer method is used. Re-verify which figure applies Nine states (Gujarat, Maharashtra, Goa, Karnataka, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Odisha and West Bengal) and several Union Territories touch the sea. Gujarat has the longest state coastline. India's EEZ is roughly 2 million square kilometres or more once island territories are counted. Re-verify

  • Andaman and Nicobar Islands: in the Bay of Bengal, close to the Malacca approaches. Indira Point at Great Nicobar is India's southernmost point. The Ten Degree Channel separates the Andamans from the Nicobars.
  • Lakshadweep and Minicoy: coral atolls in the Arabian Sea, separated from the Maldives by the Eight Degree Channel (south of Minicoy). Re-verify channel names
  • Ports: a dozen-plus major ports under the Centre (Vadhavan in Maharashtra has been approved as an additional deep-water major port) and many non-major ports under states. Re-verify count
  • Transshipment: a large share of India's container transshipment has historically gone through foreign hubs (Colombo, Singapore, Klang). Vizhinjam in Kerala and a planned port at Galathea Bay in Great Nicobar aim to change that. Re-verify project status
Geography as leverage

The Andaman and Nicobar Islands sit near the western mouth of the Malacca Strait, and the Lakshadweep chain sits on the Arabian Sea sea lanes. For a country whose geography points into the Indian Ocean, the islands give surveillance reach and a basing option beyond the mainland. See handbook 10.

Maritime heritage

India's maritime history is old and varied. The Harappan port at Lothal (Gujarat) had a dockyard, and Mesopotamian records mention contact with Meluhha, usually identified with the Indus region. Interpretation Sangam-era Tamil ports such as Muziris traded with the Roman world, and Roman writers describe the use of the monsoon winds.

  • Chola reach: Rajendra I's naval expedition against the Srivijaya empire (c. 1025 CE) is among the best-known Indian overseas campaigns.
  • Kalinga and Bali: the Bali Jatra festival in Cuttack recalls old trade links between Odisha and Southeast Asia.
  • Medieval sea powers: the Zamorin's admirals (the Kunjali Marakkars) opposed the Portuguese, and the Maratha admiral Kanhoji Angre (c. 1698-1729) ran a powerful navy on the western coast. Shivaji also built a navy and sea forts such as Sindhudurg.
  • Colonial turn: from the 16th century, Portuguese, Dutch, French and British naval power shifted control of Indian Ocean trade to Europeans.
  • Strategic writing: K.M. Panikkar's India and the Indian Ocean (1945) argued that India's security depends on the ocean around it. Established

The Indian Navy's own story from independence onward is told in handbook 1.

Shipbuilding and seafarers

India has strong shipyards (Cochin, Mazagon, GRSE, Goa) mostly serving defence and domestic needs, but a small share of global commercial shipbuilding. The government has announced financial support and a maritime development fund to expand this. Indian seafarers are a large share of the global workforce. Re-verify

Ocean science institutions and polar programmes

  • Ministry of Earth Sciences (MoES, 2006): the central ministry for ocean, atmosphere and earth sciences.
  • National Institute of Oceanography (NIO), Goa: a CSIR institute founded in 1966 for basic ocean research.
  • National Institute of Ocean Technology (NIOT), Chennai: develops deep-sea technology, buoys, desalination and submersibles.
  • INCOIS, Hyderabad: ocean information, tsunami and storm-surge warnings, and potential fishing zone advisories.
  • NCPOR, Goa: runs the Antarctic, Arctic and Southern Ocean programmes.
  • Satellites: the Oceansat series and the India-France SARAL/AltiKa mission support ocean colour, wind and altimetry. Re-verify mission details

Antarctica and the Arctic

India's first Antarctic expedition went in 1981-82 under Dr S.Z. Qasim, and India joined the Antarctic Treaty system in 1983. Its stations were Dakshin Gangotri (1983, later abandoned), Maitri (1989) and Bharati (2012). In the Arctic, the Himadri station at Ny-Ålesund, Svalbard opened in 2008, and India has observer status in the Arctic Council (2013). The Indian Antarctic Act of 2022 set up a domestic legal framework, and India published an Arctic policy in 2022. Re-verify dates and new station plans

Maritime diplomacy: SAGAR, MAHASAGAR and groupings

  • SAGAR (2015): "Security and Growth for All in the Region", announced in Mauritius; it frames India as a partner and first responder in the Indian Ocean.
  • MAHASAGAR (2025): a broader vision announced in Mauritius in March 2025 that extends the approach to a wider "Global South" setting. Re-verify the name expansion and content
  • IORA: the Indian Ocean Rim Association, in which India is a founding member, covers trade, fisheries, maritime safety and the blue economy.
  • Colombo Security Conclave: India, Sri Lanka, the Maldives and Mauritius, later widened, cooperate on maritime security. Re-verify membership
  • Information Fusion Centre-IOR: set up by the Navy in Gurugram (2018) to share maritime-domain information with partners.
  • Quad and IPOI: the Quad's maritime-awareness initiative (IPMDA) and India's Indo-Pacific Oceans Initiative (2019) cover security, ecology, resources, disaster risk and science.
  • Infrastructure partnerships: Chabahar (Iran), Sittwe (Myanmar), Duqm (Oman access) and facilities in Mauritius and the Seychelles. Some of these projects have stalled. Re-verify
First responder in the region

The Navy and Coast Guard routinely provide disaster relief, evacuation, anti-piracy patrols and hydrographic help to neighbours. The idea is that goodwill built on practical help strengthens India's standing relative to larger rivals. See handbooks 23 and 24.

India's international role and positions

  • UNCLOS and ISA: India was the first "pioneer investor" in seabed mining (1987) and holds ISA contracts for polymetallic nodules in the Central Indian Ocean Basin and for polymetallic sulphides along the Indian Ocean ridge system. More applications have been made since. Re-verify contract list
  • Stance on rules: India supports rules-based freedom of navigation, calls for respect for UNCLOS, and avoids taking a side on sovereignty questions in the South China Sea.
  • Global South voice: India often links ocean and climate discussions with development, equity and capacity-building for small island and coastal states.
  • Fisheries and livelihoods: in WTO talks India has argued for protection of small-scale fishers. Interpretation
  • UN Ocean Decade: India takes part in the 2021-2030 Decade of Ocean Science for Sustainable Development.
A live bilateral irritant: Palk Bay

Tamil Nadu and Sri Lankan fishers share the waters of the Palk Strait. Arrests of Indian fishers and the status of Katchatheevu (an islet that India recognised as Sri Lankan in the 1974-76 agreements) recur as political issues. Bottom trawling is also a cause of conflict. Disputed Re-verify current developments

Wondering at the sea's colour on a 1921 voyage, he explained it as molecular scattering of light, leading to the Raman effect (1928) and the Nobel Prize (1930). Re-verify

Why Mahan and Corbett matter for SSB

They give two vocabularies for discussing India's own maritime strategy: sea control and fleet power (Mahan), and sea use, denial and joint operations (Corbett). Candidates who can name the idea and apply it to the Indian Ocean often sound more precise than those who speak only in general terms.

Perspectives, controversies and facts

Interpretation Net security provider or regional power. Supporters see India's relief and patrol work as a public good. Critics in the region read it as pursuit of influence, and some neighbours seek balance through other partners.

Interpretation Great Nicobar development. Supporters point to a transshipment port, airport and strategic presence. Critics point to impacts on rainforest, Indigenous communities (Shompen, Nicobarese) and seismic risk. Disputed Re-verify project status and approvals

Interpretation Deep-sea mining. Proponents see critical minerals for batteries and energy transition; scientists warn about unknown ecological damage and slow recovery of deep ecosystems.

Interpretation EEZ military activity. India and several others hold that foreign military exercises in an EEZ need consent; major maritime powers disagree. This difference appears whenever warships operate near India's waters.

Scenarios and strategic options

Presented neutrally, without recommending a political position:

Blue economy takes off

Ports, shipbuilding, offshore energy and fisheries grow together, and India's coastal states gain jobs and exports. The risk lies in coastal ecology and weak enforcement.

Chokepoint or cable disruption

An attack or accident near Hormuz, Bab-el-Mandeb or a major cable cluster would hit energy, trade and data. India would need alternative routes, reserves and partners.

Seabed mining rush

If commercial mining begins before global rules are settled, India must decide whether to enter, wait or press for strict environmental standards.

Climate stress on coasts

More intense cyclones, sea-level rise and erosion strain low-lying coasts and island territories, requiring adaptation and early-warning investment.

Answer framework and worked example

For any "Why do the oceans matter to India?" question, move through definition, location, evidence, contest, analysis, test, position, concession and close. One compressed example follows.

Worked example: "Why is the Indian Ocean central to India's future?"

Define
The third-largest ocean, shaped by monsoon winds and bordered by dozens of states.
Locate
India projects into its centre; the Andaman and Nicobar Islands sit near Malacca, Lakshadweep on the Arabian Sea lanes.
Establish
Most Indian trade by volume moves by sea, the monsoon feeds agriculture, and key chokepoints lie at the ocean's edges.
Contest
Critics say India's land borders absorb most of its security effort, and that the region has other powers with greater reach.
Analyse
Energy, trade, climate and security all pass through the ocean, so land-border concerns do not replace maritime ones.
Test
Check what would happen to trade, energy and relief capacity if sea lanes were disrupted.
Position
The Indian Ocean is a core national interest requiring economic, scientific and security investment together.
Concede
Programme budgets and rankings change and need checking. Re-verify
Close
Prosperity, security and climate resilience all depend on how India uses and protects its ocean space.
