Hampi and the Vijayanagara Empire: Capital, Army, Monuments and Fall

Hampi on the Tungabhadra was the capital of the Vijayanagara Empire, one of the largest cities in the medieval world and now a UNESCO World Heritage Site. This handbook covers the founding, the rulers, the state and army, the Battle of Talikota, the great monuments, the culture, the rediscovery and conservation of the site, and what it means for India's history, with SSB-style practice material.

Using this handbook

Hampi is a place to be read as well as seen: its layout, temples, water systems and ruins record how a medieval empire organised power, faith, trade and war. The handbook takes the history first, then the monuments, then the questions of interpretation and conservation.

Dates and figures follow standard accounts of Vijayanagara history; travellers' numbers and measurements are estimates and are tagged accordingly. Where tradition and evidence differ, as in the Ramayana associations, the text says so.

Key habit

For every monument, ask what it tells you about the ruler who built it, the community that used it, and the state that paid for it. Then link it to a trade, defence or governance idea you can use in an SSB discussion.

Part I · Origins

Land, legend and foundation

Landscape, Pampa and Kishkindha

Hampi lies in the Tungabhadra basin in north-central Karnataka (the Vijayanagara district, formerly part of Bellary). The Tungabhadra bends around a landscape of enormous granite boulders and rocky ridges, which gave the city natural fortification and abundant building stone. The name Hampi is a corruption of Pampa, the old name of the river and of the local goddess Pampa Devi, who is associated with Shiva as Virupaksha.

The area was sacred long before the empire. A nearby tradition identifies it with Kishkindha, the monkey kingdom of the Ramayana, where Rama met Hanuman and Sugriva; Anjanadri hill across the river is held to be Hanuman's birthplace. Tradition These identifications are devotional rather than archaeological, but they explain the site's long pilgrimage life.

Strategic advantages

A perennial river on one side, rocky ridges on the others, and access to the Deccan, the west coast and the Krishna basin.

Earlier history

Inscriptions record a settlement here from the early centuries CE, and the Virupaksha shrine is far older than the empire. Rulers before Vijayanagara had used the nearby Anegundi.

Foundation of the empire, 1336

The Vijayanagara Empire is traditionally dated to 1336, when the brothers Harihara I and Bukka Raya I, of the Sangama family, established their rule on the Tungabhadra. They had served the small Kampili kingdom, which fell to the Delhi Sultanate under Muhammad bin Tughluq, and later they re-established an independent Hindu kingdom. The saint-scholar Vidyaranya is traditionally associated with the founding.

The name Vijayanagara means "City of Victory". The empire grew within decades to control most of the peninsula south of the Krishna, filling the vacuum left by the fall of the Hoysalas, the Kakatiyas and the Pandyas to the Delhi Sultanate and to local rulers.

Origins of the founders

Disputed Scholars differ on whether Harihara and Bukka were originally Kannada-region chieftains in the Hoysala service, Telugu officers from the Kakatiya country, or something else. Traditions that they were Delhi captives who converted back to Hinduism are also debated. The source evidence is thin.

Krishnadevaraya (r. 1509-1529) is the empire's best-known ruler. He defeated the Gajapati of Odisha in campaigns up to about 1518, and the Bijapur Sultan Ismail Adil Shah at Raichur in 1520. Contemporary traveller Domingo Paes praised his energy, generosity and administrative grasp. He was also a Telugu poet, the author of Amuktamalyada, and a patron of Telugu, Kannada, Sanskrit and Tamil.

Why Krishnadevaraya matters

He is the example of a ruler who combined military success, temple building, patronage of letters and an eye for trade, with the Portuguese on the west coast supplying horses.

Part II · State, army and fall

How the empire worked, and how it fell

Administration and economy

The empire was divided into provinces under governors, often royal princes or trusted nobles. Below them, a system of nayakas (the amara-nayaka arrangement) gave military commanders rights to revenue from land in return for maintaining troops. This tied the army to the land and made the empire a coalition of regional strongmen, whose loyalty depended on the centre's strength.

Revenue came from agriculture, taxes on trade and crafts, and temple endowments. Hampi was a trading hub for cotton, spices, pepper, precious stones and horses. West-coast ports such as Bhatkal, Honavar and Mangalore connected the empire to Arab, Persian and Portuguese commerce.

Strength of the model

Local rulers raised troops without a large standing treasury, and the empire's reach exceeded its bureaucracy.

Weakness of the model

Powerful nayakas could become autonomous as the centre weakened, a pattern seen after 1565.

The army, forts and the Deccan wars

The army combined infantry, archers, elephants and cavalry. Cavalry was a standing problem: the peninsula's climate was poor for horses, so animals were imported through the west-coast ports, and Portuguese control of that trade gave them leverage. Vijayanagara rulers also employed Muslim cavalry commanders and borrowed Persian military practice, while their Deccan neighbours used Hindu officers.

The city's defence relied on seven concentric lines of fortification, in the account of the traveller Abdur Razzaq, built into the boulder landscape, with gates, watchtowers and a road network radiating from the capital. The threats were mainly the successor states of the Bahmani Sultanate in the north (Bijapur, Ahmednagar, Golconda, Bidar and Berar), with the Raichur doab, between the Krishna and the Tungabhadra, the main contested zone.

SSB link: defence in depth

Hampi used terrain, layered walls, forward fortresses and a river line. It also depended on a supply chain (horses) controlled by outsiders, a vulnerability that matters in any study of military logistics.

Talikota, 1565, and the sack of Hampi

In January 1565 (traditionally 23 January) the armies of Vijayanagara under the regent Aliya Rama Raya met a combined force of the Deccan sultanates (Bijapur, Ahmednagar, Golconda and Bidar) at Rakkasa-Tangadi, usually called the Battle of Talikota, on the Krishna. Rama Raya had played the sultanates against one another for years; the alliance against him was partly a response to his interference and perceived arrogance.

Vijayanagara forces were reported to be winning until a decisive turn in the battle, in which Rama Raya was captured and killed, and the army broke. The victors then occupied and sacked Hampi for months. The court fled to Penukonda and later Chandragiri, and the Aravidu dynasty continued in a much-reduced form.

Reading Talikota

Interpretation Older nationalist accounts stress a Hindu-Muslim clash. Modern historians emphasise the political context: shifting alliances, mutual interference, and a defeat caused by command failure and the sultanates' combined strength. The city's destruction was thorough, but its causes were political as well as religious.

Re-verify The size of the armies and the exact turning point differ across Persian and Telugu sources.

What the travellers saw

Foreign visitors left descriptions of Hampi at its height. Nicolo Conti (c. 1420) was an Italian merchant; the Persian ambassador Abdur Razzaq (1443) described the royal palace, markets and festival; the Portuguese Domingo Paes (c. 1520-22) and Fernao Nunes wrote the most detailed accounts under Krishnadevaraya and Achyuta Raya.

Part III · The monuments

Reading the city in stone

The royal centre

Hampi's urban core divides into a sacred centre along the river and a royal centre to the south-west, enclosed by walls. The royal centre contains the Mahanavami Dibba, a tall tiered platform where the king watched the Mahanavami (Dasara) festival with parades, dancers and animals; the Queen's Bath; the Hazara Rama Temple, a private royal temple whose walls carry scenes from the Ramayana; and the Lotus Mahal and Elephant Stables.

The Lotus Mahal and Elephant Stables show a blend of Hindu and Indo-Islamic forms, with arches and domes beside Hindu motifs, an example of the cultural exchange that the court's cosmopolitan military and trade life produced.

Mahanavami Dibba

Ceremonial platform for the nine-day royal festival; also called the Dasara Dibba.

Lotus Mahal

Two-storeyed pavilion with lotus-bud arches, probably a part of the zenana enclosure.

Elephant Stables

Eleven domed chambers for the state elephants, with a central hall for musicians.

Disputed Many names (such as "Queen's Bath" or "Zenana Enclosure") are colonial-era labels whose original functions are uncertain.

The sacred centre and Virupaksha

The Virupaksha Temple, dedicated to Shiva as consort of Pampa Devi, is the oldest and the only major temple at Hampi that has remained in continuous worship. It predates the empire, was expanded under the Vijayanagara kings and was enlarged with a tall eastern gopura, usually described as about 50 metres. Re-verify Heights differ between sources.

Nearby, Hemakuta Hill holds early temples and gateways; Matanga Hill offers the best view over the ruins; the Krishna Temple was built by Krishnadevaraya after his Odisha campaign; and monolithic sculptures include the Ugra Narasimha and the giant Ganesha statues (Kadalekalu and Sasivekalu).

What the sacred centre shows

Kings built and patronised temples as acts of piety and as statements of authority. The ruler appears as the protector of the gods, and temples held land, ran markets and kept records, so they were economic as well as spiritual institutions.

Vittala Temple and the stone chariot

The Vittala Temple, from the 15th to 16th centuries and associated with Krishnadevaraya's reign, is regarded as the high point of Vijayanagara architecture. Its pillared halls, carved with elaborate figures, are the source of the so-called musical pillars, which produce notes when tapped. Re-verify The number of pillars is often given as 56, and tapping is now barred to protect them.

In the courtyard stands the stone chariot, a shrine to Garuda, shaped as a chariot with carved stone wheels. It appears on India's fifty-rupee note.

Architectural features

Ornate pillars with rearing animals, large mandapas, and carved friezes, a mature statement of the Vijayanagara style.

Why it matters

It shows the engineering, sculptural skill and resources of the late empire, and has become Hampi's best-known visual symbol.

Water, tanks and engineering

Hampi's water management is among its most underrated achievements. Stone and brick channels, aqueducts and canals carried water from the Tungabhadra and its tributaries into the city. Dams and diversion channels, some from the 14th to 15th centuries, irrigated the lower areas, and the royal centre's large stepped tank, the Pushkarani, shows the formal, geometric treatment of water in ritual and royal life.

The same river is crossed by traditional round coracle boats still used at Hampi. Control of water supported agriculture, kept the capital supplied in dry months, and gave rulers a tool of legitimacy.

SSB link: infrastructure as power

An empire's reach often rests on logistics such as water, roads and storage. Hampi shows that infrastructure is both a development and a security issue.

Part IV · Culture and heritage

Faith, letters and conservation

Religion, literature and music

The empire's rulers were mainly Shaiva and later Vaishnava in emphasis, and supported many sects, including Jain and Virashaiva institutions and Muslim communities. Dvaita philosophy flourished under Vyasatirtha, who was close to Krishnadevaraya, and the composer-saint Purandara Dasa, often called the father of Carnatic music, is associated with the empire.

Literature flourished in Kannada, Telugu, Sanskrit and Tamil. Krishnadevaraya's Telugu court is traditionally linked to eight poets, the Ashtadiggajas, and to the popular stories of the courtier Tenali Rama, whose historicity is uncertain. Tradition

Cosmopolitan court

The court used the title Hindu Suratrana ("Sultan among Hindu kings"), a phrase that reflects an engagement with the political vocabulary of neighbouring Islamic states. Interpretations vary on what it signified.

Rediscovery, UNESCO and Hampi today

After 1565 Hampi was abandoned as a capital and slowly became a ruin, though Virupaksha continued as a living shrine. In 1800 Colin Mackenzie, the Surveyor-General, surveyed the site, and A. H. Longhurst's Hampi Ruins (1917) gave a standard description. The Archaeological Survey of India and the Karnataka state department took over conservation, and the Vijayanagara Research Project from the 1980s did detailed surveys.

The Group of Monuments at Hampi was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage list in 1986 (criteria i, iii and iv). It was added to the "in danger" list in 1999 over unregulated development, including bridge construction, and removed in 2006 after remedial measures.

Pressures

Tourism and the clearance of Hampi Bazaar's modern encroachments, mining in the wider district, new roads and bridges, and the balance between living communities and a protected zone.

Responses

Buffer zones, management plans, community consultation and annual festivals such as the Hampi Utsav, with continuing debate about their balance.

Scenarios and options

Hampi raises two practical questions: how to run a living heritage site, and what a medieval defeat teaches about strategy. Options are presented with trade-offs, not as recommendations.

Scenario A: Tourism overwhelms the site

Visitor numbers, traffic and new construction damage fabric and change the landscape. Local livelihoods and conservation then pull in opposite directions.

Scenario B: Managed living heritage

Strict zoning, visitor caps and community involvement protect the monuments while keeping the village and temple traditions alive.

Options for site management

  • Strict protection: relocate encroachments and limit development, at a social cost to residents.
  • Community co-management: keep residents and rituals in place under agreed rules, at the cost of slower enforcement.
  • Visitor management: ticketed zones, shuttles and timed entry, balancing revenue and access.

One worked example: "What does Talikota teach a military leader?"

Define
A decisive defeat that led to the loss of a capital.
Evidence
Regent's isolation of the sultanates, their coalition, and a battlefield collapse.
Lessons
Alliances matter; do not alienate all neighbours; command integrity under stress.
Counter-view
Sources are partial and the battle details are contested.
Close
Prepare for a coalition against you, and protect the command structure.
