Part I · Foundations

The problem NavIC solves

Finding position at sea

A ship that does not know where it is cannot navigate, fight or be rescued. Every naval capability sits on top of position, navigation and timing (PNT).

Three things are needed: position (where am I), velocity and heading (where am I going) and time (a shared clock across ships, aircraft and shore stations). Satellite navigation supplies all three at once, which is why it is called a force multiplier: it makes weapons more accurate, fleets easier to coordinate and search and rescue faster.

The core idea

Modern navies treat PNT as critical infrastructure. Losing it does not stop a ship, but it degrades accuracy, coordination and weapon employment. Resilience, meaning several independent ways to know position, is the real objective.

How GNSS works

A Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) is a set of satellites that broadcast their own position and the exact time of transmission. A receiver on a ship measures how long each signal took to arrive, converts that into a distance, and fixes its own position where those distances meet. This is trilateration.

  • Four satellites minimum: three for position, one to correct the receiver's cheap clock.
  • Atomic clocks: a timing error of one microsecond is a distance error of about 300 m, so satellites carry rubidium or caesium clocks.
  • Error sources: ionospheric delay, satellite orbit error, multipath reflections and poor satellite geometry (dilution of precision).
  • Dual-frequency receivers cancel most ionospheric delay, which is why NavIC uses both L5 and S bands.

Satellite signals reach the Earth very weakly, so they are easy to jam. This single fact drives most of the strategic discussion later in this handbook.

Global systems use many satellites in medium Earth orbit, so some are always overhead. A regional system needs far fewer satellites but only serves one part of the world. NavIC is a regional system by design, with its satellites placed to cover the Indian Ocean and subcontinent.

Why India built NavIC

The usual starting point is the Kargil conflict of 1999. India is widely reported to have asked the US for GPS data over the Kargil sector and not received it. Disputed The US side has said that no formal denial policy existed, so treat the episode as a reported motivation, not a settled fact.

Whatever the precise facts, the lesson drawn in Delhi was clear: a system controlled by another state can be degraded, restricted or denied at a time of that state's choosing. A military that relies on foreign navigation has a vulnerability it cannot fix by buying more ships.

  • 2006: the Government approved the Indian Regional Navigation Satellite System (IRNSS), with a budget of about Rs 1,420 crore. Verify
  • Strategic need: an independent, assured signal for the armed forces, with a restricted service for authorised users.
  • Civil need: better coverage for transport, disaster warning, fishing and timing in India's own region.

The NavIC constellation

The name NavIC, short for Navigation with Indian Constellation, was given by the Prime Minister in 2016 for the operational IRNSS. It is also a Hindi word associated with a sailor, which suited the Navy link.

Space segment

  • Seven satellites: 3 geostationary (GEO) and 4 inclined geosynchronous (IGSO).
  • GEO stay fixed over the Indian Ocean; IGSO trace a figure-eight over the region.
  • Because the satellites are high above the Indian region, signals have a good angle even in urban or hilly terrain.

Signals and services

  • Bands: L5 and S; newer satellites also add L1. Verify
  • Standard Positioning Service (SPS): open to all.
  • Restricted Service (RS): encrypted, for authorised users including the armed forces.
  • Stated accuracy: better than about 20 m over India and about 10 m in practice. Verify

Ground segment: two master control centres (reported at Hassan in Karnataka and Bhopal), ranging and monitoring stations across India and abroad, and the ISRO Navigation Centre near Bengaluru that generates navigation data. Verify

The ISRO programme

  • 2013IRNSS-1A launched on PSLV. Seven first-generation satellites followed through April 2016.
  • 2016-17Rubidium atomic clocks on several satellites, including 1A, began to fail. This was the main setback of the programme. Reported
  • 2017IRNSS-1H, a replacement, failed to reach orbit because its heat shield did not separate.
  • 2018IRNSS-1I launched as a replacement.
  • 2020The International Maritime Organization recognised NavIC as a component of the World-Wide Radio Navigation System. Verify
  • 2023NVS-01, the first second-generation satellite, launched on GSLV with an indigenous atomic clock and the L1 band. Verify
  • 2025NVS-02 launched, but an engine valve problem reportedly kept it from reaching its intended orbit. Verify status
Check before quoting

The number of NavIC satellites that are fully operational has changed over time because of ageing clocks and replacements. Do not quote "seven working satellites" without checking the latest ISRO statement.

Part II · NavIC and the Navy

From the bridge to the weapon

Naval navigation, then and now

Naval navigation has moved through four layers, and each remains in use as a backup.

  1. Visual and celestial: landmarks, compass, sextant, chronometer. Still taught to every officer for the day systems fail.
  2. Radio and radar: radio beacons, radar ranging off coastlines.
  3. Inertial navigation (INS): gyroscopes and accelerometers that track movement from a known starting point. Self-contained and impossible to jam, but errors grow with time, so it needs periodic correction.
  4. Satellite navigation: GPS first, then NavIC and other systems, fused with INS.

The modern approach is integration: an INS corrected by satellite fixes, cross-checked with radar and depth data. If the satellite signal is lost, the INS carries on and drifts slowly, rather than failing outright.

Ships and submarines

  • Bridge and combat system: position feeds the electronic chart display (ECDIS), collision-avoidance, the Automatic Identification System (AIS) and the combat management system.
  • Precise timing: time from satellites synchronises data links, radars and encrypted communications between ships and aircraft.
  • Replenishment and formation: keeping station in a task force and conducting underway replenishment needs accurate relative position.
  • Surface ships: NavIC receivers, including multi-system units that use GPS and NavIC together, have been reported on Indian vessels. Verify
  • Submarines: a submerged submarine cannot receive satellite signals. It relies on its inertial system and takes a satellite fix when it raises a mast near the surface, which is a moment of risk.
Exam point

Say clearly that satellite navigation does not replace seamanship. A navigator who cannot work a position by dead reckoning, radar and soundings is a risk, whichever system is on the bridge.

Weapons, aircraft and UAVs

Precision weapons use a satellite fix to correct their inertial guidance on the way to the target. This applies to cruise missiles, guided bombs and some artillery and rocket munitions.

  • Naval aviation: shipborne helicopters and maritime patrol aircraft use satellite fixes for search patterns, sonobuoy laying and coordinating with ships.
  • Unmanned systems: drones and autonomous underwater vehicles rely heavily on satellite position when on the surface.
  • Restricted Service: the encrypted NavIC signal is intended to give authorised military users assured guidance, in principle harder to spoof than an open signal. Interpretation
  • Why it matters: a weapon that depends on a foreign signal for its last-mile accuracy is only as reliable as that foreign policy.

The practical position is mixed. Indian forces use GPS, GLONASS, Galileo and NavIC receivers as the platform and supplier allow, and the real aim is to reduce dependence on any single foreign source over time.

Fishermen and the Coast Guard

NavIC is not only military. A feature of the system is its short-message capability, which can broadcast alerts to users who have no mobile coverage.

  • Fishermen: boats can carry NavIC-based transponders that send position and receive weather, cyclone and potential-fishing-zone messages from the Indian National Centre for Ocean Information Services (INCOIS). Verify scale of rollout
  • Distress: an alert from a boat can reach shore stations and the Coast Guard even far from the coast.
  • Coast Guard and the Navy: both use position data for search and rescue, anti-piracy, fisheries surveillance and the post-26/11 coastal security network.
  • Border risk: fishing boats straying across the maritime boundary can be warned by a position alert, which reduces arrests and diplomatic friction. Interpretation

GAGAN and combined use

GAGAN (GPS Aided GEO Augmented Navigation) is a joint ISRO and Airports Authority of India system. It corrects GPS using ground stations and geostationary satellites, giving the accuracy and integrity needed for civil aviation. It is a different tool from NavIC: GAGAN improves GPS, while NavIC is an independent system.

Receivers that combine NavIC and GPS have reported better availability and accuracy than either alone, especially in difficult terrain or crowded ports. For the Navy, the principle is multi-constellation use: if one system is degraded, others continue.

Part III · Strategic analysis

Space, threats and autonomy

Space and the Navy

Navigation is one of several space services that a modern navy needs.

  • Communications: GSAT-7 (Rukmini), launched in 2013, is a dedicated naval communications satellite linking ships, submarines, aircraft and shore headquarters across the Indian Ocean. A follow-on, GSAT-7R, was launched in 2025. Verify details
  • Surveillance and reconnaissance: optical, radar and signals-intelligence satellites help track ships and coastlines. A large space-based surveillance programme was approved in 2024. Verify
  • Timing and navigation: NavIC, GPS and others.
  • Institutions: the Defence Space Agency (set up in 2019) and the Defence Space Research Organisation coordinate armed-forces space needs, with ISRO as the main builder and launcher.
Why it matters at sea

The sea is large and empty. Satellites turn a vast ocean into a monitored space, which is how a navy sees beyond its radar horizon and talks to ships thousands of kilometres from shore.

Jamming, spoofing and ASAT

Satellite signals are weak, so they are vulnerable.

Jamming

Noise on the same frequency swamps the signal. Cheap, widely available and hard to attribute.

Spoofing

A false signal convinces the receiver of a wrong position. Encrypted military signals are harder to spoof than open civil ones.

Anti-satellite (ASAT)

Kinetic or electronic attack on the satellite itself. India demonstrated a direct-ascent ASAT in 2019 (Mission Shakti). Established

Large-scale GNSS interference has been widely reported in recent years around the Baltic, the Black Sea, the Eastern Mediterranean and the Gulf, affecting both commercial shipping and military operations. Verify specifics

The defence is layered: inertial backup, multi-constellation receivers, encrypted signals, anti-jam antennas, and the habit of practising without satellite navigation.

Sovereign navigation

NavIC is part of the broader Atmanirbhar push in defence and space. The case for it is strong, but so are the limits.

Strengths

  • An independent signal that no other country can switch off.
  • Regional geometry is strong over India and the northern Indian Ocean.
  • Restricted Service gives assured military use.
  • Builds Indian industry, chips and an ecosystem.

Limits

  • Coverage is regional, so a deployed task force beyond about 1,500 km of India falls outside it. Verify
  • Seven satellites leave little spare capacity; clock failures hurt badly.
  • Consumer adoption has been slow because chipsets and handsets lagged.
  • Receivers still often rely on GPS as the main source.

The sensible reading is complement, not replacement: NavIC gives India assured access in its own region, while GPS and others remain useful elsewhere. Interpretation

The Indian Ocean picture

  • China's BeiDou has global reach and is offered with Chinese ports, infrastructure and military cooperation to partner states. Reported Chinese presence at ports and survey activity makes navigation a competitive field. Interpretation
  • Pakistan is a known BeiDou user. Whether it enjoys a military-grade signal is reported but not confirmed in open sources. Disputed
  • Partners: the US, Japan, Australia and the EU run their own systems; India works with them under agreements on exchange and logistics. NavIC compatibility with other systems has been the subject of international coordination.
  • Neighbours: India has offered NavIC services to neighbours in principle. Adoption is limited, partly because their receivers are built for GPS. Verify
  • Net assessment: navigation is a quiet instrument of influence. A system that is cheap, reliable and offered with training makes partners dependent in a good or a bad sense, depending on your viewpoint.

Knowledge toolkit

Analysis, practice and revision

Perspectives and facts

The supporter's case

NavIC is insurance. It was built because a nation of India's size cannot leave its military navigation to others. It is cheap relative to a global system, improves civil services, and sets a standard for future second-generation systems.

The sceptic's case

A regional system with ageing clocks and slow adoption cannot guarantee military assurance. Money might be better spent on inertial systems, anti-jam receivers and more satellites in orbit. Visible dependence on GPS in practice undercuts the claim of independence.

A fair answer separates the strategic logic, which most analysts accept, from the delivery record, which is open to criticism. Interpretation

Lesser-known facts

  • A 1 microsecond clock error is about 300 m of position error. Established
  • NavIC receivers have been mandated or encouraged for some vehicle-tracking and public-safety devices. Verify
  • The Kargil episode is a reported rationale, not a documented US refusal. Disputed
  • Submarines cannot use satellite navigation while submerged.

Scenarios and options

Scenarios

  • GPS degraded in a crisis: the fleet continues on INS and NavIC; open-signal users in the region are exposed.
  • NavIC itself jammed near a contested coast: encrypted Restricted Service and anti-jam antennas matter most; the Navy falls back on inertial navigation.
  • Satellite failures reduce the constellation: availability drops, showing the cost of running with few spares.
  • Task force beyond regional coverage: NavIC unavailable; GPS or other systems and military satellites substitute.

Options, and their trade-offs

OptionGainCost or risk
Expand NavIC to more satellites or wider coverageResilience, reach beyond the regionCost, launch schedule, more clocks to maintain

These are not mutually exclusive. A defensible position combines them, and says which is the priority and why.
