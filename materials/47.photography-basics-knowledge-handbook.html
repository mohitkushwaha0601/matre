Using this handbook

Photography looks complicated because the vocabulary is large, but the physics is small: a camera collects light for a controlled time through a controlled opening, and records it on a sensor. This handbook builds from that one idea.

Read Part I first; every later section leans on it. Part II explains lenses, focus and the image file. Part III covers composition, practical settings, accessories and editing. The toolkit closes with myths, practice questions, a chronology, a glossary and a revision sheet. The topic is general craft knowledge, not tied to any one exam.

Best habit

Learn on one variable at a time. Fix the aperture and change only the shutter speed; then do the reverse. Ten minutes of controlled experiments teach more than an hour of reading.

Part I · Light, camera and exposure

How an image is made

Light: the raw material

The word photography comes from Greek roots meaning "drawing with light". A photograph records light reflected from, or emitted by, a subject; the camera does not record objects, only the light that reaches it. Learning to see light matters more than any piece of gear.

Four properties describe light. Quality is hard or soft: a small, distant source such as the midday sun gives hard light with sharp shadows, while a large source such as an overcast sky gives soft, wrapping light. Direction shapes form: front light is flat, side light reveals texture, backlight creates silhouettes and rim glow. Intensity sets how much exposure is needed. Colour is measured as colour temperature in kelvin (K).

Golden hour and blue hour

The hour after sunrise and before sunset gives low, warm, directional light, which is why landscape and portrait photographers favour it. The twilight just before sunrise or after sunset, the blue hour, gives cool, even light with glowing city lights.

How a camera works

Every camera, from a pinhole box to a phone, has the same chain: a lens focuses light, an aperture limits how much passes, a shutter decides for how long, and a sensor (or film) records it. Everything else is convenience.

A DSLR (digital single-lens reflex) uses a mirror and optical prism so the photographer sees through the lens; the mirror flips up at exposure. A mirrorless camera has no mirror and shows the sensor image on an electronic viewfinder or screen, which allows a smaller body and live preview of exposure. A compact has a fixed lens. A smartphone has a tiny sensor and fixed lenses but strong software processing.

Body controls to know

  • Mode dial: Auto, P, A/Av, S/Tv, M.
  • Exposure compensation: the +/- button.
  • Drive mode: single, burst, self-timer.
  • AF mode and point: how and where it focuses.

Shooting mode meanings

  • A or Av: you set aperture, camera sets shutter.
  • S or Tv: you set shutter, camera sets aperture.
  • M: you set both, with ISO.
  • P: camera suggests a pair; you can shift it.

The exposure triangle and stops

Exposure is the total amount of light that reaches the sensor to make an image of the right brightness. Three settings control it: aperture (how wide the lens opening is), shutter speed (how long the sensor is exposed) and ISO (how strongly the signal is amplified). Together they form the exposure triangle.

Photographers count in stops. One stop is a doubling or halving of light. Opening the aperture one stop, halving the shutter time and doubling the ISO each change brightness by the same one stop, so settings can be traded. The trade is the heart of the craft: each setting also changes something other than brightness.

What each control does besides brightness
ControlBrighter image bySide effect
ApertureWider opening (smaller f-number)Shallower depth of field
Shutter speedSlower speedMore motion blur
ISOHigher numberMore noise
Equivalent exposures

f/8 at 1/125 s and ISO 100 gives the same brightness as f/5.6 at 1/250 s and ISO 100, or f/8 at 1/250 s and ISO 200. The look differs: the second has a shallower background, the third has more noise.

Aperture

The aperture is an adjustable opening inside the lens, made of overlapping blades. It is written as an f-number such as f/2.8, the ratio of focal length to the opening's diameter. A smaller f-number means a larger opening: f/1.8 lets in much more light than f/16.

The full-stop scale is f/1, 1.4, 2, 2.8, 4, 5.6, 8, 11, 16, 22. Each step halves the light, because the numbers grow by roughly the square root of two as the area halves. A lens with a wide maximum aperture, say f/1.8, is called fast.

Aperture also controls depth of field, the zone of acceptable sharpness: wide apertures give a blurred background (bokeh is the quality of that blur), narrow apertures keep more of the scene sharp. Very small apertures such as f/22 soften the whole image through diffraction, so many lenses are sharpest around f/5.6 to f/11.

Quick use

  • Portrait with blurred background: f/1.8 to f/4.
  • Group photo or landscape: f/8 to f/11.
  • Starburst on lights: f/16 or so.

Shutter speed

Shutter speed is the length of time the sensor is exposed, written in seconds or fractions: 1/1000, 1/250, 1/60, 1", 30". Each full step doubles or halves the time. Fast speeds freeze motion; slow speeds blur it, which can be a deliberate effect such as silky water or light trails.

Two kinds of blur exist and are often confused. Subject blur comes from the subject moving. Camera shake comes from the photographer moving. The reciprocal rule gives a safe handheld minimum: shutter speed at least 1/focal length, so about 1/50 s with a 50 mm lens on a full-frame camera. Image stabilisation (IS, VR, IBIS) can allow several stops slower.

Rough shutter speeds for common subjects

Panning uses a medium speed such as 1/30 s while following a moving subject, giving a sharp subject on a streaked background.

ISO and noise

ISO indicates the sensor's amplification. Base ISO, usually 100 or 200, gives the cleanest image. Doubling ISO adds one stop of brightness but also amplifies noise, the grainy speckle most visible in shadows. Noise rises because less real light was collected while the amplifier boosted the weak signal.

The practical rule is to keep ISO as low as the light, aperture and shutter limits allow, but never to accept a blurred image to avoid noise. A sharp, noisy photo is usually better than a clean, blurred one. Modern sensors and noise-reduction software have pushed usable ISO far higher than a decade ago.

Auto ISO

In A or S mode, Auto ISO with a set maximum (for example 6400) and a minimum shutter speed is a reliable way to let the camera protect against blur while you control the creative setting.

Underexposing and brightening later raises noise in the shadows. Overexposing loses highlights permanently. Exposing so the bright areas just avoid clipping, often called exposing to the right, gives the cleanest RAW file.

Metering and the histogram

The camera's meter measures the light and suggests an exposure, aiming to render the scene as average grey, about 18 percent reflectance. This works for typical scenes and fails for extremes: snow comes out dull grey and a black cat comes out washed out, so you correct with exposure compensation (+1 for snow, -1 for dark subjects).

Metering modes
ModeMeasuresBest for
Evaluative / MatrixWhole frame, analysed by sceneGeneral use
Centre-weightedEmphasis on the middlePortraits, predictable framing
SpotTiny area (a few percent)Backlit subjects, stage lights

The histogram is a graph of tones from black on the left to white on the right. A pile against the right edge suggests clipped highlights; against the left, crushed shadows. A good histogram depends on the scene: a snowy field will lean right, a night street will lean left. Highlight alerts ("blinkies") flag blown areas on the preview.

Dynamic range is the span from darkest to brightest tone a sensor can record in one frame, measured in stops. When a scene exceeds it, expose for the highlights in digital, or use bracketing and HDR (merging several exposures).

Part II · Lenses, focus and the image

What the lens sees and the sensor keeps

Lenses and focal length

Focal length, in millimetres, is the distance from the lens's optical centre to the sensor when focused at infinity. In practice it sets the field of view: short focal lengths see wide, long ones see narrow and magnify. It also changes perspective through where you stand: wide lenses used close exaggerate depth, while telephotos used from far compress it.

Focal length classes (full-frame equivalent)
ClassRangeTypical use
Ultra-widebelow 24 mmInteriors, landscapes, astro
Wide24-35 mmStreet, travel, environment
Normalabout 50 mmGeneral, close to eye view
Short tele70-135 mmPortraits
Telephoto200 mm and aboveSport, wildlife, distant detail

A prime lens has one focal length and is usually sharper, lighter and faster. A zoom covers a range for flexibility. A macro lens focuses very close, often to 1:1 magnification. Crop factor expresses how much smaller a sensor is than full-frame: about 1.5 for APS-C and 2 for Micro Four Thirds, so a 50 mm lens frames like 75 mm or 100 mm. The lens itself does not change; only the cropped view does.

Focus and depth of field

Focus places one plane of the scene at maximum sharpness. Autofocus uses either phase detection, which measures direction and distance of focus error and is fast, or contrast detection, which hunts for the sharpest edge and is slower; most modern cameras combine both and add subject and eye tracking.

AF modes

  • Single (AF-S, One-Shot): focuses once, then locks. For still subjects.
  • Continuous (AF-C, AI Servo): keeps refocusing while you hold. For moving subjects.
  • Manual focus: for macro, night and tricky scenes.

Focus technique

  • Use a single small AF point on the eye or key detail.
  • Focus-and-recompose can shift focus at wide apertures.
  • Back-button focus separates focusing from the shutter.

Depth of field (DoF) grows with a smaller aperture, a shorter focal length, a greater subject distance and a smaller sensor. Roughly one third of it lies in front of the focus point and two thirds behind. The hyperfocal distance is the focus distance that makes everything from about half that distance to infinity acceptably sharp, a landscape technique for maximum depth.

Sensors and file formats

The sensor is a grid of photosites that convert light into electrical charge. A Bayer filter places red, green and blue filters over them in a 2 green : 1 red : 1 blue pattern, and the camera reconstructs full colour by demosaicing. Megapixels count the photosites: 24 MP is roughly 6000 by 4000 pixels.

Common sensor formats
FormatApprox. sizeNotes
Full-frame36 × 24 mmBest low light and shallow depth of field
APS-Cabout 23.5 × 15.6 mmCheaper, smaller, 1.5 crop
Micro Four Thirds17.3 × 13 mmCompact system, 2.0 crop
Smartphonea few mm acrossRelies on computational processing

More megapixels do not mean better quality; a larger photosite gathers more light and usually gives cleaner images. The JPEG format is processed, compressed and 8-bit, ready to share. The RAW format stores the sensor data, typically 12 to 14 bits, leaving white balance, exposure and tone to be set later with far more headroom, at the cost of large files and mandatory editing.

White balance and colour

Our eyes adjust to lighting colour automatically; cameras do not. White balance (WB) tells the camera what colour of light is falling on the scene so neutral objects appear neutral. Auto WB works often; presets (Daylight, Cloudy, Tungsten, Fluorescent) or a custom Kelvin value give control. In RAW, WB is only metadata and can be changed freely afterwards.

Colour has three descriptive axes: hue (which colour), saturation (intensity) and luminance or brightness. Warm tones (red, orange, yellow) feel inviting; cool tones (blue, cyan) feel calm or distant. Complementary colours, such as orange against blue, create strong contrast.

Colour spaces

sRGB is the standard for screens and the web. Adobe RGB covers a wider range of greens and cyans and suits some print work. For ordinary sharing, sRGB is safe; mismatched profiles are the usual cause of colours looking different between devices.

Part III · Craft

From settings to pictures

Composition

Composition is how elements are arranged in the frame. Technical correctness gives a clean image; composition gives it a reason to exist. The guidelines below are tools, not laws, and good photographers break them deliberately.

Rule of thirds

Divide the frame into a 3 by 3 grid and place subjects or horizons on the lines or intersections.

Leading lines

Roads, rails and fences guide the eye toward the subject.

Framing

Arches, windows and branches frame the subject and add depth.

Foreground, middle, background

Layers give a flat scene a sense of depth.

Negative space

Empty area around a subject gives calm and emphasis.

Simplify

Remove distracting objects; watch for poles growing out of heads.

Other useful ideas: symmetry and patterns, contrast in colour or tone, a change of viewpoint (get low, climb high), fill the frame to remove clutter, and keep horizons level. Take a second to scan the corners of the frame before pressing the shutter.

Flash, filters and support

A tripod allows slow shutter speeds for landscapes, night and long exposures; use a remote or self-timer to avoid shake. A monopod aids long lenses. A lens hood reduces flare and protects the glass.

Flash adds light and freezes motion. A built-in flash aimed straight at a subject looks harsh; bouncing an external flash off a ceiling or wall makes it softer. The flash sync speed, commonly 1/200 to 1/250 s, is the fastest shutter speed at which the whole sensor is exposed to a flash pulse on a focal-plane shutter. Rear-curtain sync fires at the end of the exposure so motion trails appear behind a subject. Fill flash lightens shadows in bright sun.

Useful filters

  • Circular polariser: cuts reflections and deepens skies.
  • ND (neutral density): cuts light for long exposures in daylight.
  • Graduated ND: darkens a bright sky only.

Care and basics

  • Clean lenses with a blower and microfibre cloth.
  • Keep spare batteries; cold drains them quickly.
  • Back up files in two places.

Editing and workflow

Editing, or post-processing, is part of modern photography, not a betrayal of it; film darkroom workers dodged, burnt and toned prints for a century. A typical workflow: import and back up, cull the weak frames, rate the best, edit globally, edit locally, export.

  1. Straighten and crop to improve composition.
  2. Exposure and white balance to correct the base.
  3. Contrast, highlights, shadows to shape tone; the curves tool does this precisely.
  4. Colour: vibrance and saturation, then HSL for individual hues.
  5. Local edits with masks or brushes to lift a face or darken a sky.
  6. Sharpen last, and apply noise reduction as needed.

Non-destructive editing, used by programs such as Lightroom and Capture One, stores edits as instructions and leaves the original untouched. Export for the web as sRGB JPEG at around 2000 to 3000 pixels on the long edge; for print, export at the print size, at about 300 pixels per inch (ppi). Keep the line between enhancing a photograph and fabricating one clear, especially in documentary and news work.

Knowledge toolkit

Analysis, practice and revision

Myths and debates

Photography is full of confident claims. Tags below separate what is solid from what is a matter of taste or conditions.

  • EstablishedMore megapixels are not automatically better. Resolution helps cropping and large prints; photosite size and processing matter for low light.
  • EstablishedCrop factor does not change the lens. A cropped sensor only sees the central part of the image circle.
  • Disputed"Full-frame is always better." It gives better low-light and shallower depth of field, but costs more weight and money; many professionals work happily on smaller formats.
  • InterpretationRules of composition. Some see them as a useful default; others as a crutch that produces predictable pictures.
  • InterpretationHow much editing is acceptable. Competitions and news agencies set explicit limits; art photography sets few.
  • DisputedPhone versus camera. Computational photography now beats entry-level cameras in many casual scenes, but dedicated cameras still win on telephoto reach, fast action and optical control.
  • Re-verifyProduct specifications. Camera models, prices and AI features change fast; check current reviews before buying.
Lesser-known fact

Many "unsharp" photos are not out of focus but blurred by camera shake or by a shutter speed too slow for the moving subject; check shutter speed before blaming the lens.

Scenarios and kit choices

Photographers regularly face practical decisions. The options below show the trade-offs without pushing one answer.

Scenario: first serious camera

  • Stay with the phone: free, always with you, strong software.
  • Entry mirrorless or DSLR with a kit lens: manual control and interchangeable lenses.
  • Second-hand body plus a cheap prime: best image quality per rupee, less warranty.

Scenario: dim indoor event

  • Open the aperture; raise ISO; use a faster prime.
  • Use bounce flash rather than direct flash.
  • Accept some noise rather than motion blur.

Scenario: harsh midday sun

  • Find open shade or use a diffuser.
  • Use fill flash on faces.
  • Shift the style: strong shadows, graphic shapes.

Scenario: travel with limited weight

  • One wide-to-tele zoom for flexibility.
  • A small prime for low light.
  • A compact tripod only if night shooting is planned.

Glossary

Aperture
The adjustable opening in a lens, written as an f-number.
Bokeh
The look of out-of-focus areas in an image.
Bracketing
Taking several frames at different exposures.
Burst mode
Taking many frames per second while the shutter button is held.
Clipping
Loss of detail in pure white or pure black areas.
Crop factor
Ratio of full-frame sensor size to a smaller sensor.
Depth of field
The zone in front of and behind the focus point that looks acceptably sharp.
Diffraction
Softening caused by light bending around very small aperture blades.
Dynamic range
Range from darkest to brightest detail a sensor can record, in stops.
EV (exposure value)
A number that describes a combination of exposure settings; exposure compensation is in EV steps.
Exposure
The amount of light that reaches the sensor.
Focal length
The lens distance parameter, in millimetres, that sets angle of view.
Hyperfocal distance
The focus distance that gives maximum depth of field to infinity.
ISO
The sensor's signal amplification setting.
Metering
The camera's measurement of scene brightness.
Mirrorless
Interchangeable-lens camera with no mirror box.
ND filter
Neutral density filter that reduces light without altering colour.
Noise
Random speckle in an image, worse at high ISO.
Prime lens
Lens with a fixed focal length.
RAW
Unprocessed sensor data file for flexible editing.
Shutter speed
How long the sensor is exposed to light.
Stop
A doubling or halving of light.
White balance
Adjustment that makes neutral objects look neutral under a given light.

Rapid revision sheet

One-page summary

  • Exposure = aperture + shutter speed + ISO; one stop = double or half the light.
  • Wider aperture (smaller f-number): more light, shallower depth of field.
  • Faster shutter freezes motion; handheld minimum about 1/focal length.
  • Lower ISO means cleaner image; raise it rather than accept blur.
  • Aperture scale: f/1.4, 2, 2.8, 4, 5.6, 8, 11, 16, 22.
  • Meter aims at 18 percent grey: use exposure compensation for snow (+) and dark scenes (-).
  • Histogram shows tones: left shadows, right highlights; avoid clipping.
  • Focal length sets field of view; crop factor 1.5 for APS-C, 2 for Micro Four Thirds.
  • AF-S for still subjects; AF-C for moving; manual in macro and night.
  • Depth of field grows with smaller aperture, wider lens, greater distance.
  • RAW holds more data than JPEG; sRGB for the web.
  • Colour temperature: candle 1,800 K, tungsten ~3,000 K, daylight ~5,500 K, shade ~7,500 K.
  • Composition: thirds, leading lines, framing, layers, negative space, simplicity.
  • Editing order: crop, exposure, tone, colour, local edits, sharpen last.
  • Sunny 16: f/16 at 1/ISO in bright sun.
