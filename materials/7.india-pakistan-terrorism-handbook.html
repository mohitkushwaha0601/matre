Using this handbook

The short version: enough to speak accurately and calmly on India's most emotional security subject, built to be read in two to three hours.

  • First pass: the snapshot and the revision sheet.
  • Second pass: Parts I-IV in order.
  • Third pass: answer the practice questions aloud in two minutes each.
How to answer any India-Pakistan question

Define the issue. Locate it in time and place. Establish the agreed facts. Show where it is contested. Analyse causes. Test against evidence. Take a position. Concede the best counter-point. Close with a forward-looking line.

Claims are tagged Established Interpretation Disputed Re-verify. Stay factual and calm: in an interview, anger reads as poor judgement.

The conflict on one page

The lines that divide
Line Length What it is
International Border (IB) Gujarat-Jammu Agreed and largely fenced. Pakistan calls the Jammu stretch (~198 km) the “Working Boundary”. India's official figure for the whole India-Pakistan land border, including the LoC, is 3,323 km.
Line of Control (LoC) 740 km The 1949 ceasefire line, renamed by the 1972 Simla Agreement. A military line, not a border.
AGPL ~110 km Actual Ground Position Line on the Siachen glacier and Saltoro Ridge.
The conflict in three lines

Pakistan sees Kashmir as the unfinished business of Partition. India sees the 1947 accession as legal and final. Since 1989, Pakistan has used militant proxies to press its claim below the threshold of war; India has moved from restraint to punitive strikes.

Part I

Origins

Partition and accession

In 1947 some 565 princely states had to join India or Pakistan. Three did not decide cleanly.

  • Jammu & Kashmir: a Muslim-majority state under Hindu ruler Maharaja Hari Singh, who wanted time. He signed a Standstill Agreement with Pakistan; India did not sign one.
  • 22 October 1947: Pashtun tribal raiders, backed by Pakistan, invade. Baramulla is sacked.
  • 26 October 1947: the Maharaja signs the Instrument of Accession; Indian troops land at Srinagar on 27 October.
  • Junagadh (Hindu majority, Muslim ruler) acceded to Pakistan; after Indian action a plebiscite in February 1948 chose India. Hyderabad was integrated by Operation Polo in September 1948.
The logic each side uses

Interpretation India: the ruler's accession is legally valid, as it was for every other state. Pakistan: Muslim-majority contiguous areas should have come to it, and the accession was under duress. Note that Pakistan itself accepted Junagadh's accession by its ruler — against the majority's wishes.

The first war and the UN

  • Nov 1947Srinagar saved; Maj Somnath Sharma killed at Badgam — India's first Param Vir Chakra.
  • 1 Jan 1948India takes the matter to the UN Security Council.
  • Aug 1948UNCIP resolution (13 August): Pakistan to withdraw its forces first; then India reduces its forces; then a plebiscite.
  • Nov 1948Indian tanks cross Zoji La to relieve Ladakh.
  • 1 Jan 1949Ceasefire. The ceasefire line (Karachi Agreement, July 1949) leaves about a third of the state with Pakistan.

Why the plebiscite never happened. Established The first step — Pakistani withdrawal — never took place. Later, the Simla Agreement (1972) committed both countries to settle issues bilaterally, which India treats as superseding the UN route.

Part II

The wars

1965

  • AprSkirmishes in the Rann of Kutch; Pakistan concludes India is weak after 1962.
  • AugOperation Gibraltar: infiltrators sent into Kashmir to spark a revolt. Locals do not rise; India captures the Haji Pir Pass.
  • 1 SepOperation Grand Slam: Pakistani armour drives towards Akhnoor.
  • 6 SepIndia crosses the IB towards Lahore and Sialkot.
  • 8-10 SepAsal Uttar: Pakistani tank thrust defeated; CQMH Abdul Hamid awarded the PVC. Major tank battles around Sialkot follow.
  • 23 SepUN ceasefire. Tashkent Declaration (10 January 1966) returns captured territory, including Haji Pir.

Interpretation Militarily a stalemate. Strategically, Pakistan failed in its aim — Kashmir did not rise and India was not defeated.

1971 and Simla

India's most decisive military victory, and the birth of Bangladesh.

  • 25 MarPakistan's army launches Operation Searchlight in East Pakistan. About 10 million refugees flee to India.
  • 9 AugIndo-Soviet Treaty of Peace, Friendship and Cooperation.
  • 3 DecPakistan launches air strikes on Indian bases; war begins on both fronts.
  • 4-5 DecNavy's Operation Trident strikes Karachi; PNS Ghazi sinks off Visakhapatnam. Longewala holds in the west.
  • 16 DecSurrender at Dhaka: Lt Gen Niazi to Lt Gen Jagjit Singh Aurora. About 93,000 prisoners.
  • 2 Jul 1972Simla Agreement: bilateral settlement of disputes; the ceasefire line becomes the LoC.
Was Simla a missed chance?

Interpretation Critics say India returned prisoners and territory without securing a Kashmir settlement. Supporters say a humiliating peace would have bred revenge, and bilateralism was itself a gain.

Siachen

The 1949 and 1972 lines stopped at grid point NJ9842, then said only “thence north to the glaciers”. Pakistan began authorising foreign expeditions into the area.

  • Operation Meghdoot, 13 April 1984: India occupies the Saltoro Ridge ahead of a planned Pakistani move.
  • 1987: Naib Subedar Bana Singh captures the Quaid Post (renamed Bana Post) at about 21,000 ft — PVC.
  • A ceasefire has held since 2003. Most deaths are from weather and altitude, not combat.

Interpretation Siachen separates Pakistan-occupied territory from Aksai Chin and overlooks the Shaksgam Valley. India will not demilitarise without Pakistan agreeing and authenticating the current positions.

Kargil, 1999

Three months after the Lahore bus diplomacy, Pakistani troops were found on the heights above Kargil.

  • Feb 1999Lahore Declaration (21 February).
  • May 1999Intrusions detected across the LoC by troops of Pakistan's Northern Light Infantry, posing as militants.
  • Jun-JulOperation Vijay: Tololing, Tiger Hill (4 July), Point 4875. The IAF's Operation Safed Sagar. India does not cross the LoC.
  • 4 JulUnder US pressure, Nawaz Sharif agrees to withdraw.
  • 26 JulOperation declared complete — Kargil Vijay Diwas. 527 Indian personnel killed.

Heroes to know: Capt Vikram Batra, Lt Manoj Kumar Pandey, Grenadier Yogendra Singh Yadav and Rifleman Sanjay Kumar (all PVC). The Kargil Review Committee (K. Subrahmanyam) led to major security reforms.

Why Kargil matters

It showed that nuclear weapons had not ended limited war, and that restraint (not crossing the LoC) could win international support.

Part III

Kashmir inside

The insurgency since 1989

Pakistan did not create Kashmiri discontent, but from 1989 it armed, trained and directed it.

  • 1987J&K Assembly election, widely alleged to have been rigged; several losing candidates later lead militant groups.
  • 1989-90JKLF violence; the kidnapping of Rubaiya Sayeed (December 1989). AFSPA applied in 1990.
  • 1990Exodus of Kashmiri Pandits after targeted killings and threats. Disputed Numbers vary widely; most estimates are well over 100,000.
  • Mid-1990sPakistan-based groups and foreign fighters replace the local JKLF; the Rashtriya Rifles become the Army's counter-insurgency force.
  • 2008-16Mass street protests (2008, 2010, 2016); Burhan Wani killed in July 2016.
Human rights — the balanced line

Interpretation Security forces operate under great strain against an enemy that hides among civilians. Accountability for abuses strengthens, not weakens, the state's legitimacy. Both sentences are needed.

Article 370 and after

  • What it was: a “temporary” constitutional provision giving J&K special status, with its own constitution; Article 35A defined permanent residents.
  • 5 August 2019: Article 370 made inoperative; the state reorganised into two Union Territories — Jammu & Kashmir (with a legislature) and Ladakh (without) — from 31 October 2019.
  • December 2023: the Supreme Court upheld the move, and directed elections and restoration of statehood “at the earliest”.
  • 2024: Assembly elections held with high turnout; an elected government took office. Re-verify Statehood has been promised but check its current status.

Supporters say

  • Full integration; national laws apply
  • Investment, tourism and lower street violence
  • Record voter turnout

Critics say

  • Done without the consent of the people's elected representatives
  • Long communication shutdowns and detentions
  • Statehood not yet restored

Part IV

Terrorism and response

The terror infrastructure

The main groups
Group Base and leader Known for
Lashkar-e-Taiba (LeT) Muridke, Punjab; Hafiz Saeed 26/11 Mumbai; fronts include the TRF
Jaish-e-Mohammed (JeM) Bahawalpur; Masood Azhar Parliament 2001, Pathankot, Pulwama
Hizbul Mujahideen Syed Salahuddin Largest local Kashmiri group in the 1990s
The Resistance Front (TRF) LeT proxy, formed 2019 Claimed and then denied Pahalgam 2025

The state link: Pakistan's Inter-Services Intelligence (ISI) has long been accused of training, funding and directing these groups. Masood Azhar was released in exchange for passengers of hijacked IC-814 in December 1999 and founded JeM weeks later.

Pressure that worked: the FATF kept Pakistan on its grey list from 2018 to 2022; Masood Azhar was listed by the UN in May 2019; Hafiz Saeed has been convicted in Pakistan on terror-financing charges.

Major attacks and India's responses

From restraint to punishment
Attack Toll India's response
Parliament, 13 Dec 2001 9 + attackers Operation Parakram: ten-month mobilisation; about 798 soldiers died, mostly in accidents and mines. No war.
Mumbai 26/11, 2008 166 Diplomatic pressure; Ajmal Kasab tried and executed. No military action.
Pathankot, Jan 2016 7 A joint investigation with Pakistan; no result.
Uri, 18 Sep 2016 19 Surgical strikes across the LoC, 29 September 2016.
Pulwama, 14 Feb 2019 40 CRPF Balakot air strike, 26 February 2019 — first air strike across the IB since 1971. Pakistan's air response on 27 February.
Pahalgam, 22 Apr 2025 26 Indus Waters Treaty held in abeyance; Operation Sindoor (7 May 2025) — strikes on terror sites including Bahawalpur and Muridke; four days of hostilities; understanding to stop, 10 May. Re-verify
The new normal

Interpretation Each major attack since 2016 has brought a visible, larger military response. The aim is to raise Pakistan's cost of using terror. The risk is that each round sets a higher starting point for the next.

Handle with care. Claims about damage at Balakot and about losses on either side in May 2025 are contested. Do not quote specific numbers; say the claims are disputed.

Nuclear shadow and the LoC

  • 1998: India tests (11 and 13 May); Pakistan tests (28 and 30 May).
  • India: credible minimum deterrence and No First Use, with massive retaliation.
  • Pakistan: no NFU; “full-spectrum deterrence”, including short-range battlefield weapons such as Nasr.
  • LoC ceasefire: agreed in November 2003; frequent violations after 2016; renewed on 25 February 2021 by the two DGMOs.
  • New threats: drones dropping weapons and drugs in Punjab and Jammu; tunnels under the IB.
The stability-instability paradox

Nuclear weapons make full-scale war unlikely — which can encourage lower-level violence such as terrorism, because the stronger side fears escalation. India's strikes since 2016 are an attempt to show there is room for conventional punishment below the nuclear threshold.

Water, China and the world

The Indus Waters Treaty (1960)

Signed on 19 September 1960 with World Bank mediation. India got the eastern rivers (Ravi, Beas, Sutlej, ~33 MAF); Pakistan got the western rivers (Indus, Jhelum, Chenab, ~135 MAF), with limited Indian rights for run-of-river hydropower. It survived three wars. In April 2025 India placed it in abeyance. Re-verify

The wider picture

  • China: Pakistan's main arms supplier (roughly 80% of major imports Re-verify); CPEC; blocks some UN listings.
  • United States: swings between Pakistan as a counter-terror partner and pressure over terror groups; India is a strategic partner.
  • Gulf and others: Saudi Arabia and the UAE fund Pakistan but have grown closer to India.
  • Economy: India's economy is about 10-11 times Pakistan's — the gap keeps widening.

Part V

Knowledge toolkit

Views, controversies, lesser-known facts

Schools of thought in India
School Argument
Punitive deterrence Every attack must bring a larger military cost; no talks until terror ends.
Engagement Keep back-channels open; trade and people-to-people ties build peace constituencies.
Containment Isolate Pakistan diplomatically and economically; focus on India's own growth.

Controversies

  • Nehru's UN referral (1948): a mistake that internationalised Kashmir, or a reasonable move at the time? Interpretation
  • Talks and terror: “Terror and talks cannot go together” versus the view that silence gives hardliners a veto.
  • Escalation risk of the post-2016 doctrine.

Lesser-known facts

  • The Maharaja signed a Standstill Agreement with Pakistan, not India.
  • The 1948 UN resolution required Pakistan to withdraw first.
  • Pathankot came eight days after the Indian PM's surprise stop in Lahore (December 2015) — as Kargil followed the Lahore bus.
  • The Indus Waters Treaty is often cited as one of the world's most durable water treaties.

Scenarios and options

Armed peace

Most likely: no dialogue, LoC calm but tense, periodic attacks.

Another crisis

A mass-casualty attack triggers a larger Indian strike and faster escalation.

Cautious thaw

Back-channel talks, trade and visas — possible only if terror clearly declines.

Pakistan in turmoil

Economic or political collapse could reduce or worsen the threat.

What India can do

  • Deter: credible, quick conventional options; better ISR, drones and precision weapons.
  • Defend: intelligence, counter-infiltration, anti-drone systems, a strong police in J&K.
  • Diplomacy: keep FATF, UN listings and partners focused on terror financing.
  • Win Kashmir's people: jobs, clean governance, statehood, accountability.
A balanced closing line

Make terror costly, make peace possible, and win the people of Kashmir — India needs all three.

Glossary

Instrument of Accession
The legal document by which a ruler joined a princely state to India or Pakistan.
LoC
Line of Control — the military line dividing J&K since 1972.
AGPL
Actual Ground Position Line on the Siachen glacier.
NJ9842
The last demarcated point of the LoC, south of Siachen.
Proxy war
Using non-state fighters to wage war while denying responsibility.
Surgical strike
A limited, precise cross-border raid on specific targets.
AFSPA
Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act — powers and legal protection for forces in “disturbed areas”.
FATF grey list
Countries under increased monitoring for terror financing and money laundering.
No First Use (NFU)
A pledge not to use nuclear weapons first.
Stability-instability paradox
Nuclear stability at the top can permit instability at lower levels.
OGW
Over-ground worker — a militant supporter providing logistics, not fighting.

One-page revision

Numbers

  • LoC 740 km · IB in Jammu ~198 km · AGPL ~110 km
  • 1971: ~93,000 prisoners
  • Kargil: 527 Indian dead
  • Parakram: ~798 deaths without war
  • Attacks: 26/11 — 166 · Uri — 19 · Pulwama — 40 · Pahalgam — 26
  • IWT: India ~33 MAF · Pakistan ~135 MAF
  • Economy gap: ~10-11×

The arc

Disputed accession (1947) → UN and ceasefire → failed Pakistani gambles (1965, Kargil) → decisive Indian victory (1971) → insurgency and proxy war (1989-) → nuclear shadow (1998) → restraint (2001, 2008) → punishment (2016, 2019, 2025).

Ready answers

  • Why Kashmir is disputed: legal accession versus a Muslim-majority claim; the UN process stalled on Pakistani withdrawal.
  • Talks with Pakistan? Keep channels, but no normal relations while terror is state policy.
  • Did strikes work? They raised costs and changed the pattern, but did not end terror — and they raise escalation risks.
  • Kashmir's future: security plus governance plus statehood.
