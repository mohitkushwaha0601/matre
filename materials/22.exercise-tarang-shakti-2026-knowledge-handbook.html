What is Tarang Shakti?

Tarang Shakti is India’s large, multinational air combat exercise designed to build interoperability among air forces that operate different aircraft, doctrine and command structures. In practice, it is more than a show of force: it is a training platform for precision strikes, air defence integration, tactical coordination and mutual familiarity under realistic combat conditions.

For India, the event matters because it sits at the intersection of force modernisation, regional deterrence and the wider Indo-Pacific security conversation. Air warfare is rarely an isolated contest; aircraft, sensors, data links, basing, maintenance and political signalling all shape outcome. A multilateral exercise therefore tests not only pilot skill but also how national air forces work together when operating across language, doctrine and procurement systems.

The exercise also carries diplomatic weight. Even where no formal alliance is created, repeated joint training signals that air power cooperation can be expanded in a region where China’s military reach, Pakistan’s security calculus and the Western Pacific balance all matter. This is why Tarang Shakti is often read as both a military event and a strategic message.

Why it matters now

Air power is central to deterrence, surveillance and crisis escalation in the Indo-Pacific. The rising complexity of modern air warfare stems from drone swarms, long-range sensors, satellite surveillance, electronic warfare and increasingly contested airspace. In this environment, nations value exercises that force commanders to integrate different aircraft and command philosophies without waiting for a real crisis.

India’s security situation adds urgency. Border tension with China, the Pakistan security challenge, the wider maritime theatre around the Indian Ocean and the growing salience of the South China Sea all create incentives for realistic joint training. Exercises allow smaller but strategically relevant forces to rehearse how they would respond to a multi-front air problem, from early warning to combat air patrols and layered air defence.

Tarang Shakti is therefore not just about “flying together”; it is about learning how to sustain operations, share understanding and reduce friction in a high-tempo environment. A state that can coordinate fighter aircraft, transport platforms, air defence batteries and ISR assets across different service cultures gains a real advantage in a contested environment.

Part I · Foundation

Air warfare doctrine and exercise context

Origin and strategic evolution

India has long preferred a mix of bilateral and multilateral deterrence-focused exercises, but Tarang Shakti reflects a deliberate shift toward larger, more visible interoperability programmes. The broader strategic trend is that no air force can assume it will operate alone in a future contingency. Even if the exercise is not framed as an alliance drill, the underlying logic is cooperative deterrence.

Its evolution also aligns with India’s push for indigenous equipment and deeper operational integration. The Indian Air Force has invested in modern fighters, airborne early warning aircraft, transport fleets, precision strike capacity and a stronger network-centric ISR architecture. Interoperability becomes more valuable when a theatre requires rapid data exchange, command discipline and multi-node force coordination.

The political language surrounding Tarang Shakti remains disciplined. India presents it as a training and readiness event rather than as a formal anti-China bloc-building move. This distinction matters: the exercise is designed to strengthen operational competence without foreclosing a broader diplomatic balancing strategy. That nuance is one reason the exercise is valuable to analyse.

Exercise architecture

A large air exercise needs more than fighter sorties. It depends on the sequencing of aircraft generation, maintenance, radar coverage, deconfliction, operating bases, communications architecture and politically sensitive airspace management. In Tarang Shakti, the planning challenge is to bring together diverse force structures while maintaining safety, realism and operational tempo.

The exercise typically tests several layers at once: high-risk air combat manoeuvres, air-to-air refuelling coordination, strike packages, suppression of enemy air defences, electronic warfare, relief and transport operations, and airborne command posts for joint control. Each element asks a different question: can platforms communicate reliably, can crews trust each other’s procedures, and can commanders translate a tactical picture into effective action under stress?

India often gains a major advantage from being the host because it can choreograph basing, authority chains and exercise narrative. Yet host status also creates operational complexity: large-scale drills require airspace coordination with civil aviation, weather planning, defence infrastructure and strict safety procedures. In crisis-era conditions, even a well-planned exercise can reveal hidden friction in logistics or command systems.

Part II · Forces & systems

Participants, platforms and readiness

Participants and force mix

Tarang Shakti brings together a wide range of air force capabilities, often blending frontline fighter aircraft, transport platforms, early warning and surveillance assets, reconnaissance aircraft, and support systems. The resulting force mix is especially useful for analysing how air forces with different generation profiles adapt to common mission sets.

For India, the learning value is especially high because it can compare native platforms such as the Su-30MKI, Rafale, Mirage 2000, Tejas, transport aircraft and air defence networks against partners’ fleets and operating concepts. An exercise becomes a practical test of procurement choices, maintenance cycles and pilot training depth. It also shows whether the Indian Air Force can integrate modern fighters with legacy types in a common operational picture.

Air exercises also exchange labelling, radio procedures and tactical language. Even when aircraft are technically compatible, nearly every force still has distinct command habits — a subtle but significant challenge in high-intensity operations.

Airbases and airspace management

Large-air exercises rely on a network of airbases, support aircraft, maintenance depots and carefully allocated training ranges. India’s geography provides a strong strategic advantage for this: the country’s large airspace, varied terrain and access to maritime approaches allow synthetic combat scenarios to be tracked across different operational contexts.

Airspace management becomes particularly important when multiple aircraft families fly together. Deconfliction, communications discipline, emergency procedures and overlapping radar coverage all create stress points. An exercise may look seamless to the public, yet the real work lies in preventing mishaps while still creating realistic training complexity.

What matters strategically is the signal: the host state can demonstrate it can safely manage high-tempo joint operations in a contested setting without excessive friction. That is useful for deterrence, even when the exercise does not include live missile launches or large-scale warfighting at full intensity.

Air-combat systems and tactics

The core combat question in any modern exercise is not whether aircraft can fly fast, but whether they can detect, share and act on information under stress. Tarang Shakti therefore tests radar integration, data links, airborne early warning, electronic warfare resilience and command-and-control reliability. These are the systems that transform a collection of aircraft into a coherent fighting force.

For India, tactical learning is especially relevant in a period of force modernisation. The challenge is not only platform procurement but also integration across generations of technology. Some aircraft may have superior range or sensors, but without robust networking and doctrine they may underperform in combined operations. A multinational exercise is an efficient way to expose these gaps before they become strategic liabilities.

Exercises also stress decision-making under time pressure. A pilot may have to choose between stealthier ingress, lower-altitude penetration, or preserving fuel for recovery. A manoeuvring force with fragmented data links may become militarily vulnerable even if individual aircraft are technically capable. This is why air exercises are as much about command discipline as they are about aircraft performance.

Command, logistics and sustainment

Experienced air commanders often say the decisive variable in a large exercise is not which fighter is faster, but which force can sustain its tempo. Tarang Shakti therefore includes a hard operational reality: pilots, engineers, fuel, munitions, spares, maintenance crews and surveillance assets all have to keep moving in step.

In high-intensity planning, the biggest hidden problems are not dramatic but systemic. Bad maintenance turnaround, late mission data, confused sequencing of aircraft recovery, or mismatched communications procedures can reduce combat efficiency far more quickly than a doctrinal gap. Multinational drills reveal these bottlenecks in a way that a pure procurement debate rarely does.

That is why senior leaders view exercise participation as a strategic confidence-building activity. It projects capability and reveals where readiness needs investment. In a region with several active air policing and maritime security pressures, the value of a well-run joint exercise is difficult to dismiss.

Worked example: evaluating readiness

One practical way to interrogate a large air exercise is to evaluate the operating chain from sensing to strike and recovery. That is the logic behind a simple “define, locate, establish, contest, analyse, test, position, concede, close” framework used in many strategic assessments.

Define
Is the mission objective clearly assigned and measurable?
Locate
Do commanders know the relevant airspace, threat picture and support nodes?
Establish
Are the aircraft, maintenance teams and communications links in place?
Contest
Can the force survive deception, jamming or contested radar space?
Analyse
Are decision-makers using real-time data to adapt rapidly?
Test
Do crews rehearse realistic strike and recovery procedures under pressure?
Position
Can leaders allocate force among deterrence, defence and sustainment tasks?
Concede
What vulnerabilities remain even after a successful drill?
Close
What lessons are institutionalised into future force design and training?

On that basis, a successful exercise is not judged by sortie numbers alone. It is judged by whether the force learned how to handle complexity, share situational awareness, and commission improvements that translate into more effective planning and operations.

Part III · Strategic impact

Regional balance and big-picture implications

Maritime and continental linkages

Tarang Shakti is not only a continental air exercise; it has maritime implications. Air assets are critical for reconnaissance, transport, anti-submarine warfare support, force projection and the protection of sea lanes. In the Indo-Pacific, the maritime and continental theatres are tightly linked: a force trained for joint integration can support deterrence over land and sea.

That matters because several strategic flashpoints in the region combine air, naval and ground-based threats. From the Arabian Sea to the Bay of Bengal and from the South China Sea to western air approaches, commanders need to understand how air power can work with naval forces without losing situational awareness or creating needless escalation. In that sense, a multinational exercise becomes a rehearsal for coordinated strategic signalling as well as tactical integration.

India’s own maritime security challenges also strengthen the logic of such drills. Rapid deployment, air defence coverage, tanker support and emergency recovery are all easier to rehearse with multinational partners than through purely national training cycles.

Regional actors and diplomacy

Tarang Shakti is read through a regional lens. For India, the exercise sends a message of operational confidence without necessarily escalating into explicit alliance politics. It can be framed as a modern training event that reflects Indian defence capacity and interest in standardised practices for air power cooperation.

For other states, the exercise offers a practical barometer of how comfortable they are with operational coordination under Indian leadership. It also tests how visible the relationship is in a region where states are often reluctant to appear to be aligning too openly against a single power. The diplomatic value of such exercises is often implicit rather than overt.

Verify Fact The exercise’s diplomatic meaning is strongest when read as a signal of readiness, not as a formal military alliance. The practical defence value is usually clearer than any immediate political commitment.

Capability gains and trade-offs

There are clear strategic gains from exercises of this scale. They sharpen interoperability, allow partner states to learn from India’s operational context, and demonstrate that Indian forces can handle a more diverse theatre of operations than a purely bilateral drill permits. They also strengthen the reputational value of domestic force modernisation by showing that new systems can function in larger, more demanding missions.

Yet there are trade-offs. A multinational exercise can consume scarce maintenance time, aircrew availability and infrastructure. It can also create a false sense of readiness if participants are not tested under the sort of degraded conditions that real war creates: jamming, cyber disruption, weather, fuel constraints and command uncertainty. The strategic value of such training lies in how honestly it exposes risk without overplaying the optics.

The lesson is that technical compatibility matters, but it is not enough. What counts is operational trust, shared procedures, resource realism and joint learning. In other words, a well-run exercise improves the force only if it leads to changes in planning, procurement and doctrine.

Risks, limitations and blind spots

Every multinational air exercise has limits. Simulated combat cannot fully replicate battlefield pressure, political escalation, mobilisation timelines, or the effects of cyber disruption, ISR deception and missile attrition. Training also tends to work best in controlled conditions; real war is rarely clean. That gap matters because tactical competence is partly measured by how a force reacts when information becomes ambiguous or assets are degraded.

Another risk is over-reading the event as a “proof of strategic alignment” when it is more accurately a demonstration of readiness and professional familiarity. The presence of many aircraft is a powerful visual, but the less visible learning — shared maintenance routines, data-links, doctrine adaptation and contingency planning — is often the real strategic gain.

Finally, there is a risk of overconfidence. A successful multinational exercise can improve confidence and credibility, but only if decision-makers remain honest about limitations. The most important question is not “did the exercise look impressive?” but “what did it reveal about our operational gaps, and what did we change afterward?”

Perspectives and controversies

Analysts often read Tarang Shakti through three lenses. The defence-planning view treats it as a critical readiness event for force integration and training realism. The geopolitical view sees it as a signal of deterrence and strategic confidence in an increasingly contested Indo-Pacific. The sceptical view argues that exercises can overstate capability if they do not test hostile conditions, degraded communications or sustained attrition.

These disagreements are not trivial. A well-managed exercise can strengthen deterrence, but it can also become a form of political theatre if the operational lessons are shallow or the event is treated as an image campaign rather than a professional test. The most credible conclusion is that Tarang Shakti is a useful instrument of readiness and signalling — but only when judged in operational terms, not headlines alone.

Strategic takeaway The real question is not whether the exercise is dramatic, but whether it exposes gaps, builds trust and improves the system’s ability to fight and sustain operations in a degraded environment.

Scenarios and strategic options

Scenario planning helps convert an exercise into a strategic instrument. One likely scenario is a rapid deterioration in airspace security near a contested border or maritime corridor, requiring coordinated fighter patrols, ISR, tanker support and air defence engagement. Another is a response to a regional military event that demands quick intelligence sharing and force dispersion across multiple bases.

India’s strategic options include deepening joint doctrine with partner aircraft and information-sharing systems, expanding maintenance resilience, investing in airborne early warning and command nodes, and refining procedures for high-threat, degraded communications environments. A wider option is to align training more closely with realistic integrated missions involving air, navy and missile defence elements.

These options are not mutually exclusive. The main challenge is not choosing a single doctrine, but ensuring the force can operate flexibly across different risks — from air patrols and surveillance to rapid response and strategic deterrence. Scenario-based planning keeps that debate grounded in operational realism rather than symbolic signalling alone.

Glossary

Air superiority
Control of the airspace relevant to military operations, enabling friendly movement and constraining enemy freedom of action.
AEW&C
Airborne early warning and control systems that detect threats, coordinate intercepts and improve situational awareness.
Interoperability
The ability of different militaries to communicate, coordinate and operate together despite differences in equipment and doctrine.
ISR
Intelligence, surveillance and reconnaissance: the collection and use of data to understand the battlefield and support decisions.
Sabre-rattling
Publicly dramatic displays of military capacity, often designed to deter or signal resolve rather than to initiate combat.
Scenario planning
Structured analysis of possible crisis conditions to test force readiness, options and weaknesses before a real event occurs.
