Using this handbook

The short version: enough to speak accurately on India-China relations, built to be read in two to three hours.

  • First pass: the snapshot, the sector table and the revision sheet.
  • Second pass: Parts I–IV in order.
  • Third pass: answer the practice questions aloud in two minutes each.
How to answer any China question

What is the issue? Why now? What is the background? What is India's interest? The security angle, then the economic angle. The best case for and against. Close with what India should do.

Claims are tagged Established Interpretation Disputed Re-verify. Events after 2024 move fast — check the latest before you speak.

The dispute on one page

What India claims

  • Aksai Chin (~38,000 km²), held by China since the 1950s
  • The Shaksgam Valley (~5,180 km²), ceded by Pakistan to China in 1963
  • The McMahon Line as the eastern boundary

What China claims

  • Almost all of Arunachal Pradesh (~83,743 km²), which it calls “Zangnan”
  • That the McMahon Line is an illegal colonial line
  • Aksai Chin as part of Xinjiang and Tibet
The core problem

There is no agreed boundary, and not even an agreed line of actual control. Both sides patrol to where they believe the line is, and the overlaps are where clashes happen.

The LAC, sector by sector

Why the terrain matters: Tibet is a high plateau with good roads for China, while India's side climbs steeply from the plains. India has historically found it harder to move forces up to the line, though its roads and tunnels have improved rapidly since 2017.

The Siliguri Corridor — the “Chicken's Neck” — is only about 20–22 km wide and connects the Northeast to the rest of India. It lies close to the Chumbi Valley and Doklam.

Part I

Origins and war

Tibet and the colonial lines

The dispute exists because British India never agreed its boundary with China, and Tibet was the buffer between them.

  • West: the British drew several lines. The Johnson Line (1865) put Aksai Chin in Kashmir; the Macartney-MacDonald Line (1899) left most of it to China. Neither was agreed with China. India adopted the Johnson alignment.
  • East: at the Simla Convention (1913–14), Britain and Tibet agreed the McMahon Line along the Himalayan crest. China's delegate initialled but China never ratified it.
  • 1950: the People's Liberation Army entered Tibet. The buffer was gone; India and China now had a direct border.
The logic of both sides

Interpretation India says the boundary follows tradition, custom and the watershed. China says the lines were imposed by imperialism when China was weak. Both have partial evidence. That is why neither will easily concede.

Bhai-bhai to breakdown, 1950–61

  • 1954Panchsheel Agreement (29 April) on trade with Tibet: the Five Principles of Peaceful Coexistence. India gave up its extra-territorial rights in Tibet. The boundary was not discussed.
  • 1956–57China builds a road through Aksai Chin linking Xinjiang and Tibet. India learns of it only in 1957–58.
  • Mar 1959Tibetan uprising; the Dalai Lama flees to India and is given asylum.
  • 1959Clashes at Longju (August) and Kongka La (October).
  • Apr 1960Zhou Enlai visits Delhi. Interpretation Many read his position as an offer to trade recognition of the McMahon Line for Aksai Chin. India refused.
  • 1961India's Forward Policy: small posts pushed into disputed areas to assert claims.

The 1962 war

A month-long war that India lost badly, and that shaped Indian policy for the next sixty years.

  • 20 OctChina attacks in both sectors. Defeat at Namka Chu; Tawang falls.
  • NovSecond offensive: Se La and Bomdila fall; fighting at Walong.
  • 18 NovRezang La: C Company, 13 Kumaon, fights nearly to the last man. Major Shaitan Singh is awarded the Param Vir Chakra. Chushul is held.
  • 21 NovChina declares a unilateral ceasefire and withdraws in the east, keeping Aksai Chin.

Why India lost

  • Political: the belief that China would not attack; the Forward Policy without the forces to back it.
  • Military: troops not acclimatised, poorly equipped and badly led at the top; confused command in the east.
  • Air power: the IAF was not used offensively. Interpretation Many analysts think it could have made a difference.
  • The Henderson Brooks–Bhagat report examined the failures. It remains officially classified; parts were published abroad in 2014.

Part II

The long peace

Nathu La to Sumdorong Chu, 1967–87

  • 1967Nathu La (September) and Cho La (October), Sikkim: Indian troops hold firm and inflict heavy losses. About 88 Indian soldiers died. Disputed Chinese losses are contested.
  • 1975Tulung La, Arunachal: four Assam Rifles personnel killed in an ambush — the last deaths until 2020.
  • 1976Ambassadors exchanged again after 15 years.
  • 1986–87Sumdorong Chu: a standoff near Tawang. Army Chief Gen K. Sundarji's large deployment (Operation Falcon) deters escalation. Arunachal becomes a state in February 1987.
The lesson of 1967

Nathu La showed that 1962 was not destiny. Well-led, well-positioned Indian troops could hold and hit back. It is the most cited counterpoint to the 1962 narrative.

Agreements and the managed border, 1988–2019

From 1988 both sides agreed to keep the boundary dispute from blocking the rest of the relationship. It worked for three decades.

The key agreements

Warning signs: standoffs at Depsang (2013) and Chumar (2014); the LAC was never clarified on maps (only the middle sector maps were exchanged, in 2002). The Wuhan (2018) and Mamallapuram (2019) informal summits sought to reset ties.

Why the system broke

Interpretation The agreements rested on restraint, not on an agreed line. When China's capabilities and ambitions grew and India built roads up to the LAC, each side saw the other changing the status quo.

Part III

The new confrontation

Doklam, 2017

A 73-day standoff on Bhutanese-claimed territory — the first time India deployed troops to stop China on a third country's soil.

  • What: in June 2017 China began extending a road on the Doklam plateau, claimed by Bhutan, towards the Jampheri ridge.
  • Why India acted: the ridge overlooks the Siliguri Corridor. India cited its security interest and its special relationship with Bhutan.
  • Outcome: on 28 August 2017 both sides disengaged from the face-off site. Disputed China has since built extensively elsewhere on the plateau.
What Doklam showed

India was willing to take risks for a small partner, and China was willing to wait and build around a problem. Both lessons fed into 2020.

Galwan and the 2020 crisis

In the spring of 2020, the PLA moved large forces to several points along the LAC in eastern Ladakh. The long peace ended.

  • Apr–May 2020PLA build-up blocks Indian patrols at Pangong Tso (Fingers 4–8), Galwan, Gogra-Hot Springs and Depsang.
  • 15 Jun 2020Galwan clash: hand-to-hand fighting with clubs and stones. 20 Indian soldiers killed, including Col B. Santosh Babu (Maha Vir Chakra). China officially acknowledged 4 deaths in February 2021. Disputed Chinese losses are believed to be higher.
  • 29–30 Aug 2020India occupies the Kailash Range heights on Pangong's south bank — giving it leverage in talks.
  • 2020India bans Chinese apps (TikTok and others) and tightens investment rules (Press Note 3, April 2020).
Why did China move in 2020?

Interpretation Common explanations: Indian roads approaching the LAC (the Darbuk-Shyok-DBO road); the change of Ladakh's status in August 2019; opportunity during COVID-19; and a wider Chinese assertiveness. Most analysts see a mix, not one cause.

Part IV

Balance and rivalry

The military balance

China's advantages

  • A defence budget roughly three times India's Re-verify
  • Better roads, railways and airbases on the Tibetan plateau
  • Unified command under the Western Theatre Command
  • Stronger defence industry; drones, missiles, space and cyber

India's advantages

  • Combat-experienced, high-altitude trained army
  • Chinese airbases on the plateau are high, limiting aircraft payloads Interpretation
  • Improving infrastructure: Atal Tunnel (2020), Sela Tunnel (2024), the Vibrant Villages Programme
  • Partners: the US, France, Japan, Australia; the Quad
The two-front problem

The China-Pakistan partnership means India must plan for pressure on both borders at once. Since 2020, India has shifted forces from the Pakistan front to the China front — a lasting change in its posture.

Part V

Knowledge toolkit

Views, controversies, lesser-known facts

Schools of thought in India
SchoolArgument
HawksChina is an adversary; prioritise deterrence, closer US ties and economic decoupling.
PragmatistsDeter on the border but keep trade and dialogue; avoid a two-front war.
AccommodationistsSeek a boundary settlement; China is too big to confront alone.

Controversies

  • Who started 1962? Neville Maxwell's India's China War blamed India's Forward Policy. Most Indian historians argue China planned the war and chose the timing. Disputed
  • Should India have accepted Zhou's 1960 “swap”? Interpretation No government has been able to cede claimed territory politically.
  • Trade vs security: cutting Chinese imports hurts Indian manufacturers that need Chinese inputs.

Lesser-known facts

  • The Sikkim sector is the only part with a treaty basis (1890) that China generally accepts.
  • From 1975 to 2020 no shots were fired with lethal result on the LAC — the 1996 agreement's no-firearms rule is why Galwan was fought with clubs and stones.
  • India's middle-sector LAC maps are the only ones ever exchanged (2002).

Scenarios and options

Managed rivalry

Most likely. A tense, heavily armed border; limited dialogue; trade continues.

Another clash

Possible at unresolved points or in Arunachal (Yangtse, December 2022, was a reminder).

Two-front crisis

Pressure from China during an India-Pakistan crisis. Low probability, high impact.

Boundary settlement

Unlikely in the near term; needs political will on both sides.

What India can do

  • Military: keep building roads, tunnels and airfields; ISR, drones and long-range fires; jointness.
  • Diplomatic: keep talking at every level; deepen ties with the Quad, France, Japan and ASEAN without joining an alliance.
  • Economic: reduce critical dependence (electronics, solar, APIs) through manufacturing incentives, while allowing investment that does not threaten security.
  • Neighbourhood: be the first responder for Bhutan, Nepal, Sri Lanka and Maldives.
A balanced closing line

Deter firmly, engage steadily, and build the economy — because in the long run the border balance follows the economic balance.

Glossary

LAC
Line of Actual Control — the de facto line separating Indian- and Chinese-held territory; never jointly marked.
McMahon Line
The eastern boundary agreed by Britain and Tibet in 1914.
Aksai Chin
A high plateau in the west, claimed by India, held by China.
Patrolling Point (PP)
A point up to which Indian troops patrol, marking India's perception of the LAC.
Disengagement / De-escalation / De-induction
Pulling troops apart at a face-off / reducing forces in the area / sending them back to peacetime bases.
Buffer zone
An area neither side patrols after disengagement.
Salami slicing
Taking territory in small steps, each too small to justify war.
Grey-zone tactics
Coercion below the threshold of war — maps, names, villages, patrols.
WMCC
Working Mechanism for Consultation and Coordination on border affairs (diplomats).
Corps Commander talks
Military-level talks between senior commanders on the LAC.

One-page revision

Numbers

  • 1962: India ~1,383 killed, ~1,696 missing, ~3,968 POW; China ~722

  • Doklam: 73 days
  • Galwan: 20 Indian dead; China acknowledged 4
  • Siliguri Corridor: 20–22 km
  • Trade deficit: US$85–100 bn+ Re-verify

The arc

Colonial lines never agreed → China in Tibet (1950) → friendship and the Aksai Chin road → 1962 defeat → Nathu La (1967) → the managed peace (1988–2019) → Doklam → Galwan → slow disengagement, no de-escalation.

Ready answers

  • Why 1962 was lost: political miscalculation, poor preparation, air power unused.
  • Why 2020 happened: Indian infrastructure, Ladakh's changed status, opportunity, Chinese assertiveness.
  • What India should do: deter, engage, build the economy, win the neighbourhood.
