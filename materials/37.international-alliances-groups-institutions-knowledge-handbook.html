From alliances to universal institutions

How to use this handbook

Alliance, bloc, forum, institution

The words are often used loosely, and mixing them up leads to wrong answers. A military alliance is a treaty in which members promise to help one another, as in NATO's Article 5. Collective security is different: members agree to act together against any aggressor, including one of their own, which is the UN Security Council idea. A bloc or grouping is a looser set of states with shared interests, such as BRICS. A forum is a regular meeting without binding power, such as the G20. An institution has a secretariat, rules and often a budget, such as the IMF. A regime is a set of rules around one subject, such as the nuclear non-proliferation regime.

Five forms compared

Why the difference matters for India

India has avoided treaty alliances since independence but joins many groupings and institutions, including some that rival one another. Understanding the forms explains how that is possible: it takes on no defence duties but gains a voice in many rooms. Interpretation

The United Nations system

The UN was founded in 1945, after the Charter was signed at San Francisco in June and came into force on 24 October. It has 193 member states, with the Holy See and Palestine as non-member observers. India was an original member. The UN's purposes are to maintain peace and security, develop friendly relations, promote human rights and development, and be a centre for harmonising national action. The IMF and World Bank are formally specialised agencies but work independently. The UN is funded by assessed contributions based on ability to pay and by voluntary funds, and funding gaps have repeatedly limited operations. Re-verify the current scale of contributions

The Security Council, the veto and reform

The Security Council has 15 members: five permanent (the United States, United Kingdom, France, Russia and China) and ten elected for two-year terms, with seats shared among regions. A resolution needs nine votes and no veto by a permanent member. Chapter VI of the Charter covers peaceful settlement, and Chapter VII allows sanctions and force. Russia took the Soviet seat in 1991, and the People's Republic of China replaced the Republic of China in 1971 under General Assembly Resolution 2758. Established

  • The veto reflects the power balance of 1945 and has blocked action on Syria, Ukraine, Gaza and other crises. The General Assembly's 1950 "Uniting for Peace" resolution and a 2022 rule requiring the Assembly to debate after a veto are partial responses.
  • India's record: India has been elected eight times, most recently for 2021-22, and has an endorsed candidacy for a later term. Re-verify
  • Reform camps: the G4 (India, Brazil, Germany, Japan) seek permanent seats. The "Uniting for Consensus" group, which includes Pakistan and Italy, opposes new permanent seats. The African Union's Ezulwini Consensus asks for two permanent seats with veto for Africa.
  • Why reform stalls: a Charter amendment needs two-thirds of the General Assembly and ratification by all five permanent members, so any one can block it, and talks have continued since 2009 without a text. Established
India's case and its limits

India points to its population, economy, peacekeeping record and nuclear status. Opponents point to regional rivals, doubts about new vetoes and the practical need for the support of every permanent member. China's position is the one most often discussed. Disputed

Peacekeeping and India's role

UN peacekeeping began in 1948 with observers in the Middle East. It is not in the Charter as such, and is often called "Chapter VI and a half". Traditional principles are consent of the parties, impartiality and non-use of force except in self-defence and defence of the mandate, though some missions have robust mandates under Chapter VII. Missions are authorised by the Security Council, staffed by contributed national units, and paid for from a separate peacekeeping budget. Established

  • India's contribution: India has served in dozens of missions since the Korean War custodian force in the 1950s, and has sent a very large cumulative number of personnel, with the loss of well over a hundred Indian peacekeepers. Re-verify the figures
  • Notable firsts: India deployed the first all-women formed police unit to Liberia in 2007.
  • Current areas: Indian units have served in Lebanon, South Sudan, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, the Golan Heights and Abyei, among others. Re-verify current deployments
  • Criticisms: troop contributors want more say in mandates, and missions have faced allegations of abuse and failures to protect civilians.
Link to the Security Council bid

India argues that major troop contributors should be consulted in drafting mandates and uses its record to support its claim for a larger role. Interpretation

Part II · Security alliances and blocs

Who is bound to defend whom

NATO

The North Atlantic Treaty Organization was created by the Washington Treaty of 4 April 1949 to counter Soviet power in Europe. Its core is Article 5: an armed attack on one member is treated as an attack on all, with each ally taking "such action as it deems necessary", which does not automatically mean military force. Article 4 allows consultation when a member feels threatened. Article 5 has been invoked once, after the attacks of 11 September 2001. Established

  • Membership: 32 members after Finland (2023) and Sweden (2024) joined, a direct consequence of Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine. The headquarters are in Brussels and the Secretary General since 2024 is Mark Rutte. Re-verify
  • History: the Warsaw Pact (1955-91) was the Soviet counterpart. France left the integrated military command in 1966 and rejoined in 2009. Enlargement after 1999 is central to Russia's grievances. Disputed
  • Spending: at the 2025 summit allies agreed a target of 5% of GDP on defence and related spending by 2035. Re-verify
  • Partners: NATO has partnership programmes and works with Indo-Pacific partners such as Japan, Australia, South Korea and New Zealand. India is not a partner.
  • Strains: burden sharing, differing views on Russia, and questions about US commitment have appeared repeatedly. Interpretation
Not a global alliance

Article 5 covers only the territory of members in Europe and North America, so it does not extend to the Indo-Pacific. The "NATO-plus" and "Asian NATO" labels sometimes applied to the Quad are inaccurate. Established

US alliances, AUKUS, Quad and Five Eyes

Outside Europe the United States built bilateral treaty alliances, the "hub and spoke" system: Japan (1951, revised 1960), South Korea (1953), the Philippines (1951), Australia and New Zealand (ANZUS, 1951) and Thailand. Each spoke is tied to the US, not necessarily to the others, though trilateral links such as US-Japan-South Korea cooperation have grown since 2023. Established

The Quad has no treaty, secretariat or mutual-defence clause. Its agenda includes maritime domain awareness, vaccines, critical technology, infrastructure and humanitarian relief, and it began with the 2004 tsunami core group. Leaders' summits have been delayed on occasion, and the group's pace depends on US-India ties, which were strained in 2025 by tariff disputes. Re-verify the present position

India and the US framework

India signed the foundational military agreements (GSOMIA, LEMOA, COMCASA, BECA) but stays outside any treaty alliance. It values access and interoperability while avoiding obligation. Interpretation

CSTO, EAEU and the SCO

The Collective Security Treaty Organisation (CSTO) is Russia's alliance in the former Soviet space. Its treaty dates from 1992, and its members have included Russia, Belarus, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan and Armenia, though Armenia has frozen its participation. The CSTO sent forces to Kazakhstan in January 2022 but did not act in the Armenia-Azerbaijan conflicts, which raised doubts about its reliability. Re-verify membership The Eurasian Economic Union (2015) is its economic counterpart.

The Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO) grew from the "Shanghai Five" of 1996 and was formed in 2001. India and Pakistan joined in 2017, Iran in 2023 and Belarus in 2024, giving ten members. Re-verify Its regional anti-terrorism structure is in Tashkent, and its main activities are security consultation, counter-terrorism exercises and economic projects. It is not a military alliance.

  • India's interests: access to Central Asia, a platform on terrorism, and a seat in a non-Western body.
  • India's reservations: India has declined to endorse China's Belt and Road because the China-Pakistan Economic Corridor crosses disputed territory, and it has had differences over how terrorism is described in joint texts.
  • Other treaties: China's only formal treaty ally is North Korea (1961), and Russia and North Korea signed a mutual-defence treaty in 2024. Re-verify
Contrast

The CSTO is a collective-defence alliance, while the SCO is a grouping with a security agenda. In an answer, never call the SCO "the Russian-Chinese NATO". Interpretation

Arms-control and export-control regimes

These regimes decide who may hold or trade sensitive technology, and they directly affect India. The Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT), opened in 1968 and in force from 1970, recognises five nuclear-weapon states, defined as those that had tested before 1 January 1967. India, Pakistan and Israel never joined, and India calls the treaty discriminatory. North Korea withdrew in 2003. The Comprehensive Test Ban Treaty (1996) is not in force and India has not signed it. The 1974 link

The NSG was founded in response to India's 1974 nuclear test, which is why its membership is a recurring issue for India. Established

Part III · Economic, regional and legal bodies

Forums, regional groupings and rule-makers

G7 and G20

The G7 (Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, the United Kingdom and the United States, with the EU attending) began in 1975 as a group of advanced economies. Russia joined in 1997 to make a G8 and was suspended after annexing Crimea in 2014. The G7 is a club of like-minded democracies with no treaty or secretariat, and it often invites India and other partners as guests. Established

The G20 was created in 1999 for finance ministers after the Asian financial crisis, and raised to leaders' level in 2008 during the global financial crisis. It has 19 countries, the European Union and, since 2023, the African Union, which together account for most of world output, trade and population. It decides by consensus and has no binding power. Established

  • India's presidency (December 2022 to November 2023) produced the New Delhi Declaration by consensus despite divisions over Ukraine, the admission of the African Union and the India-Middle East-Europe Economic Corridor announcement.
  • Later hosts: Brazil (2024), South Africa (2025) and the United States (2026). Re-verify the present status
  • Limits: the G20's agenda is wide, outcomes are voluntary, and it is strained when major members disagree.
Club versus forum

The G7 is a club of similar economies, and the G20 is a broader forum with rivals sitting together. India belongs only to the second, though it attends the first as a guest. Interpretation

BRICS and its expansion

The acronym BRIC was coined in 2001 by an economist at Goldman Sachs for Brazil, Russia, India and China. The four held their first summit in 2009, and South Africa joined in 2010. From January 2024, Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran and the United Arab Emirates joined, and Indonesia joined in 2025. A new "partner country" category was created in 2024-25. Saudi Arabia was invited but its status has been unclear. Re-verify the member and partner lists

  • Institutions: the New Development Bank (2015, Shanghai) and the Contingent Reserve Arrangement.
  • Agenda: reform of global governance, development finance, and a greater voice for the Global South.
  • Differences: members hold diverse positions. China and Russia see BRICS as a counterweight to Western power, while India and Brazil stress that it is not anti-Western. Disputed
  • Currency: proposals for a common currency or for de-dollarisation have been discussed, but India has said it does not seek to replace the dollar. Re-verify
  • India's 2026 role: India holds the chair and hosts the summit in 2026. Re-verify
Expansion dilemma

Larger membership raises BRICS's weight but makes consensus harder and increases the risk that the group is seen as a China-led bloc, which is a concern for India. Interpretation

South Asia and the Indian Ocean

The South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation (SAARC) was formed in Dhaka in 1985 with seven members, and Afghanistan joined in 2007. It has been largely inactive since the 2016 Islamabad summit was cancelled after India and others withdrew following the Uri attack. Pakistan's refusal to allow transit and India-Pakistan hostility are the main obstacles. Established

  • BIMSTEC (Bay of Bengal Initiative for Multi-Sectoral Technical and Economic Cooperation, 1997) links Bangladesh, India, Myanmar, Sri Lanka, Thailand, Nepal and Bhutan, with a secretariat in Dhaka. India favours it as a SAARC alternative that excludes Pakistan. Its sixth summit was held in Bangkok in 2025. Re-verify
  • IORA (Indian Ocean Rim Association, 1997) covers the wider region, and the Colombo Security Conclave deals with maritime security among India, Sri Lanka, the Maldives and Mauritius. Re-verify membership See handbook 10 for the maritime detail.
  • Competing frameworks: China has sought its own regional formats with Pakistan and Bangladesh, and the extent of these is evolving. Re-verify
  • India's approach: the "Neighbourhood First" policy prefers sub-regional links (BBIN, BIMSTEC) to a stalled SAARC.

IMF, World Bank, WTO, NDB and AIIB

The IMF and World Bank were designed at the Bretton Woods conference in 1944. The IMF supports balance of payments and monetary cooperation, and its votes are weighted by quota, with the United States holding the largest share and an effective veto over decisions needing an 85% majority. The World Bank lends for development, and its president since 2023 is Ajay Banga. Established The WTO replaced the GATT in 1995, works by consensus, and has a dispute-settlement system whose Appellate Body has been paralysed since late 2019 because of blocked appointments. Re-verify

Reform argument

Emerging economies say that voting power in the Bretton Woods bodies still reflects 1945 and that the newer banks give them alternatives. Western governments reply that the older institutions set higher standards and that newer ones can be used for influence. Interpretation

ICJ, ICC, IAEA and WHO

  • International Court of Justice (ICJ): the UN's judicial organ, with 15 judges elected for nine-year terms by the General Assembly and Security Council. It decides disputes between states that accept its jurisdiction and gives advisory opinions. Judgments bind the parties but enforcement depends on the Security Council. In 2019 it ruled against Pakistan on consular access in the Kulbhushan Jadhav case. Established
  • International Criminal Court (ICC): set up by the 1998 Rome Statute, in force 2002, to try individuals for genocide, war crimes and crimes against humanity. India, the United States, China and Russia are not parties. Arrest warrants against Vladimir Putin (2023) and Benjamin Netanyahu (2024) show both its reach and its enforcement limits. Re-verify
  • IAEA: the International Atomic Energy Agency (1957, Vienna) promotes peaceful nuclear use and runs safeguards. India has an India-specific safeguards agreement from 2008 that supports civil nuclear trade.
  • WHO: founded 1948, it coordinates global health. The United States announced its withdrawal in 2025, and its effect on funding is still being worked out. Re-verify
  • Other bodies: the Permanent Court of Arbitration and the tribunal under UNCLOS settled the 2016 South China Sea case that China rejects, and a 2025 arbitration dispute over the Indus Waters Treaty is contested by India. Disputed
Law without police

International courts rely on consent and political pressure rather than force, so powerful states can ignore them at a cost. India accepts the ICJ's jurisdiction with reservations and does not accept the ICC's. Interpretation

Part IV · India's position

Autonomy without isolation

Non-alignment to multi-alignment

India refused to join either bloc in the Cold War. Nehru's non-alignment found expression at Bandung in 1955 and in the Non-Aligned Movement founded in Belgrade in 1961. In practice India leaned toward the Soviet Union, culminating in the 1971 Treaty of Peace, Friendship and Cooperation. After 1991 it opened to the United States and the West while keeping its Russian ties. Today's policy is usually called strategic autonomy or multi-alignment: partnerships on specific issues with different powers, without treaty alliances. Established

India also joins smaller minilaterals such as I2U2 (India, Israel, UAE, United States) and RIC (Russia-India-China, dormant for years), and promotes connectivity projects such as the India-Middle East-Europe Economic Corridor. Re-verify the active status of each Critics say multi-alignment risks being seen as unreliable by all sides, and supporters say it maximises options in a world with several power centres. Disputed

The test of autonomy

Autonomy is tested when two partners clash, as with Russian oil purchases and Western sanctions, or tariffs and trade deals with the United States. How India answers in practice, more than its slogans, shows its policy. Interpretation

Perspectives, controversies and facts

Interpretation Are alliances stabilising? Supporters say they deter aggression and reassure members. Critics say they raise tension, trap members in others' quarrels and provoke counter-blocs.

Interpretation Is the UN still relevant? It cannot stop wars involving permanent members, yet its humanitarian, health, peacekeeping and norm-setting work continues, and there is no replacement.

Interpretation BRICS: counterweight or talking shop? It gives emerging economies visibility and alternative finance, but its members disagree on goals.

Interpretation Is multi-alignment sustainable? It works while major powers compete for India's support, and may be harder if they press for a choice.

  • Established India joined three of the four export-control regimes after 2016.
  • Established The African Union became a permanent G20 member at the 2023 New Delhi summit.
  • Established The Quad has no treaty and no permanent secretariat.

Scenarios and strategic options

Presented neutrally, without recommending a political position:

A Security Council seat remains out of reach

Reform talks stall for another decade. India must decide whether to keep investing in the effort or to prioritise other forums.

BRICS turns more anti-Western

Expanded membership pulls the group toward confrontation with the West. India must weigh staying to moderate against reducing its role.

The Quad is asked to take on a defence role

Members push for more military coordination. India must decide how far to go without a treaty.

US-China rivalry forces choices

Partners press India to align on technology, trade and sanctions. Autonomy depends on India's bargaining power.

Answer framework and worked example

For any question on a grouping or institution, move through definition, location, evidence, contest, analysis, test, position, concession and close. One compressed example follows.

Worked example: "Should India join a formal military alliance?"

Define
A formal military alliance is a treaty that commits members to defend one another, as in NATO's Article 5.
Locate
Existing alliances are mostly Euro-Atlantic and US-centred in East Asia, with Russian and Chinese equivalents in Eurasia and Korea.
Establish
India has stayed outside treaty alliances since 1947, signed the 1971 treaty with the Soviet Union, and today joins the Quad and SCO without defence obligations.
Contest
Supporters say an alliance would deter China and give security guarantees. Opponents say it would reduce choice, drag India into others' wars and strain ties with Russia and others.
Analyse
India's main threats are land borders and terrorism, which allies are unlikely to fight for it, while its main need is technology, finance and diplomatic cover, which partnerships can provide without treaties.
Test
Look at whether allies stand by their guarantees (the CSTO case), and whether India's partners respect its red lines in practice.
Position
India is better served by issue-based partnerships and strong national capability than by a treaty alliance.
Concede
If a direct threat from a major power grew sharply, the balance could change. Re-verify the current context
Close
Autonomy is a means of keeping options open, and it works only if India keeps building its own strength.

Practice bank: questions and motions

Fourteen questions. Answer each aloud, standing, in ninety seconds.
