“Wars are fought with the weapons you have, not the weapons you ordered.”

An old staff-college maxim, and the whole of this subject in one line. Modernisation is not a shopping list. It is the system that decides what to buy, who builds it, how quickly it arrives, whether it can be sustained in a long war, and whether the people who use it have been trained and organised to fight with it together.

Using this handbook

This is a reference document, not a narrative. Read Section 2 (the answer framework), Section 3 (the snapshot) and Section 57 (the revision sheet) first. Then read Part V — Atmanirbhar Defence — because it is the part of this subject on which boards most often probe, and the part on which candidates most often recite slogans instead of arguments.

Four labels used throughout

Defence procurement is a field of shifting numbers: contract values, delivery dates, squadron counts and indigenous-content percentages are revised constantly, and some are deliberately kept vague. Four tags are used:

Established Documented and broadly agreed — contract signatures, commissioning dates, official budget figures, published committee reports.

Interpretation A reasonable reading that serious analysts dispute. Adopt one if you like, but say you are adopting one.

Disputed Figures or claims where official and independent sources differ materially — indigenous-content percentages, combat-loss claims, the real cost of a programme.

Re-verify Fast-moving. Anything after 2024 in this document — contract signings, deliveries, office holders, budget numbers — must be re-checked against the Ministry of Defence, PIB and the latest Union Budget in the week before your interview.

The single most useful habit

Separate platform, capability and system in every sentence. A Rafale is a platform. Long-range precision strike is a capability. The chain that finds a target, passes the data, authorises the strike and assesses the damage is the system. India has repeatedly bought platforms without the system around them — and the most sophisticated answer you can give to almost any modernisation question is to ask which of the three is actually missing.

What this handbook deliberately does

  • Treats Atmanirbharta as a strategy, not a virtue. Indigenisation is strategically necessary in some areas and economically irrational in others. The question is always where and at what cost, never simply whether.
  • Gives the delays and failures their due. Tejas, Arjun, INSAS and Kaveri are not embarrassments to be hidden; they are the case studies from which current policy was learned. A candidate who can explain why they were slow sounds far more credible than one who calls them triumphs.
  • Refuses the two lazy poles. Neither “DRDO has failed, just buy the best from abroad” nor “everything must be made in India” survives contact with the record.
  • Keeps money in the frame. Every capability discussed has a cost, and the defence budget is a fixed pie shared between salaries, pensions, running costs and new equipment. Section 16 is the most important background for everything else.
  • Presents India's options neutrally. Section 50 lays out choices and their costs. It does not tell you which to prefer.

A word on numbers. Contract values are quoted in the currency and year in which they were announced, and are approximate. Squadron counts, fleet sizes and inventory numbers are open-source estimates; official figures are not always published. In an interview, “approximately, and I would want to check the latest figure” is a strong answer, not a weak one.

The nine-step answer framework

One structure answers almost every question on this subject. Whether the board asks about the CDS, Tejas, the S-400, Agnipath or iDEX, it is asking the same nine questions in a different order.

  1. What?

    State the programme, reform or policy in one or two sentences — what it is, who runs it, when it began.

    Trap: starting with specifications. The board wants to know you understand what the thing is for before it wants to hear its range.

  2. Why?

    What capability gap or institutional failure produced it? Separate the trigger (a war, a scandal, a crisis) from the driver (a structural need).

    Trap: “to make India strong.” Name the gap: squadron shortfall, artillery obsolescence, import dependence, lack of jointness.

  3. Background?

    The minimum history: two or three dates that explain the present. For most programmes, the sanction date and one turning point are enough.

    Trap: narrating forty years of Tejas history. Say 1983, 2001, 2016, and move on.

  4. India's interest?

    Deterrence, operational readiness, strategic autonomy, industrial capacity, jobs, exports, supply-chain security — say which, and whether vital or merely desirable.

    Trap: treating “self-reliance” as self-evidently the interest. Self-reliance serves autonomy; ask whether it serves readiness too, or trades against it.

  5. Security implication?

    What does it change on the ground — force levels, readiness, the two-front calculation, sustainability in a long war, vulnerability to sanctions?

    Trap: “it strengthens our security.” Against whom, where, and by when?

  6. Economic implication?

    Cost, opportunity cost, domestic value addition, employment, export potential, foreign-exchange saving, and the salary-pension share of the budget.

    Trap: skipping this rung. On this topic it is often the decisive one.

  7. Argument for?

    The strongest case for the programme or policy, as its advocates would make it.

    Trap: a straw man. The case for importing, or for Agnipath, or for going slow on theatre commands, deserves to be stated properly.

  8. Argument against?

    The strongest case against, stated with equal force.

    Trap: omitting this rung because it sounds unpatriotic. Conceding a real weakness and then answering it is what makes a position sound earned.

  9. What should India do?

    A recommendation that is sequenced, affordable and names its own cost. Then stop talking.

    Trap: “India should become self-reliant in all defence technology.” That is a sentiment, not a policy.

Worked example · Is the Tejas a success?

What
India's indigenous single-engine light combat aircraft, designed by the Aeronautical Development Agency (ADA) and built by HAL, in squadron service with the IAF since 2016.
Why
The LCA programme was sanctioned in 1983 to replace the MiG-21 and to create an Indian capability to design a modern fighter — the second purpose was as important as the first.
Background
Sanctioned 1983; first flight 4 January 2001; Initial Operational Clearance 2013; Final Operational Clearance 2019; 83 Mk1A ordered in 2021.
India's interest
Squadron numbers, which have fallen well below the sanctioned 42; and a design base — people, test infrastructure, certification capacity — without which no future Indian fighter is possible.
Security
A light fighter fills numbers but cannot on its own replace heavier multi-role aircraft; the squadron gap persists because of slow production and engine supply.
Economic
Hundreds of Indian suppliers, a certification ecosystem, and the foundation for Mk2 and AMCA. But the engine is American, and so is a large share of the value.
For
It flies, it is in service, it has a good safety record, and it created an aerospace ecosystem that did not exist in 1983. The alternative was permanent dependence.
Against
Over three decades from sanction to squadron service; the aircraft it was meant to replace had to be flown far beyond their intended life, with losses; and it still depends on an imported engine.
India should
Treat Tejas as a production problem now, not a design problem — raise the production rate, add lines, secure engine supply — and apply its lessons to AMCA: freeze requirements early, embed the user from the start, and fund the engine separately. Cost: accepting that for a decade the IAF will fly fewer squadrons than it wants.

The vocabulary of procurement

Half the difficulty of this subject is jargon. These are the terms a board member may drop into a question to see whether you follow.

AoN — Acceptance of Necessity
The first formal approval, granted by the Defence Acquisition Council, that a capability is needed. An AoN is not a contract; many AoNs never become one. When a headline says “₹X crore approved,” it usually means AoN.
DAC — Defence Acquisition Council
Chaired by the Raksha Mantri; the apex body that approves acquisition categories and AoNs.
CCS — Cabinet Committee on Security
Chaired by the Prime Minister; must approve high-value contracts before signature.
IDDM — Indigenously Designed, Developed and Manufactured
The highest-priority acquisition category, introduced in DPP 2016, requiring Indian design and a minimum level of indigenous content.
IC — Indigenous Content
The share of a product's cost that is Indian. Definitions and measurement are contested — a “70% IC” figure may conceal an imported engine or chip that is 30% of cost and 100% of the dependency.
QR / SQR — (Staff) Qualitative Requirements
The specifications a service sets. Over-ambitious or frequently changed QRs are among the most common causes of indigenous programme delay.
L1
Lowest bidder among technically compliant vendors. The default selection rule, criticised for rewarding cheapness over lifecycle value.
FMS — Foreign Military Sales
The US government-to-government route, in which Washington procures on India's behalf.
IGA / G2G
Inter-governmental agreement; a direct deal between governments, as for the Rafale and the S-400.
ToT — Transfer of Technology
Often means a licence to manufacture rather than genuine transfer of design know-how. The distinction is central to the Atmanirbhar debate.
Offsets
An obligation on a foreign vendor to spend a share of the contract value in India. Largely dropped in DAP 2020 for government-to-government and single-vendor deals.
PIL — Positive Indigenisation List
A list of items the services (or DPSUs) may no longer import after a stated date.

Part I

Inheritance and the lessons of war, 1947-1999

1947: what India inherited

India began independence with a large, battle-experienced army, a small navy and air force, a network of colonial-era ordnance factories — and almost no capacity to design a modern weapon. Every argument about Atmanirbharta starts here.

Army

The British Indian Army was divided roughly two-to-one between India and Pakistan. Two million Indians had served in the Second World War, so experience was abundant; senior Indian officers were few, because Indianisation of the officer corps had been slow. Equipment was British, much of it war-worn.

Navy

The Royal Indian Navy was a coastal force of sloops, frigates and minesweepers. Its first cruiser, INS Delhi, came in 1948; the first aircraft carrier, INS Vikrant, in 1961 — both second-hand British ships.

Air Force

The Royal Indian Air Force had a handful of squadrons of Spitfires, Tempests and Dakotas. The Dakota airlift to Srinagar on 27 October 1947 was its defining early act.

The industrial base

  • Ordnance factories. A chain of government arsenals, the oldest dating to the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries under the East India Company. They made small arms, ammunition and clothing to British designs — production capacity, not design capacity.
  • Hindustan Aircraft. Founded at Bangalore in 1940 by the industrialist Walchand Hirachand with the Mysore government, taken over by the Government of India during the war, and merged with Aeronautics India in 1964 to form Hindustan Aeronautics Limited (HAL).
  • Shipyards. Mazagon Dock in Bombay and Garden Reach in Calcutta were repair and small-craft yards; neither had built a warship.
  • Research. Scattered technical development establishments. The Defence Research and Development Organisation (DRDO) was formed only in 1958.
The founding choice

Nehru's government prioritised economic development over military spending, and defence spending stayed below about 2% of GDP through the 1950s. Interpretation Critics call this complacency that set up 1962; defenders argue that a poor, newly independent country had to build its economy first and could not have afforded a large modern military anyway. Both views have evidence behind them; the stronger answer is that the problem was less the amount spent than the absence of a threat assessment linking spending to a likely adversary.

1947-48 and 1962: the hard lessons

The first war was won by improvisation. The second was lost by the absence of a system. Between them they taught India that political direction, intelligence, roads and equipment must all move together — a lesson that took decades to institutionalise.

1947-48: improvisation

The Kashmir war was fought with the inheritance: Dakotas flying troops into Srinagar, Stuart tanks hauled over the Zoji La in November 1948 — at the time the highest altitude at which tanks had operated — and infantry battalions pulled from partition duties. Established It showed that the inherited force could fight, but that logistics and air mobility would decide mountain warfare.

1962: failure of system

The Sino-Indian war of October-November 1962 is covered in depth in the India-China handbook. For modernisation, five lessons matter:

  1. Equipment. Many Indian infantrymen still carried the bolt-action .303 Lee-Enfield against Chinese semi-automatic and automatic weapons. The first Indian self-loading rifle, the 7.62 mm Ishapore 1A, entered production only after the war.
  2. Clothing and acclimatisation. Troops were sent to high altitude without winter clothing or time to acclimatise.
  3. Roads. There was almost no road network to the frontier; the Chinese had built one on the plateau.
  4. Air power. The IAF's combat arm was not used, for fear of escalation and of Chinese retaliation against cities. Interpretation Many analysts now argue this was a serious error.
  5. Higher direction. Political decisions outran military means, and professional military advice was not sought or not heeded. The Henderson Brooks-Bhagat report remains officially classified.

The post-1962 expansion

  • A First Defence Plan (1964-69) — the first attempt at five-year defence planning.
  • The Army expanded sharply, with new mountain divisions raised for the northern border.
  • The IAF set a target of 45 squadrons (later settled at the long-standing sanctioned figure of 42 in the 1990s).
  • The first MiG-21 deal with the Soviet Union (signed 1962) included licence production by HAL — the beginning of the Soviet relationship that would dominate Indian inventories for half a century.
  • The Border Roads Organisation, founded in 1960, was given new urgency.
The line to remember

1962 was not lost by soldiers. It was lost by a system that sent them to the wrong places, without roads, clothing, weapons or air support, on the basis of an untested assumption that China would not fight. Every subsequent reform — from the CDS to border infrastructure — is in some way an answer to 1962.

1965 and 1971: the post-1962 force tested

The 1965 war was a stalemate fought with a force halfway through its post-1962 rebuilding. The 1971 war was the rebuilt force's decisive victory — and it was won as much by planning, timing and jointness as by equipment.

1965

  • Pakistan fielded American M47/M48 Patton tanks and F-86 Sabres and F-104 Starfighters, supplied under its alliance with the United States. India fielded British Centurions, older Shermans, and a mixed air force of Hunters, Mysteres, Gnats and a few MiG-21s.
  • The small Folland Gnat, licence-built by HAL, earned the nickname “Sabre Slayer.” The Battle of Asal Uttar became known for the destruction of large numbers of Pattons.
  • The lesson for modernisation: dependence is a strategic vulnerability. Both the US and Britain imposed arms embargoes on both sides. This pushed India further towards Soviet supply — and, more slowly, towards thinking about self-reliance.

1971

  • The Indo-Soviet Treaty of Peace, Friendship and Cooperation (August 1971) secured diplomatic cover and supply.
  • Soviet equipment — MiG-21s, Su-7s, T-55 tanks, PT-76 amphibious tanks, Osa-class missile boats — was used alongside British and Indian systems.
  • Operation Trident (4-5 December 1971): Osa missile boats towed towards Karachi fired Soviet Styx anti-ship missiles, sinking Pakistani warships and setting fuel stores ablaze. 4 December is Navy Day for this reason.
  • The Army's rapid advance to Dhaka, supported by the IAF and a naval blockade (with INS Vikrant in the Bay of Bengal), ended in the surrender of 16 December 1971.
What 1971 teaches modernisation

The equipment was not better than Pakistan's on paper. What was better was preparation time (the Army Chief, General Sam Manekshaw, is widely credited with persuading the government to wait until winter), inter-service coordination, and clarity of political aim. Interpretation The coordination was achieved through personal relationships among the three chiefs rather than through institutions — which is exactly why the jointness debate continues fifty years later.

1974 and the strategic programmes

The first nuclear test brought technology denial. Technology denial, paradoxically, forced India to build its own missiles, space launchers and nuclear submarine — the areas where Indian indigenisation has been most successful.

  • 18 May 1974Pokhran-I (“Smiling Buddha”), described by India as a peaceful nuclear explosion.
  • 1974-75The Nuclear Suppliers Group is formed, largely in response. Western technology controls on India tighten for three decades.
  • 1983Integrated Guided Missile Development Programme (IGMDP) launched under Dr A. P. J. Abdul Kalam: Prithvi, Agni, Trishul, Akash and Nag.
  • 1983Light Combat Aircraft programme sanctioned.
  • 1987The Missile Technology Control Regime (MTCR) is established by Western states, restricting missile and space-launch technology transfers.
  • 1998Pokhran-II (11 and 13 May). India declares itself a nuclear weapon state; US and other sanctions follow.
  • 2016-17India joins the MTCR (2016), the Wassenaar Arrangement (2017) and the Australia Group (2018). NSG membership remains blocked, principally by China.
The paradox of denial

Interpretation India's strongest indigenous capabilities — ballistic missiles, space launch, the nuclear submarine reactor — are precisely the ones where no foreign supplier would sell. Where imports were available, as for fighters, tanks and artillery, indigenous programmes competed against a readily available foreign alternative and struggled for user support. Denial created the one condition indigenous programmes most need: a customer with no other option.

Meghdoot, Brasstacks, Pawan, Cactus

Four operations of the 1980s, each teaching something different about what a modern military needed.

Operation Meghdoot · 13 April 1984

India occupied the Saltoro Ridge above the Siachen Glacier ahead of Pakistan. It created the world's highest battlefield and a permanent demand for high-altitude clothing, helicopters (Cheetah/Chetak, later Dhruv) and logistics — much of which had to be imported at first and was gradually indigenised.

Exercise Brasstacks · 1986-87

The largest Indian military exercise to that point, under General K. Sundarji, testing mechanised forces and new doctrine near the Pakistan border. It triggered a crisis with Pakistan and showed how exercises can be misread as mobilisation — an early lesson in signalling.

Operation Pawan · 1987-90

The Indian Peace Keeping Force in Sri Lanka fought the LTTE in counter-insurgency conditions it had not trained for, with poor intelligence and inadequate maps. Over 1,100 Indian soldiers were killed. The lessons fed into counter-insurgency doctrine used later in Jammu and Kashmir.

Operation Cactus · November 1988

Paratroopers flown to the Maldives within hours to reverse a coup attempt; the Navy intercepted the escaping mercenaries at sea. It demonstrated airborne rapid reaction and set a template for India as a regional first responder.

Kargil 1999 and the audit that followed

Kargil was a victory that exposed failures. It is the single most important event in India's modern defence reform, because for the first time the government commissioned a public, systematic audit of the entire security system.

What Kargil exposed

  • Intelligence and surveillance. Intrusions across the LoC in the Kargil-Drass-Batalik sector went undetected for weeks. India lacked persistent aerial and satellite surveillance of the area.
  • Equipment shortfalls. Shortages of high-altitude clothing, snow boots, night-vision devices, radars and artillery ammunition. Some items had to be bought urgently from abroad at premium prices.
  • Artillery. The Bofors FH-77B, bought in the 1980s and politically toxic ever since, proved decisive in direct and indirect fire on the peaks.
  • Air power. The IAF (Operation Safed Sagar) initially lost two fighters and a helicopter to shoulder-fired missiles, then adapted — Mirage 2000s with laser-guided bombs struck Pakistani positions and supply dumps, including at Tiger Hill and Muntho Dhalo.
  • Jointness. Army-Air Force coordination was slow at the start.
  • Small arms. The indigenous INSAS rifle received complaints in high-altitude conditions (Section 38).

The Kargil Review Committee

Established Chaired by the strategist K. Subrahmanyam, constituted on 29 July 1999, report submitted in December 1999 and tabled in Parliament in February 2000. It was the first time an Indian government had published an audit of a war. Its central finding was that the failure was systemic — of intelligence coordination, of higher defence organisation, and of the separation of the military from policy-making — rather than attributable to any individual.

Why Kargil matters more than any other war for this subject

The 1962 report was classified and remains so. The 1965 and 1971 wars produced no public audit. Kargil produced the KRC and then the Group of Ministers — and almost every structural reform of the next two decades, from the Strategic Forces Command to the CDS, can be traced to recommendations first made in 1999-2001.

The post-Kargil reforms

The Kargil Review Committee diagnosed the problem. A Group of Ministers turned the diagnosis into a programme. Most of it was implemented within four years; the most important recommendation — a Chief of Defence Staff — took twenty.

Why did the CDS take twenty years?

Interpretation Three reasons usually given: political caution about concentrating military authority in one officer; inter-service reservations, particularly from the smaller services about Army dominance; and bureaucratic reluctance in the Ministry of Defence to share control. Each has a legitimate core — civilian control is a constitutional principle, and the IAF's concern about dividing scarce assets is real — which is why the answer is not simply “bureaucratic inertia.”

The CDS and the Department of Military Affairs

Announced by the Prime Minister on 15 August 2019 and created on 1 January 2020, the Chief of Defence Staff is the most significant change to India's higher defence organisation since independence.

The CDS's roles

Hat 1 · Permanent Chairman, COSC

Chairs the Chiefs of Staff Committee; principal military adviser to the Raksha Mantri on tri-service matters. The service chiefs continue to advise on their own services. The CDS does not exercise operational command over the services.

Hat 2 · Secretary, Department of Military Affairs

The DMA is a new department within the Ministry of Defence, staffed by both military and civilian officers — the first time uniformed officers have been integrated into the ministry in a secretary-level role.

The mandate given to the CDS and DMA

  • Bring about jointness in operations, logistics, training, communications and maintenance within three years of the first CDS's appointment.
  • Facilitate restructuring of commands, including the establishment of joint or theatre commands.
  • Promote the use of indigenous equipment by the services.
  • Prioritise tri-service capital acquisitions and rationalise infrastructure.
  • Serve as military adviser to the Nuclear Command Authority.
  • Administer tri-service agencies and commands, including those for space, cyber and special operations.

Three new tri-service agencies, 2018-19

  • Defence Space Agency — military use of space (Section 25).
  • Defence Cyber Agency — military cyber operations (Section 26).
  • Armed Forces Special Operations Division — coordinates the Army's Para (SF), the Navy's MARCOS and the IAF's Garud Commando Force.

These were created as agencies rather than full commands, with smaller mandates than originally proposed — an example of the incremental approach to reform.

Theatre commands and jointness

India has seventeen single-service commands — seven Army, seven Air Force and three Navy — plus two tri-service commands. The theatre-command reform proposes to reorganise most of these into a small number of joint commands, each responsible for a geographic theatre or function.

The proposal, in outline

Re-verify The model most widely reported has been three theatre commands: a northern theatre facing China, a western theatre facing Pakistan, and a maritime theatre for the Indian Ocean. Earlier versions included separate air defence and logistics commands. The final design, timing and headquarters locations have been subject to repeated revision; check the latest official position.

The enabling law

Established The Inter-Services Organisations (Command, Control and Discipline) Act, 2023 gives the commander of an inter-services organisation disciplinary and administrative authority over personnel of all three services under his command — previously each service's personnel were governed only by their own Act (Army Act 1950, Navy Act 1957, Air Force Act 1950). This removes a legal obstacle to theatre commands.

The case for theatres

  • One commander per theatre, with all assets under him, can plan and fight a joint campaign — the model of the US, China (since 2016) and most major militaries.
  • Removes duplication of headquarters, logistics and infrastructure.
  • Forces joint planning in peacetime, so that jointness in war is not improvised as it was in 1971 and early in Kargil.
  • China's Western Theatre Command covers the entire Indian border; India faces it with several separate Army and Air Force commands.

The case for caution

  • The IAF has fewer squadrons than it needs. Dividing them among theatres risks losing the ability to concentrate air power where the decisive battle is.
  • India's two main threats may be simultaneous (collusion). Assets allocated to one theatre may be needed in the other.
  • Theatre commands work best with integrated communications, logistics and doctrine — which should come first.
  • A badly designed theatre structure, once created, is hard to undo.
The honest middle position

Almost no one argues against jointness. The argument is about sequence and design: whether to create theatres first and let jointness follow, or to build joint logistics, communications, training and doctrine first and let theatres formalise what already exists. Steps already taken on the second path include joint logistics nodes (from 2021), tri-service postings and cross-service training. A strong answer acknowledges both and names the IAF concern specifically.

Manpower, Agnipath and the pension squeeze

India has one of the world's largest armed forces — about 1.4 million active personnel. People are its greatest strength and its largest cost. The manpower question is therefore also a modernisation question.

The arithmetic

Salaries and pensions together have for years consumed roughly half or more of the total defence budget. Pensions alone are close to a quarter. One Rank One Pension (OROP), implemented from 2015 and revised in 2022, addressed a long-standing grievance of veterans but increased the pension bill. Every rupee spent on personnel is a rupee not spent on equipment — which is the core of the modernisation squeeze.

Agnipath

Established Announced on 14 June 2022, the Agnipath scheme recruits soldiers, sailors and airmen below officer rank (“Agniveers”) for four years, after which up to 25% are selected for regular service. Those who leave receive a lump-sum “Seva Nidhi” package (partly funded by their own contributions) but no pension. Several central and state governments announced reservations in police and paramilitary recruitment for former Agniveers.

The argument for

  • Reduces the long-term pension liability and frees money for equipment.
  • Lowers the average age of the force.
  • Creates a larger pool of trained, disciplined young people in society.
  • Replaces a regimental recruitment system in some arms with all-India, all-class recruitment.

The argument against

  • Four years, including training, may be too short to produce a fully proficient soldier in technical or high-altitude roles.
  • Unit cohesion, built over long service, may weaken.
  • Seventy-five per cent released each cycle may struggle to find employment.
  • Savings on pensions materialise only over decades.

Re-verify The scheme has been modified since its launch (including age relaxations in the first year) and reviews have been discussed. Check for any recent changes to the retention percentage or terms.

Five things to know about the budget

  1. As a share of GDP it has fallen — from around 2.5-3% in the late 1980s to below 2% today — even as absolute amounts have risen with a fast-growing economy. Many analysts argue for 2.5-3% given the two-front threat; others argue that efficiency matters more than share.

The line to remember

India spends a large absolute amount on defence, but a large share of it pays for people rather than equipment. The central modernisation dilemma is not “how much?” but “how is it divided?” — and every reform from Shekatkar to Agnipath to theatre commands is, in part, an attempt to shift that division towards capability.

Services and capabilities

Army modernisation

The Army is India's largest service — roughly 1.2 million personnel — and faces the hardest modernisation problem: it must hold two long, active land borders at once, from the deserts of Rajasthan to the high Himalaya, while re-equipping a force whose core inventory is Soviet-era.

Artillery: the long FARP story

The story of Indian artillery is the single clearest case study of how a procurement scandal can freeze capability for a generation — and of how indigenous industry eventually filled the gap.

  • 1986Contract for about 410 Bofors FH-77B 155 mm howitzers, with licence production planned.
  • 1987Allegations of kickbacks. The licence-production and further purchases never happen; Bofors becomes politically toxic.
  • 1999The Bofors gun proves decisive at Kargil. The Field Artillery Rationalisation Plan (FARP) is approved, aiming to standardise on 155 mm and acquire roughly 2,800-3,000 guns of different types.
  • 2000sRepeated tenders fail or are cancelled amid blacklisting of vendors, single-vendor situations and trial disputes. No new towed artillery gun is inducted for nearly three decades after Bofors.
  • 2016145 M777 ultra-light howitzers ordered from the US via FMS (BAE Systems, assembly with Mahindra) — liftable by Chinook for the mountains.
  • 2017100 K9 Vajra-T self-propelled guns contracted to L&T with Hanwha of South Korea; a follow-on order for 100 more was signed in December 2024.
  • 2019Dhanush — a 155 mm/45 calibre gun developed by the Ordnance Factory Board from the Bofors design documentation — begins induction.
  • March 2025Re-verify Contract for 307 ATAGS (Advanced Towed Artillery Gun System), designed by DRDO and produced by Bharat Forge and Tata Advanced Systems — the first large order for an Indian-designed private-sector-built artillery gun.

Rockets: Pinaka

Established The DRDO-developed Pinaka multi-barrel rocket launcher, first used in a limited way during Kargil, is now in multiple regiments with extended-range and guided variants in development or production. It has been exported to Armenia. Pinaka is a good example of a programme that succeeded because the user was involved early and the requirement was stable.

The lesson of Bofors

The gun was good. The scandal was about the contract, not the weapon. But the political consequence — a fear of signing any big contract with a foreign vendor — cost the Army thirty years of artillery modernisation. Interpretation This is the strongest argument for a transparent, predictable procurement system: not only to prevent corruption, but because the fear of corruption paralyses decisions.

Navy: the builder's navy

The Navy is the Indian service that indigenised earliest and most successfully. It began designing its own warships in the 1960s, created an in-house design bureau, and now builds nearly all its surface combatants in Indian shipyards.

Why the Navy indigenised first

  • A Directorate of Naval Design was created in the 1960s, giving the Navy its own warship-design capability rather than relying on DRDO or foreign designers.
  • The Leander-class frigates built at Mazagon Dock from the late 1960s were the first major licence-built warships; the lessons fed into the indigenous Godavari class.
  • Ships are large, built slowly, and allow foreign weapons and sensors to be integrated into Indian hulls — a model sometimes summarised as “float, move, fight”: the “float” (hull) indigenised first, the “move” (propulsion) partly, the “fight” (weapons and sensors) last.

Strategic forces and the triad

India's nuclear forces are almost entirely indigenous — the warheads, the missiles and the submarine reactor were built at home, largely because no one would sell them. They are the clearest example of strategic necessity overriding cost.

Doctrine

Established The Cabinet Committee on Security statement of 4 January 2003 sets out: credible minimum deterrence; No First Use, with nuclear weapons used only in retaliation against a nuclear attack on Indian territory or Indian forces anywhere; retaliation that will be massive and designed to inflict unacceptable damage; the option of nuclear retaliation against a major biological or chemical attack; and civilian political control through the Nuclear Command Authority.

Nuclear Command Authority

Political Council, chaired by the Prime Minister — the only body that can authorise nuclear use. Executive Council, chaired by the National Security Adviser — provides inputs and executes directives. The Strategic Forces Command (2003) manages the forces.

Why the triad matters for NFU

A No First Use policy is only credible if the arsenal can survive a first strike and retaliate. Land-based missiles can be targeted; aircraft can be destroyed on the ground. A submarine at sea is the most survivable leg — which is why the SSBN completes the doctrine.

The cost-exchange problem

A cheap drone costs a few thousand dollars; an interceptor missile can cost hundreds of thousands or more. Shooting down swarms with missiles is financially unsustainable. That is why guns (including old L-70s and ZU-23s given new sensors), electronic jamming and directed-energy weapons such as lasers are returning to the centre of air defence — and why India's layered model, with cheap systems at the bottom, is sound in principle.

UAVs, loitering munitions, counter-drone

For decades India bought its drones from Israel. Since 2020 the combination of Galwan, Ukraine and cross-border drone drops has pushed drones — and defences against them — to the top of the procurement agenda.

From import to industry

  • Israeli Heron and Searcher — the backbone of Indian surveillance drones since the late 1990s. The Harop loitering munition was also bought from Israel.
  • DRDO Rustom / TAPAS — a medium-altitude long-endurance drone that had a long, difficult development and has not yet entered large-scale service. Re-verify
  • MQ-9B — 31 armed high-altitude long-endurance drones ordered from the US via FMS in October 2024, split among the three services.
  • Ghatak — a stealth unmanned combat aircraft under development, with a scaled demonstrator flown in 2022. HAL's CATS Warrior is a “loyal wingman” concept to fly alongside manned fighters.
  • Loitering munitions — Indian firms now produce them, including Nagastra-1 by Solar Industries and the SkyStriker made in Bengaluru by an Indian-Israeli joint venture.
  • Policy — a Production-Linked Incentive for drones (2021), a ban on import of foreign drones for most civilian uses (2022), and liberalised drone rules helped create a domestic drone industry.

The counter-drone problem

Established On 27 June 2021, two drones dropped explosives on the IAF station at Jammu — the first drone attack on an Indian military base. Drones from across the border have repeatedly been used to drop weapons, ammunition and narcotics in Punjab and Jammu. Counter-drone systems now include DRDO's integrated drone detection and interdiction system, radio-frequency jammers, and laser directed-energy weapons under test.

The mass-versus-quality question

Interpretation Ukraine showed that thousands of cheap, expendable drones can matter more than a few expensive ones. India's drone procurement has historically favoured a small number of high-end systems. The debate now is whether to shift towards large numbers of cheaper, domestically produced drones — which Indian startups can make — while still buying a few high-end platforms like the MQ-9B for long-range surveillance.

Space and military satellites

India's space programme was built for civilian development, but its satellites now provide much of the military's communications, navigation and surveillance. Space is increasingly a warfighting domain.

Cyber, electronic warfare and AI

The least visible capability areas and in some ways the most important, because they affect every other system.

Cyber

The Defence Cyber Agency (2019) handles military cyber operations. Civilian bodies include CERT-In and the National Critical Information Infrastructure Protection Centre. Attacks on power grids and ports, attributed in open sources to state-linked actors, have shown the vulnerability of critical infrastructure. A comprehensive national cyber-security strategy has long been discussed. Re-verify

Electronic warfare

Jamming, spoofing and signal interception. Ukraine showed EW can neutralise drones and GPS-guided weapons at scale. India has indigenous EW systems from DRDO and BEL for land, sea and air, but the pace of adaptation needed is now measured in weeks, not years.

Artificial intelligence

A Task Force on AI for national security (2018) led to a Defence AI Council and Defence AI Project Agency (2019). Uses include image analysis, predictive maintenance, logistics and decision support. The debate on autonomous weapons — how much human control to keep — is live internationally and in India.

ISR and network-centric warfare

Intelligence, surveillance and reconnaissance (ISR) find the target. Networks pass the information to whoever can strike it. Without both, a modern weapon is a very expensive way to miss.

  • Airborne early warning: three Israeli-Russian Phalcon AWACS (from 2009) and indigenous Netra AEW&C on Embraer jets (from 2017), with larger follow-on versions approved. India has far fewer such aircraft than China.
  • Maritime ISR: P-8I, MQ-9B, coastal radar chains, and the Information Fusion Centre – Indian Ocean Region (2018) which shares maritime data with partners.
  • Networks: the IAF's AFNET (2010), the Navy's network-centric operations system, and Army battlefield management systems at various stages of development. Linking them across services is a jointness problem as much as a technical one.
  • The kill chain: the time from detecting a target to striking it. Shortening it is the purpose of most modern military networking. Sindoor was reported to have used a combination of space, air and ground sensors to identify and strike targets. Re-verify
Back to the habit

ISR and networks are where “platform, capability, system” matters most. India can buy or build good sensors and good shooters. What has historically lagged is the system that connects them across services — which is why jointness is not an abstract organisational question but a technical one about whose radar can talk to whose missile.

Logistics, ammunition, border infrastructure

Amateurs talk tactics; professionals talk logistics. The unglamorous questions — how much ammunition, how many spares, how good the roads — decide whether a force can fight for days or weeks.

Ammunition

Established A Comptroller and Auditor General report (2017) found serious ammunition shortages, with many types held in quantities far below the level required for a war of the prescribed duration. The government responded with emergency procurement powers, a revised stocking target, and a push for domestic ammunition production — including by private firms. Ukraine has since shown that artillery ammunition is consumed at rates far higher than peacetime planners assumed.

Emergency procurement

After Uri (2016) and especially after Galwan (2020), the services were given powers to buy urgently needed equipment quickly, within set financial limits and delivery timelines. Interpretation Emergency procurement filled real gaps fast, but repeated reliance on it can be read as evidence that the normal procurement system is too slow.

Border infrastructure

  • Border Roads Organisation (BRO) budgets rose significantly after 2020.
  • Darbuk-Shyok-Daulat Beg Oldi road (completed 2019) — access to the northernmost post in Ladakh.
  • Atal Tunnel under Rohtang Pass (opened October 2020) and Sela Tunnel in Arunachal Pradesh (opened March 2024) — all-weather access.
  • Advanced landing grounds in Arunachal Pradesh and Ladakh upgraded; new high-altitude airfields.
  • Vibrant Villages Programme (2023) — developing border villages to prevent depopulation along the northern border.
The 1962 echo

In 1962 there were almost no roads. Today the gap with China's infrastructure on the Tibetan side is narrowing but not closed. Interpretation The old Indian doctrine of leaving the border undeveloped so that an invader would struggle has been abandoned in favour of building roads to the LAC — on the view that the defender needs mobility more than the attacker is slowed by its absence.

How India buys weapons

From DPP to DAP

Before 2002 India had no single published procurement rulebook. Since then it has had eight versions, each trying to balance three goals that pull against each other: speed, probity and indigenisation.

  • DPP 2002First structured procedure, a product of the GoM reforms.
  • DPP 2005/06Offsets introduced — foreign vendors must invest a share of large contracts in India.

The acquisition categories, in order of preference

  1. Buy (Indian-IDDM) — Indian design, Indian development, Indian manufacture.
  2. Buy (Indian) — from an Indian vendor with a minimum indigenous content.
  3. Buy and Make (Indian) — an Indian vendor with a foreign technology partner, manufacturing in India.
  4. Buy and Make — initial purchase from a foreign vendor, then licensed production in India.
  5. Buy (Global – Manufacture in India) — foreign vendor, but with manufacture of the whole or part in India.
  6. Buy (Global) — outright import, justified only if no Indian option exists.

How a typical capital acquisition works

Service identifies need → AoN from the DAC → Request for Proposal → technical evaluation → field trials → staff evaluation → commercial negotiation (usually L1) → CCS approval → contract. Interpretation Each step exists for a reason — mostly to prevent corruption and ensure fitness for purpose — but together they can take many years. Frequent changes to requirements, single-vendor situations and re-tendering are the usual causes of delay.

The procurement trilemma

Speed, probity and indigenisation cannot all be maximised at once. A fast procurement risks corruption allegations; a scrupulously transparent one is slow; an indigenous one takes time to develop. Every DPP revision has shifted the balance. A strong answer identifies which of the three a particular reform prioritised and what it cost the other two.

The US factor

India-US defence trade grew from almost nothing before 2005 to many billions of dollars. The Russia factor

Russia remains the source of much of India's current inventory — Su-30MKI, T-90, T-72, S-400, Kilo submarines, Talwar frigates — and a partner in BrahMos and the AK-203. The US CAATSA sanctions law created a risk of sanctions for buying the S-400; no sanctions were imposed on India. The Ukraine war since 2022 has raised questions about Russia's ability to deliver spares and systems on time.

Diversification as policy. Interpretation India's supplier base has diversified significantly — France, the US and Israel have risen as Russia's share has fallen. Diversification reduces the risk of any one supplier cutting off supply, but it increases the logistics burden of operating many different types, and it is not the same as self-reliance.

Offsets and technology transfer

Two ideas that were supposed to turn imports into indigenous capability — and mostly did not, or not as much as hoped.

Offsets

From 2005, foreign vendors winning large contracts were required to invest a share (typically 30%, and 50% for some deals) of the contract value in India — by buying Indian goods, investing in Indian firms, or transferring technology. Established A CAG report (2019) was critical of how offset obligations had been discharged, finding that many had not been fulfilled or had produced limited technology transfer. DAP 2020 dropped offsets for inter-governmental, government-to-government and single-vendor deals.

Licence production versus design capability

What licence production gives

  • Manufacturing skills and a workforce
  • Supply chains for components
  • Ability to maintain and overhaul
  • Employment

What it does not give

  • The ability to design the next generation
  • The right to modify or upgrade freely
  • The source code for software
  • Control over critical components

Interpretation HAL has licence-built MiG-21s, MiG-27s, Jaguars, Hawks and Su-30MKIs over sixty years. Critics argue this built an assembler, not a designer. Defenders point out that the manufacturing base made Tejas and Dhruv possible. Both are partly right: licence production is a foundation for design capability, but only if it is combined with deliberate investment in design.

Atmanirbhar Defence

The dependence problem

For most of the last three decades India has been one of the world's two largest arms importers. For a country that sees itself as a major power, that dependence is a strategic vulnerability.

Import rank

Re-verify SIPRI data for 2020-24 places India as the world's second-largest arms importer, with roughly 8% of global imports — behind Ukraine.

Supplier mix

Re-verify Russia's share fell to roughly one-third or a little more of India's imports in 2020-24, from about three-quarters a decade earlier. France and Israel rose, with the US also significant.

Domestic production

Re-verify Government figures put domestic defence production at about ₹1.27 lakh crore in FY24 and around ₹1.5 lakh crore in FY25 — record highs.

Why dependence is dangerous

  • Supply cut-off in war. A supplier can withhold spares or ammunition at the moment of crisis. The 1965 US arms embargo is the historical example.
  • Political leverage. A supplier can use arms supplies to influence foreign policy.
  • Sanctions exposure. Buying from Russia risks US sanctions (CAATSA); Russia under Western sanctions has struggled to deliver on time.
  • No control over upgrades. A country that does not have the source code cannot modify its own weapons.
  • Economic cost. Money spent abroad does not build Indian industry, jobs or technology.

Success cases: BrahMos, Akash, Pinaka, Vikrant

The successes are just as instructive — and they share common features.

A joint venture that combined Russian propulsion with Indian systems and production. Now in service with all three services, exported to the Philippines, and increasingly Indian in content.

A surface-to-air missile from the IGMDP. After a long development it was inducted by the IAF and Army in the 2010s and cleared for export in December 2020.

A multi-barrel rocket launcher developed from the late 1980s, used in Kargil, now produced with private-sector participation and exported to Armenia.

When to make, when to buy

Atmanirbharta does not mean making everything at home. No country, not even the United States, does that. The real question is what to make, what to co-develop, and what to buy — and on what criteria.

  1. Strategic, no one will sell — nuclear weapons, SSBN reactors, long-range missiles. Must make, whatever the cost.
  2. Critical in war, high volume — ammunition, spares, drones, small arms, radios. Should make: supply must not be cut off in war, and scale makes it affordable.
  3. Complex platforms with long lead times — fighters, submarines, engines. Co-develop with partners, ensuring technology and design rights are transferred.
  4. Urgent gaps — capabilities needed now that India cannot build in time. Buy, but with local maintenance and a plan to replace with an Indian option later.
  5. Niche and low-volume items — where Indian production would never be economic. Buy from diversified, reliable suppliers.
The balanced answer

“Atmanirbharta is not autarky. It means India controls the capabilities that matter in war — the ones that could be denied at a moment of crisis — while buying what it cannot yet make, on terms that build Indian capability over time. The measure of success is not the import percentage but whether India can fight a war for as long as it needs to without anyone else's permission.”

Competing schools of thought

Informed people disagree about Indian defence policy. Knowing the main positions lets you present an argument fairly and then take your own view.

1. The indigenisers

Self-reliance is a strategic necessity. Accept higher costs and delays in the short run to build lasting capability. Point to the missile programme and nuclear submarines.

2. The pragmatists

The threat is now. Buy the best available and build domestic capability in parallel — do not leave soldiers under-equipped for an industrial policy. Point to the fighter squadron gap.

3. The reformers

The problem is not money or technology but structure — jointness, procurement process, and the relationship between services and bureaucracy. Point to the Kargil Review and Shekatkar reports.

4. The fiscal realists

The budget cannot grow fast enough. Cut manpower and pension costs, prioritise, and invest in cheaper asymmetric capabilities. Point to the salary and pension share.

5. The technology first

Future wars will be decided by drones, AI, cyber, space and missiles, not by tanks and carriers. Shift investment accordingly. Point to Ukraine and Sindoor.

6. The partnership school

No country builds everything. Deep co-development with trusted partners (the US, France, Israel and others) is the fastest route to real capability. Point to BrahMos and the F414 deal.

Scenarios

Scenarios test whether you understand the drivers. For each one, ask: what changes, what is the risk, and what should India do?

A. A two-front crisis

A crisis on the LAC coincides with tension on the LOC. Stress point: fighter numbers, ammunition, and the ability to move forces between fronts. What matters: stocks, infrastructure and jointness.

B. Russian supply collapses

Russia can no longer supply spares for Su-30MKI, T-90 and S-400. Stress point: much of India's inventory. What matters: reverse-engineering spares, indigenous replacement and diversification.

C. Real technology transfer from the US

The F414 co-production and other deals deliver real design-level transfer. Opportunity: a leap in engine and aerospace capability. Risk: new dependence and US conditions.

D. The budget squeeze

Economic slowdown holds defence spending flat. Stress point: capital budget. What matters: prioritisation, manpower reform, and choosing cheaper asymmetric capabilities.

India's strategic options

The main paths presented neutrally. Most serious proposals combine several.

1. Accelerate indigenisation
Stricter import lists, bigger domestic earmarks, more R&D money. Gain: long-term autonomy. Cost: short-term capability gaps.
2. Co-development partnerships
Deep partnerships with the US, France and others for engines, fighters and submarines. Gain: faster access to technology. Cost: dependence on partners' political decisions.
3. Structural reform first
Theatre commands, procurement reform, an independent evaluation agency. Gain: more capability from the same money. Cost: institutional resistance.
4. Raise defence spending
Move towards 2.5-3% of GDP. Gain: faster modernisation. Cost: trade-offs with development spending.
5. Asymmetric investment
Prioritise drones, missiles, EW, cyber and space over expensive platforms. Gain: cost-effective deterrence. Cost: risk of neglecting capabilities still needed.
6. Private-sector-led growth
Level playing field, larger private orders, export promotion. Gain: competition, efficiency, scale. Cost: dependence on commercial incentives.

Shorter training; weaker regimental bonds; uncertainty for the 75% not retained; risk to recruitment quality. Disputed
India should
Keep the reform but review it on evidence — retention percentage, training length, and post-service placement — with changes made openly. Cost: admitting the first design may need adjustment.

Brief 2 · Was buying the S-400 the right decision?

What
A Russian long-range surface-to-air missile system, contracted in 2018 for about US$5.4 billion.
Why
India had no long-range air-defence system capable of covering large areas against aircraft and missiles from two adversaries.
Background
Signed despite the US CAATSA sanctions law; deliveries began in 2021, with the last units delayed by the Ukraine war.
India's interest
Air defence against China and Pakistan; and strategic autonomy in choosing suppliers.
Security
Significant capability; reported to have been used in Operation Sindoor. Re-verify
Economic
High cost; spending abroad rather than in Indian industry; lifetime support depends on Russia.
For
No equivalent was available from any other source at the time; it filled an urgent gap; the US did not impose sanctions.
Against
Deepened dependence on Russia; delivery delays; sanctions risk; limited technology transfer.
India should
Use the S-400 fully while developing an indigenous long-range air-defence system to replace it over time. Cost: years of parallel spending.

Brief 3 · Should India create theatre commands now?

What
Integrated commands under a single commander, controlling Army, Navy and Air Force assets for a geographic region or function.
Why
India has 17 single-service commands; modern war needs the services to fight as one.
Background
Recommended in various forms since Kargil; the CDS was created in 2020 with a mandate to bring about jointness. Re-verify current status.
India's interest
Maximum capability from limited resources, especially scarce air assets.
Security
Better integration for a two-front threat; but a badly designed structure could be worse than the present one.
Economic
Savings from shared logistics, training and procurement.
For
Every major military has moved to joint structures; India's own wars showed the cost of poor coordination.
Against
The IAF's concern about dividing limited air assets; disruption during a live border tension; the need for joint culture before joint structure.
India should
Move in stages — joint logistics and training first, then functional commands, then geographic theatres — with political direction to settle disagreements. Cost: accepting slower progress to avoid a flawed structure.

Brief 4 · Should the private sector replace DPSUs as the core of Indian defence industry?

What
The question of whether private companies should lead defence manufacturing rather than state-owned DPSUs.
Why
DPSU delays and quality concerns; the rise of capable private firms.
Background
Defence production opened to the private sector in 2001; OFB corporatised in 2021; private firms now build aircraft (C-295), guns and missiles components.
India's interest
Faster, better, cheaper production and a competitive industrial base.
Security
More suppliers mean more resilience; but strategic systems may still need state control.
Economic
Competition lowers costs; private firms drive exports.
For
Efficiency, innovation, capital, and export orientation.
Against
DPSUs hold decades of expertise and infrastructure; private firms follow profit and may exit unprofitable lines.
India should
Aim for competition, not replacement — a level playing field where DPSUs and private firms compete and collaborate. Cost: DPSUs losing guaranteed orders.

Glossary

Atmanirbhar Bharat
“Self-reliant India” — the policy framework announced in May 2020, applied to defence as a push for domestic design and production.
CDS
Chief of Defence Staff; the senior-most uniformed officer and head of the Department of Military Affairs, created 1 January 2020.
DMA
Department of Military Affairs within the Ministry of Defence, headed by the CDS.
DAP
Defence Acquisition Procedure (2020), successor to the Defence Procurement Procedure (DPP).
DPSU
Defence Public Sector Undertaking — a government-owned defence company such as HAL, BEL or MDL.
DRDO
Defence Research and Development Organisation, founded 1958.
iDEX
Innovations for Defence Excellence (2018); funds startups and small firms to solve defence problems.
IGMDP
Integrated Guided Missile Development Programme (1983), which produced Prithvi, Agni, Akash, Trishul and Nag.
Jointness
The ability of the Army, Navy and Air Force to plan and fight as a single force.
Theatre command
An integrated command with forces from all three services under one commander for a region or function.
Squadron
The basic IAF combat unit, typically 16-18 aircraft. The sanctioned strength is 42 squadrons.
SSBN / SSN / SSK
Nuclear-powered ballistic missile submarine / nuclear-powered attack submarine / conventional diesel-electric submarine.
AIP
Air-Independent Propulsion; lets a conventional submarine stay submerged longer.
MIRV
Multiple Independently targetable Re-entry Vehicle — several warheads on one missile, each aimed at a different target.
NFU
No First Use — India's commitment not to use nuclear weapons first.
AEW&C / AWACS
Airborne early warning and control aircraft carrying long-range radar.
ISR
Intelligence, surveillance and reconnaissance.
EW
Electronic warfare — jamming, spoofing and intercepting enemy signals.
Loitering munition
A drone that circles over an area and then dives onto a target, destroying itself.
Cost-exchange ratio
The cost of a defensive weapon compared with the cost of the threat it defeats.
CAATSA
Countering America's Adversaries Through Sanctions Act (2017) — a US law allowing sanctions on countries buying major Russian weapons.
LEMOA / COMCASA / BECA
India-US foundational agreements on logistics (2016), secure communications (2018) and geospatial cooperation (2020).
War Wastage Reserve
Stocks of ammunition and stores held for a war of a specified duration and intensity.
Emergency procurement
Fast-track purchasing powers given to the services for urgent needs, used after Uri and Galwan.
OFB
Ordnance Factory Board — dissolved in 2021; its factories reorganised into seven DPSUs.
SP model
Strategic Partnership model (2017) — Indian private firms partnering with foreign manufacturers for major platforms.

The arc in eight sentences

  1. India inherited a small, colonial-era force and a thin industrial base in 1947, and chose to depend on imports while building public-sector research and production.
  2. The defeat of 1962 forced rapid expansion and a turn to Soviet weapons, which became the backbone of the forces for four decades.
  3. The strategic programmes — missiles and nuclear weapons — succeeded because they were given priority, secrecy and continuity that conventional programmes lacked.
  4. The Bofors scandal of 1987 froze key procurement and created a culture of caution that lasted a generation.
  5. Kargil in 1999 exposed gaps in surveillance, equipment and jointness; the reforms that followed took twenty years to complete, with the CDS created only in 2020.
  6. From 2014, and especially from 2020, the government used procurement categories, import lists, budget earmarks and private-sector opening to push domestic production.
  7. Production and exports reached record levels, but critical dependencies — engines, chips, rare earths — remain, and indigenous content is not the same as indigenous design.
  8. Ukraine and Operation Sindoor have shifted attention towards drones, air defence, ammunition and industrial depth — the capabilities that decide whether a country can sustain a war on its own.

Five facts that win arguments

  • The Bofors gun performed well in Kargil — the scandal was about the contract, not the weapon.
  • India's nuclear submarine, missiles and warheads are indigenous — proof that Indian defence R&D can deliver with sustained priority.
  • DAP 2020 dropped offsets for G2G and single-vendor deals after a CAG report found them poorly implemented.
  • The first drone attack on an Indian base was in June 2021 — the threat is not theoretical.
  • Every major arms producer had failed programmes — failure is part of learning to design, not proof that it cannot be done.

Four answers to have ready, in your own phrasing

  • What does modernisation mean? Not just new platforms — the capability to find, decide and strike, sustained over time, with the structures to use it jointly.
  • What does Atmanirbharta mean? Controlling the capabilities that could be denied in a crisis, while buying what India cannot yet make on terms that build Indian capability.
  • What is the biggest gap? Name one with a reason — fighter numbers, engines, or jointness — and say what would close it.
  • What should India do? Fix structures, fund the essentials, build what matters, buy what is urgent, and measure success by staying power, not import percentages.

Last word before you walk in

No one expects you to know every missile's range or every contract value. The board wants to see whether you understand that weapons are only as good as the system that buys, integrates and sustains them; whether you can praise an Indian success without hiding its weaknesses and criticise a failure without despair; and whether you can say “I am not certain, sir, I would want to check.” Know the ten big ideas properly. Then talk like yourself.
