What are naval exercises?

Naval exercises are structured rehearsals of maritime operations designed to test fleet readiness, communication discipline, command flow, weapons systems, and crisis response under realistic conditions. They are not simply “show-of-force” events; they are operational learning tools.

For navies, the value of an exercise lies in how it simulates pressure. Surface combatants, submarines, maritime patrol aircraft, helicopters, missile defence assets and supporting logistics platforms all have to function together. In a real contingency, one weak point in communications, maintenance, or command architecture can matter as much as the quality of a weapon system.

That is why exercises are so important in 2025-26. Maritime security is increasingly shaped by contested sea lanes, anti-access measures, missile threats, persistent surveillance, and regional diplomatic signalling. A navy that trains under these conditions develops habits of coordination that are hard to create in peacetime routine alone.

Why this period matters

2025-26 is a particularly meaningful moment for maritime exercises because several strategic trends are converging. The Indo-Pacific remains a theatre of great-power competition, even as the global economy is still vulnerable to shipping disruption, energy stress, and regional flashpoints. At the same time, states are paying more attention to undersea surveillance, missile defence, and the ability to sustain naval formations far from home ports.

For India, this matters because the country’s naval posture sits at the intersection of the Indian Ocean, Red Sea and South China Sea security debates. Whether the issue is freedom of navigation, escort missions, anti-piracy, search and rescue, or regional deterrence, maritime exercises help turn abstract strategy into task-specific operational doctrine. They expose what a navy can do in a tense environment and what it still cannot manage without deeper interoperability.

The same logic applies to other navies. In 2025-26, some exercises are designed to build coalition readiness; others are meant to test the viability of force projection, anti-submarine warfare, or logistics in degraded conditions. All of them are part of a larger strategic conversation about who can maintain security at sea and under what conditions.

Part I · The maritime architecture

Exercises as a governance and readiness tool

The exercise ecosystem

Naval exercises come in several formats. Bilateral drills focus on interoperability between two navies; multilateral exercises widen the coordination challenge by incorporating more states, more aircraft, and more command structures. Some are defensive and focused on maritime security, while others centre on missile defence, anti-submarine warfare, carrier operations, or amphibious landing scenarios.

What makes the ecosystem relevant is that each category reveals different capabilities. A search-and-rescue exercise tells you about logistics and cooperation; a live-fire missile exercise tells you about precision and command discipline; an anti-submarine drill tells you about datalinks, underwater awareness, and sensor integration. The cumulative effect is a clearer picture of force readiness than any single annual review could provide.

Exercises also create political signals. They show confidence, align partner states operationally, and allow military leaders to test doctrine without escalating to open conflict. However, they also carry a risk: if they are treated as purely symbolic, they can overstate readiness and hide genuine gaps in maintenance, training or integration.

India’s exercise calendar

India’s defence planning in 2025-26 is shaped by a dense mix of bilateral, multilateral and strategic sea exercises. The Indian Navy uses these events to rehearse operations in the Arabian Sea, Bay of Bengal, Indian Ocean and wider Indo-Pacific. They promote familiarity with partners while also testing India’s own ability to sustain high-tempo operations across geography.

Among the core issues in India’s exercise calendar are carrier operations, maritime surveillance, anti-submarine warfare, amphibious logistics, and coordinated action with air and coastguard assets. This is particularly significant because modern maritime security is no longer a single-service concern. Naval exercises increasingly involve air forces, coastal radar networks, cyber protection units and strategic communications architecture.

From a strategic perspective, these events matter because they help India calibrate deterrence without relying only on static force posture. A navy will often be judged less by the size of its fleet and more by whether it can integrate platforms, communicate effectively and sustain operations under stress. That is exactly what repeated naval exercises are designed to test.

Part II · Operations and lessons

What exercise design actually tests

Warfighting traits under test

Naval exercises are built around a small number of traits that matter in any maritime conflict: situational awareness, sensor dominance, speed of decision-making, credible force protection, and sustained operational tempo. These are what separate a capable fleet from one that can only operate in routine peacetime conditions.

Exercise scenarios often include missile threats, evasive enemy submarines, surveillance gaps, weather disruption, and fuel or maintenance constraints. They also test coordination between naval forces, aircraft and shore-based command centres. A fleet may appear capable in a static parade or an official review, but an exercise reveals whether it can adapt when the problem becomes dynamic and contested.

That is also why exercises are relevant to strategic analysis. When a navy reveals strong anti-submarine performance, high-quality communications architecture, or seamless carrier operations, it is not simply improving training—it is shaping deterrence. Every successful training event carries implications for political risk, escalation management and the credibility of a side in a disputed maritime environment.

Command, control and communications

In modern naval operations, the decisive variable is often not the weapon but the decision cycle. A fleet can have excellent missiles, good radar and capable crews, yet still underperform if its command architecture cannot process information fast enough. Naval exercises therefore test communications architecture as intensely as they test combat drills.

Questions that matter in this domain include: can sensors share data across platforms, can commands change plans rapidly without confusion, and can coordination survive jamming, degraded signals or atmospheric disruption? These are exactly the issues that multilateral exercises expose because participating ships and aircraft often use different communication chains and operating procedures.

For India, this is a central strategic concern. The ability to integrate platforms from multiple partners, while preserving command authority and operational clarity, is a core part of maritime realism. That is why command-and-control drills are often more valuable than headline publicity from a single large-scale exercise.

ASW and air defence

Two of the most important maritime domains in modern naval warfare are anti-submarine warfare and air defence. Underwater detection is a difficult challenge because it combines sonar, surveillance, intelligence, timing and tactical patience. A single undersea platform can dominate a theatre if it can remain concealed and exploit gaps in detection coverage.

Air defence is similarly complex. Missile threats can arrive from different directions, involve different trajectories and generate severe pressure on fleet command. The challenge is not only intercepting the missile but also sustaining a layered defensive architecture with aircraft, ships and land-based sensors. Exercises test whether this system can remain coherent even under deception, ambiguity and sensor fatigue.

In the 2025-26 context, states are especially interested in whether they can combine anti-submarine detection, air patrols and escort operations without losing tempo. The result is a stronger understanding of where the real defence gaps remain, and which upgrades are urgent rather than cosmetic.

Amphibious and HADR missions

Not all naval exercises are about direct combat. Many also test amphibious operations, disaster-response planning and humanitarian assistance and disaster relief (HADR). These missions are strategically significant because they reveal how navies can project force, support civil authorities and operate in crisis conditions without escalating conflict.

Amphibious drills are especially useful for testing logistics, landing craft viability, beachhead planning, close air support coordination, and troop transfer under risk. HADR exercises focus on medical support, disaster zones, communication with civilian agencies, and operational resilience when infrastructure is damaged. In regions vulnerable to cyclones, flooding or maritime disruption, these are not peripheral capabilities.

That matters for India because the navy is sometimes expected to act as both a security instrument and a humanitarian support force. The better the exercise routine, the more coherently the navy can shift from deterrence posture to relief operations or crisis response without losing efficiency or political credibility.

Worked example: evaluating exercise value

One way to assess a naval exercise is to use a simple defence-planning frame: define, locate, establish, contest, analyse, test, position, concede, and close. This keeps the analysis grounded in operational reality rather than media optics.

Define
What is the mission — escort, patrol, surveillance, blockade, amphibious landing or disaster response?
Locate
Where are the key threats, routes and support systems?
Establish
Are the required ships, aircraft and command nodes ready and integrated?
Contest
Can the force operate when sensor noise, jamming or hostile tracking are present?
Analyse
Are leaders using real-time information to adapt rapidly?
Test
Do crews practice realistic escalation and recovery steps?
Position
Does the exercise improve deterrence or crisis stability?
Concede
What vulnerabilities remain after the drill?
Close
What doctrine or procurement adjustments follow from the result?

Applied properly, this framework shows that a naval exercise is valuable not just because it is visible, but because it reveals what the force can actually sustain when conditions become complex and contested.

Part III · Regional drivers

Sea lanes, alliances and strategic balance

QUAD and key partner exercises

Exercises involving India, the United States, Japan and Australia matter because they combine maritime security, logistics, surveillance and doctrine. These drills are important not because they automatically imply a formal military alliance, but because they improve coordination in shared theatres such as the Indian Ocean and Western Pacific.

The strategic value of such exercises lies in operational familiarity. States can test how ships communicate, how aircraft coordinate from disparate command structures, and how search-and-rescue and escort missions work when time pressure rises. The shared learning is especially relevant in contested sea lanes, where geography, weather and political risk can all complicate a real operation.

For India, this is a valuable complement to bilateral exercises with regional partners. It allows the navy to engage with different force postures while also building confidence in how to plan and sustain a wider maritime security architecture.

Sea lanes, chokepoints and bloc politics

Maritime exercises are always shaped by the geography of trade and strategic routes. The Indian Ocean, Malacca Strait, South China Sea and Arabian Sea are not only conduits for trade; they are also pressure points for naval posturing, energy security and diplomatic signaling. This is where shipping volumes, security incidents and deterrence strategies intersect.

When states conduct exercises nearby to these routes, they are often testing not just combat skills but also the credibility of broader deterrence. The location matters because these sea lanes are tied to sanction regimes, energy access, commercial confidence and regional military balancing. In other words, maritime exercises are often about more than “naval drills”; they are part of a wider security negotiation.

Neutral analysts tend to emphasise a simple point: maritime exercises are valuable when they increase safety, coordination and crisis management, and less valuable when they are used mainly to signal dominance without operational depth. The distinction is critical in understanding why some exercises become strategic milestones while others remain largely symbolic.

Capability gains and trade-offs

One of the major gains from naval exercises is the way they reveal the difference between paper capability and real operational performance. They force navies to work through the details: maintenance schedules, fuel planning, troop readiness, weapons readiness, air support, sonar reliability and communications resilience.

These exercises also improve the quality of procurement and doctrine. If a force learns that a vessel needs better sensor fusion, or that command orders are too slow to transmit across fleets, then the exercise has identified a real institutional gap. That is the point of training: to turn weakness into a measurable action plan.

Yet there are trade-offs. Large exercises can be expensive, require high operational tempo, and may divert attention from routine readiness. If force planning is driven only by good optics, then a navy may appear agile while remaining strategically brittle. Serious force modernization therefore depends on balancing visible exercise participation with deeper maintenance, training and surveillance investment.

Risks, limits and blind spots

Naval exercises are useful but not complete. They cannot replicate every condition of actual warfare, including the political pressures of escalation, intelligence surprise, cyber interference, or a rapid deterioration in satellite communications. Exercise planning usually takes place in controlled settings, while real conflict is often messy, degraded and fast-moving.

There is also a risk of overconfidence. A well-publicised exercise can give the impression of a stronger navy than exists in operational reality if the drill is too scripted, too safe, or too narrow in its objectives. The distinction between an “impressive exercise” and a “meaningful readiness event” matters. The former may satisfy headline politics; the latter improves actual force capability.

That is why the most credible analysis asks not just whether a naval exercise happened, but what it tested, what it revealed, and what changed afterwards. That standard is especially important in a region where maritime signalling is never purely military.

Perspectives and controversies

Analysts read naval exercises through several lenses. The operational view sees them as essential tools for readiness, interoperability and crisis training. The geopolitical view sees them as a form of deterrence and diplomatic signalling, particularly in the Indo-Pacific. The sceptical view argues that some exercises are more about optics than actual capability, especially when they avoid realistic degraded conditions, cyber disruption, and long-range missile threats.

These debates are not just academic. A large maritime exercise can build trust between navies, but it can also provide a misleading sense of confidence if leaders confuse “visibility” with “readiness.” The most honest conclusion is that exercises matter when they generate hard learning. If the exercise does not change doctrine, training or procurement priorities, then it risks becoming a performative ritual rather than a strategic instrument.

Strategic takeaway In maritime security, the real issue is not how big the exercise looks on a news feed, but how much it improves actual decision-making, coordination and force sustainment under pressure.

Scenarios and strategic options

Scenario planning is a useful way to move from public signalling to strategic utility. One realistic scenario is a maritime confrontation involving contested sea lanes, constrained communications, and a need to escort ships while tracking hostile submarines and aircraft. Another is a humanitarian emergency in which a navy must quickly shift from deterrence to disaster relief and civil support under complex political conditions.

In response, navies have a few broad options. They can deepen interoperability and data-sharing with partners; improve anti-submarine, missile defence and logistics capacities; invest in command resilience; and expand air-sea coordination with land-based surveillance. They can also demand more realistic tactical conditions during exercises to make training harder and more representative.

These options are not mutually exclusive, but they raise a practical question: should a navy plan primarily for deterrence, or for resilience and disaster response, or for both? The strongest strategic posture is usually not a single answer but a force that can adapt across threat levels without losing internal coherence.

Glossary

ASW
Anti-submarine warfare: methods used to detect, track and counter hostile submarines.
HADR
Humanitarian assistance and disaster relief: naval missions that support rescue, logistics and relief during crises.
Interoperability
The ability of naval forces from different states or services to operate together effectively despite doctrinal and technological differences.
Sea lane
A maritime route used for trade, transit, energy shipment and strategic movement.
Sensor fusion
The merging of data from multiple platforms and systems to create a clearer operational picture.
Deterrence
The use of capability and readiness to dissuade adversaries from coercive or military action.
