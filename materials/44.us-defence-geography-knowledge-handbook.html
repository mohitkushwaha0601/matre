US Defence and Geography: Moat, Commands, Bases and Reach

The United States is a continental power with weak neighbours and two oceans on its flanks, yet it keeps the world's largest network of forward forces. This handbook explains how that geography shaped US strategy, how the defence establishment is organised, how each region is covered by commands and bases, how sea, air and nuclear forces use the map, and what it means for India, with SSB-style practice material.

Using this handbook

US defence policy makes the most sense when read off a map. Distance from rivals, access to oceans, and the location of allies and bases explain more of US posture than doctrine documents do. This handbook keeps one question running throughout: what does geography give the United States, and what does it cost?

Force numbers, budgets and base lists change every year. Figures below are approximate and tagged where they should be checked against current official sources (Department of Defense, Congressional Budget Office, SIPRI, IISS Military Balance).

Key habit

For any US deployment, ask three things: which command covers it, which base or ally makes it possible, and which chokepoint or distance constrains it. That trio gives a usable answer in an SSB discussion.

Part I · Geography and history

How the map made the strategy

The geographic position

The United States covers roughly 9.8 million sq km, with the Atlantic to the east, the Pacific to the west, Canada to the north and Mexico to the south. Its land borders are with two neighbours that are militarily far weaker or friendly, a situation sometimes called the moat advantage: no major power can easily attack the homeland by land, and sea approaches are long. Verify Border lengths are about 8,900 km with Canada (including Alaska) and about 3,100 km with Mexico.

Three older ideas explain how Americans read the map. Alfred Thayer Mahan (The Influence of Sea Power upon History, 1890) argued that sea control and bases decide national power. Halford Mackinder saw Eurasia's heartland as the pivot of world politics. Nicholas Spykman (1944) argued that the decisive ground was the Eurasian rimland, and that the US must prevent any single power from dominating it. Post-1945 US policy followed Spykman's logic closely.

What geography gives

  • Security from invasion; low need for large standing land forces at home.
  • Two-ocean access with a Panama Canal link and major ports on both coasts.
  • Resource depth: energy, agriculture, industry, large population.
  • Outlying territories (Hawaii, Guam, Alaska) as forward positions.

What geography costs

  • Allies and rivals lie across oceans, so every operation needs sealift, airlift and bases.
  • Long supply lines: the "tyranny of distance" in the Pacific.
  • Dependence on allies for access, which gives them leverage.
  • Two coasts and a global role mean forces are spread thin.

Expansion and doctrine

The country grew from the Atlantic seaboard to the Pacific within about a century. The Louisiana Purchase (1803) doubled its size, the Mexican-American War (1846-48) added the south-west, and Alaska was bought from Russia in 1867. This continental consolidation removed European powers from North America's interior and made the homeland defensible.

The Monroe Doctrine (1823) declared the Western Hemisphere closed to new European colonisation; the Roosevelt Corollary (1904) added a US right to intervene in the region. The Spanish-American War (1898) brought Puerto Rico, Guam and the Philippines, and Hawaii was annexed the same year. The Panama Canal (opened 1914) let the Navy shift between oceans without sailing around South America, and the US retained a lease on Guantanamo Bay, Cuba, from 1903.

Geographic logic

Each acquisition was about positions: Hawaii and Guam for Pacific coaling and basing, Panama for ocean transit, the Caribbean for the canal's approaches. Overseas territory started as a naval-basing strategy.

World wars, Cold War and alliances

In both world wars the US entered late, mobilised industrial power, and fought on other continents. After the attack on Pearl Harbor (7 December 1941) the lesson drawn was that isolation does not guarantee safety. In 1947 the National Security Act created the Air Force, the National Security Council and the CIA, and the Department of Defense took its present form in 1949.

Containment of the Soviet Union produced a ring of alliances along the Eurasian rimland: the Truman Doctrine (1947), NATO (1949), ANZUS (1951), treaties with Japan (1951, revised 1960) and South Korea (1953), and SEATO (1954, dissolved 1977). US forces stayed on in Germany, Japan and Korea after the wars, which is the origin of the permanent forward-basing network.

Part II · Structure

How the establishment is organised

Structure, budget and industry

Civilian control is constitutional: the President is Commander-in-Chief, Congress raises forces and funds them, and a civilian Secretary of Defense runs the Pentagon (in Arlington, Virginia). Operational orders flow from the President through the Secretary to the combatant commanders; the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff is the principal military adviser but is not in the chain of command. This was set by the Goldwater-Nichols Act (1986), which strengthened joint operations over single-service rivalry. Verify A 2025 executive order authorised "Department of War" as a secondary name; the statutory name remains Department of Defense unless Congress changes it.

The services are the Army, Navy, Marine Corps, Air Force, Space Force (established December 2019) and the Coast Guard, which sits under the Department of Homeland Security except in wartime. The National Guard serves both states and the federal government. Active-duty strength is about 1.3 million. Verify

Budget

US defence spending is far above any other state: about $900 billion a year in national defence funding, roughly 3% of GDP and over a third of world military spending in SIPRI's counts. Verify Spending concentrates on personnel, operations, procurement and research.

The industrial base matters as much as the budget. President Eisenhower warned of a military-industrial complex in his 1961 farewell address. Large prime contractors, DARPA-style research agencies, and the "offset strategies" (nuclear weapons in the 1950s, precision and stealth in the 1970s-80s, AI and autonomy today) aim to offset numerical disadvantages with technology.

The combatant commands

The world is divided into six geographic combatant commands, each led by a four-star officer with responsibility for all forces in the region, plus functional commands with global tasks. The Unified Command Plan is revised periodically, so boundaries shift.

India on the command map

India falls within INDOPACOM, while Pakistan falls within CENTCOM. INDOPACOM was renamed from Pacific Command in 2018, a change widely read as an acknowledgement of India's place in US strategy.

Part III · Geography in practice

Region by region

Homeland, Arctic and missile defence

NORAD, created in 1958, is a binational US-Canada command for air and maritime warning over North America. NORTHCOM was set up after 9/11 for homeland defence and support to civil authorities. Missile defence rests on ground-based interceptors at Fort Greely, Alaska and Vandenberg, California, plus sensors and radars across the northern approaches. Verify Interceptor numbers.

The Arctic is turning into a strategic region as ice retreats: the shortest missile and bomber routes between Russia and the US cross the polar region, and new sea lanes are opening. Key sites are Alaska's air bases and Pituffik Space Base in Greenland (formerly Thule), a missile-warning location. Washington's interest in Greenland and Canadian Arctic security has drawn public attention since 2025. Verify

Golden Dome

A 2025 executive order launched a planned multi-layer homeland missile shield, often called Golden Dome, with space-based sensors and interceptors. Its scale, cost and feasibility are debated, and the programme's status should be checked. Verify

Europe and the Atlantic

US power in Europe rests on NATO, now 32 members after Finland (2023) and Sweden (2024) joined. Article 5 treats an attack on one as an attack on all. Major US sites include Ramstein Air Base and EUCOM headquarters in Germany, Naval Station Rota in Spain (missile-defence destroyers), Naples (the Sixth Fleet), Aviano in Italy, and Aegis Ashore missile-defence sites in Romania and Poland. About 80,000 US troops are normally in Europe. Verify

Geography creates fixed concerns: the GIUK gap (Greenland-Iceland-UK) is the route for Russian submarines to the Atlantic; the Suwalki Gap is a narrow land corridor between Poland and Lithuania, squeezed between Belarus and Russia's Kaliningrad exclave; and the Baltic and Black Seas are semi-enclosed waters whose access Turkey and Denmark control through straits. Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine moved more NATO forces to the eastern flank.

Indo-Pacific and the island chains

China is treated as the pacing challenge, so the Pacific dominates current planning. The key concept is the island chains, lines of islands that either screen China or contain it, depending on perspective.

Diego Garcia in the Indian Ocean is a key bomber and logistics base; its sovereignty arrangement between the UK and Mauritius changed in 2025 with US access preserved. Verify

Tyranny of distance

Hawaii is over 8,000 km from Taiwan, Guam roughly 3,000 km. Dispersed bases are therefore both vital and exposed to missile attack, which is why the Marine Corps has built Marine Littoral Regiments and the services plan more distributed operations.

Middle East and Central Asia

CENTCOM covers a region of oil, chokepoints and conflicts. The Fifth Fleet is based in Bahrain; Al Udeid in Qatar hosts the main air operations centre; Kuwait, the UAE and Saudi Arabia provide further access. The Strait of Hormuz, only about 21 nautical miles wide at its narrowest shipping lanes, carries roughly a fifth of world oil trade. Verify

After the 1991 Gulf War the US kept a lasting regional presence. The 2001 Afghanistan and 2003 Iraq wars were costly; US forces left Afghanistan in August 2021 and ended the combat mission in Iraq in 2021. In June 2025 US aircraft struck Iranian nuclear sites, showing how long-range bombers can reach the region from the continental US. Verify

Geographic lesson

Afghanistan showed how a landlocked theatre depends on neighbours' territory for supply. The US relied on routes through Pakistan and Central Asia, giving those states leverage.

Africa and the Western Hemisphere

In Africa the US has a smaller footprint through AFRICOM (established 2007). Its main base is Camp Lemonnier in Djibouti, next to the Bab-el-Mandeb strait, supporting counter-terrorism and Red Sea operations. Several Sahel states have asked US troops to leave in recent years, for example Niger in 2024. Verify

In the Western Hemisphere SOUTHCOM focuses on drug trafficking and migration, with Guantanamo Bay as the oldest overseas base. The Panama Canal was handed to Panama in 1999 under a treaty that also gives the US a right to defend its neutrality; it remains a chokepoint of strategic concern, and Washington has recently stressed the hemisphere again, including expanded naval deployments in the Caribbean. Verify

Why it matters

The Monroe Doctrine tradition means US planners treat nearby regions as a homeland-adjacent priority, which can compete with Indo-Pacific resources.

Sea power and chokepoints

The US Navy operates 11 nuclear-powered aircraft carriers, the world's largest carrier fleet, backed by nuclear submarines, destroyers and amphibious ships. Roughly 290 ships form its battle force. Verify Seven numbered fleets are assigned by region, for example the Seventh in the western Pacific and the Fifth in the Gulf. The Marine Corps provides expeditionary and amphibious forces.

Sea power links geography to the world economy: the US Navy protects sea lines of communication through chokepoints such as Hormuz, Malacca, Bab-el-Mandeb, Suez, Panama and the Taiwan Strait, and conducts freedom of navigation operations against claims it regards as excessive. The US has not ratified UNCLOS, although it generally follows its navigation provisions, a point critics cite when it accuses others of ignoring the convention. Fact

Air, space and nuclear forces

Air power compensates for distance. A large fleet of tankers (KC-135, KC-46) and transports (C-17) provides global reach, and TRANSCOM coordinates sealift and airlift. Bombers such as the B-2 (Whiteman, Missouri) and B-52 can fly from the continental US to distant targets with aerial refuelling. The Space Force operates GPS, missile-warning and communications satellites that all services depend on.

The nuclear triad places each leg in a geographic location: Minuteman III ICBMs in silos at Malmstrom (Montana), F.E. Warren (Wyoming) and Minot (North Dakota); ballistic-missile submarines at Kings Bay (Georgia) and Bangor (Washington); and bomber bases such as Barksdale, Minot and Whiteman. ICBM fields were located in the interior so that Soviet missiles would have to cross the Arctic and strike a thinly populated heartland. Modernisation includes the Sentinel ICBM, the Columbia-class submarine and the B-21 bomber.

Arms control

New START limited deployed strategic warheads to 1,550 per side and was due to expire in February 2026. What replaced or followed it is worth checking. Verify

Part IV · Strategy and India

Posture, partners and the India link

Strategy, alliances and basing

The 2022 National Defense Strategy stressed integrated deterrence and named China the pacing challenge and Russia an acute threat. More recent US policy statements put greater weight on homeland and hemisphere defence while still prioritising deterrence of China along the first island chain. Verify The shifts show that the geographic balance between regions is a live argument inside Washington.

The US has bases or troops in dozens of countries; counts vary widely by definition, from several hundred to some 750 sites in about 80 countries in some estimates. Disputed Basing comes in tiers: large permanent bases (Ramstein, Kadena), smaller rotational sites, "lily pads" for contingency use, and access agreements with partners. Alliances differ from partnerships: treaty allies receive defence commitments, while partners such as India cooperate without them.

Strengths of the network

Early warning, presence that deters, logistics hubs, interoperability, and the ability to act without crossing an ocean first.

Vulnerabilities

Host-nation politics, exposure to missiles, cost, and the risk of entanglement in allies' disputes.

US defence and India

US-India defence ties grew from the 2005 defence framework to a Major Defence Partner designation (2016) and the "2+2" ministerial dialogue (from 2018). The foundational agreements are LEMOA (2016, logistics support), COMCASA (2018, secure communications) and BECA (2020, geospatial data sharing). A new 10-year defence framework was reported signed in 2025. Verify

Equipment links include C-17 and C-130J transports, P-8I maritime patrol aircraft, Apache and Chinook helicopters, MH-60R helicopters, M777 howitzers, and MQ-9B drones; co-production of engines and other systems is under discussion. Joint exercises include Malabar, Yudh Abhyas, Vajra Prahar, Tiger Triumph and Cope India.

Geography and friction

India sits between INDOPACOM and CENTCOM and astride the main Indian Ocean sea lanes, so it matters to US planners for Malacca and the western Indian Ocean. India keeps strategic autonomy: it buys Russian equipment such as the S-400, which raised CAATSA sanctions questions, and it does not seek a treaty alliance. Trade and tariff disputes in 2025 added strain. Verify

India's position

Both sides gain from cooperation: India obtains technology and maritime-domain awareness, while the US gains a large partner on the Indian Ocean rim. India's calculus balances this against its relationship with Russia, its neighbourhood, and its aim to build domestic defence industry.

Knowledge toolkit

Analysis and practice

Perspectives and controversies

Case for forward presence

Presence deters aggression, reassures allies, secures trade routes and prevents rivals from dominating Eurasia. Fighting far from home is better than fighting at home, and alliances multiply US power.

Case for restraint

The moat makes many deployments unnecessary; allies free-ride; wars like Iraq and Afghanistan were costly; bases invite attack and resentment; money could be used at home.

  • Disputed Whether defence spending is too high or too low relative to threats.
  • Interp Whether Chinese missile range makes forward bases such as Guam and Okinawa too vulnerable.
  • Interp Whether US posture is a global public good (freedom of navigation) or a tool of hegemony.
  • Fact The US keeps the world's largest carrier fleet and the largest network of overseas military sites.

Scenarios and options

Taiwan Strait crisis

Distance, base vulnerability and allied access (Japan, Philippines) decide US response options. India's choices: diplomatic restraint, humanitarian and maritime-domain awareness support, protection of its own interests in the Indian Ocean.

Hormuz disruption

US naval escort and air power could reopen the strait, but India's energy imports and diaspora in the Gulf are exposed. Options: diversify energy, coordinate naval escort with partners, maintain dialogue with Iran and Gulf states.

US retrenchment

If the US reduces forward presence, partners may build their own capabilities. India's options: expand its navy and domestic industry, deepen Quad and other minilateral cooperation, keep multiple partnerships.

Glossary

AOR
Area of responsibility assigned to a combatant command.
Combatant command
Unified military command with a geographic or functional mission.
Forward presence
Stationing or rotating forces overseas to deter and respond.
Island chain
Lines of islands in the western Pacific used to describe containment and defence.
Rimland
Coastal belt of Eurasia, in Spykman's theory the key to world power.
SLOC
Sea lines of communication: routes for trade and military movement.
Chokepoint
Narrow passage through which much traffic must pass.
Triad
Nuclear forces on land (ICBMs), sea (submarines) and in the air (bombers).
Integrated deterrence
2022 concept of combining all instruments and allies to deter.
FONOP
Freedom of navigation operation challenging excessive maritime claims.
EDCA
Enhanced Defense Cooperation Agreement with the Philippines.
2+2
Joint ministerial dialogue of foreign and defence ministers.
