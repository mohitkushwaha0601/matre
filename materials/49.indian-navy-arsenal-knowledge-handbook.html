Part I · Foundations

What the Navy has, and why it has it

What an arsenal is

An arsenal is not a list of ships. It is the set of platforms, weapons, sensors and people that together let a navy do four jobs: deny, control, project and deter.

  • Platforms carry things: ships, submarines, aircraft, unmanned vehicles.
  • Weapons hurt things: missiles, torpedoes, guns, mines, depth charges.
  • Sensors find things: radar, sonar, electronic-warfare suites, satellites.
  • Command and communications connect all three, so a fleet acts as one.
The core idea

A ship is only as good as its weapons and sensors, and a weapon is only as good as the sensor that cues it. Always describe a platform by its role, its main weapons and its sensors, not by tonnage alone.

The four naval roles in one line each

Sea denial
Stop an adversary using an area. Submarines and anti-ship missiles are the classic tools.
Sea control
Use an area yourself and protect shipping in it. Needs surface ships, air cover and anti-submarine capability.
Power projection
Reach land from the sea: carrier aircraft, land-attack missiles, amphibious forces.
Deterrence
Nuclear and conventional forces that make an adversary expect a cost. The nuclear-armed submarine is the sea leg of the triad.

From inheritance to indigenous build

  • 1947A small Royal Indian Navy of sloops and minesweepers, mostly British designs.
  • 1957INS Vikrant (the first, ex-HMS Hercules) acquired; India becomes a carrier navy.
  • 1960sSoviet supply begins: Petya frigates, Foxtrot submarines, Osa missile boats.
  • 1971Osa missile boats and Operation Trident, then Python, strike Karachi. Missile boats become a lasting doctrine.
  • 1980sIndigenous design begins: Godavari-class frigates (Project 16); Delhi class follows. First leased nuclear submarine (Chakra) in 1988.
  • 1999-2000sShivalik frigates, Talwar-class frigates, Scorpene deal (2005), Vikramaditya contracted (2004).
  • 2009INS Arihant launched, the first indigenous nuclear-powered submarine.
  • 2013-22Vikramaditya (2013) and Vikrant (2022) commissioned.
  • 2020sNilgiri and Visakhapatnam classes, the Kalvari class completes, and nearly all current orders are placed with Indian yards.

The pattern is steady: buy, license-build, then design. The Navy has been the most successful service in indigenous warship design, because ships can be designed and built in India even when many weapons and engines still come from abroad.

Part II · Surface fleet

Carriers, destroyers, frigates and the rest

Aircraft carriers

INS Vikramaditya

  • Former Soviet Admiral Gorshkov, a Kiev-class heavy aviation cruiser, rebuilt in Russia.
  • Commissioned in 2013 after years of delay and cost increases.
  • About 45,000 tonnes; STOBAR (short take-off but arrested recovery) with a ski-jump.
  • Carries MiG-29K fighters and helicopters.

INS Vikrant (IAC-1)

  • India's first indigenous carrier, built at Cochin Shipyard.
  • Commissioned on 2 September 2022.
  • About 40,000 to 45,000 tonnes; also STOBAR. Verify figures
  • Air wing: MiG-29K, Kamov and MH-60R helicopters, with Rafale M and other aircraft planned.

Why carriers matter: they put air power at sea, provide fleet air defence, and give reach over the Indian Ocean without relying on foreign bases. Why they are debated: they are expensive, they need a large escort group, and they are vulnerable to submarines and long-range anti-ship missiles. A third carrier, sometimes called IAC-2 or a larger catapult-launched design, has been discussed for years without a firm decision. Verify

Exam point

STOBAR versus CATOBAR: STOBAR (ski-jump) is simpler and cheaper but limits aircraft weight and sortie rate. CATOBAR (catapult) launches heavier aircraft and larger sensors but needs more power and complexity. India's current carriers are STOBAR, and the debate on the next one is largely about this choice.

INS Viraat, the former HMS Hermes, served until 2017 and was India's only carrier for a time after Vikrant (the first) retired in 1997.

Destroyers are the fleet's main air-defence and strike escorts, usually armed with long-range surface-to-air missiles, anti-ship missiles and anti-submarine systems.

A frigate is smaller and cheaper than a destroyer and often specialises in anti-submarine work and escort. In practice, Indian frigates and destroyers overlap, and the names reflect project history as much as role.

Corvettes, OPVs and small craft

  • Kamorta class (P-28): four anti-submarine corvettes built in India (Kamorta, Kadmatt, Kiltan, Kavaratti), with high indigenous content in steel and sensors.
  • Kora, Khukri, Veer classes: missile corvettes and missile craft of Soviet lineage; ageing.
  • Next Generation Corvettes and Missile Vessels: replacements planned or ordered; the programme has had delays. Verify
  • Anti-Submarine Warfare Shallow Water Craft (ASW SWC): a series of 16 small, quiet ships for coastal sub-hunting, starting with INS Arnala in 2025. Verify
  • Offshore patrol vessels: Saryu class and others, used for EEZ patrol and anti-piracy.
  • Mine-countermeasure vessels: an acknowledged gap; the old Pondicherry class are retired or old and the replacement programme has stalled.
  • Survey vessels: Sandhayak class and Nistar-class divers' support ships, which also serve submarine rescue.

These small ships are the workhorses of a navy: they patrol, escort and guard ports, far more than the headline ships do.

Amphibious and support ships

  • Landing Platform Dock: INS Jalashwa, the former USS Trenton, bought from the US in 2007; its purchase and use gave India amphibious experience. New LPDs have been proposed. Verify
  • Landing Ship Tank (Large): the Magar and Shardul classes, plus smaller landing craft.
  • Fleet tankers and support ships: Deepak class and newer Fleet Support Ships (built by Hindustan Shipyard); these let the fleet remain at sea far from base. Verify
  • Submarine rescue and diving support: DSRV (deep submergence rescue vehicles) and divers' ships.
  • Hospital and training ships: sail training ships INS Tarangini and Sudarshini; the Navy has no dedicated hospital ship of the Indian Ocean type, which has been mentioned as a HADR gap.
Why support ships decide range

Warships carry fuel and food for weeks, not months. Tankers and stores ships determine how long and how far a task force can operate, which is why logistics, not guns, set the real reach of a navy.

Why submarines are the Navy's most urgent shortfall: a diesel boat must surface or snorkel regularly to recharge, which makes it detectable. AIP lets it stay submerged for weeks, so it is quieter for longer. India's conventional fleet has been shrinking as old boats retire faster than new ones arrive, and an old planning document called for 24 boats. Verify

Exam point

Submarines are valued for being hidden. Quietness, endurance and the ability to fire without being found are what count, not tonnage. This is also why anti-submarine warfare (ASW) is the largest hidden cost in naval planning.

Nuclear submarines

The two kinds

  • SSBN carries ballistic missiles with nuclear warheads: the sea-based deterrent.
  • SSN is nuclear-powered but conventionally armed: a fast, long-endurance hunter of ships and submarines.

The strategic logic. India's nuclear doctrine is No First Use with credible minimum deterrence. A submarine at sea is the most survivable weapon, so it makes an adversary's first strike pointless: retaliation is assured. The weakness is that Indian SSBNs are comparatively noisy and their missiles have shorter range than those of the large powers, which means they must patrol closer to a target. Interpretation

The programme is run under the Advanced Technology Vessel (ATV) project, kept largely secret, with the reactor developed at the Bhabha Atomic Research Centre and construction at Visakhapatnam. Russian help in the early stages is widely reported. Details disputed

The BrahMos story

BrahMos is the Navy's signature weapon: fast enough that a ship has little time to react, and one of the few supersonic anti-ship missiles in service. It is also India's best-known arms export, with Philippines as the first foreign buyer. Verify The joint venture takes its name from the Brahmaputra and Moskva rivers.

Torpedoes, guns, mines and defence

  • Heavyweight torpedoes: Varunastra (indigenous), Black Shark (Italian, for Scorpenes), older Russian types.
  • Lightweight torpedoes: Shyena (indigenous) and imported MK 54 for helicopters and aircraft.
  • Main guns: 76 mm and 100 mm naval guns on most ships; Indian-built 76 mm guns have been supplied by BHEL. Verify
  • Close-in defence: AK-630 and Kashtan rapid-fire systems; last-ditch protection against missiles and boats.
  • Anti-submarine rockets: RBU-6000 and indigenous launchers.
  • Decoys and countermeasures: chaff, flares, and the Maareech torpedo-defence decoy system.
  • Mines: a modest stock; an area where the Navy is thin compared with the threat from adversary mining. Interpretation

The principle is layered defence: long-range missile, then medium, then short, then gun, then decoys. No single layer is relied upon.

Sensors, command and special forces

  • Radar: air-search, surface-search and fire-control radars, with Israeli, Russian, Dutch and Indian sets in use.
  • Sonar: hull-mounted, towed-array and dunking sonars. Indian sonars include the HUMSA and Abhay families, developed by DRDO's naval laboratories. Verify
  • Electronic warfare: radar warners and jammers such as the Indian Shakti system.
  • Command and control: the Navy's combat-management systems and data links tie ships and aircraft together; GSAT-7 gives secure satellite links. See the NavIC and Navy handbook for the space and navigation layer.
  • MARCOS: the Navy's special force, trained for maritime raiding, counter-terrorism and hostage rescue.
  • Marine and amphibious capability: the Navy has no separate marine corps; Army units and MARCOS take amphibious tasks. Debated

The pattern

  • Hulls and numbers: deliveries of destroyers, frigates and small ASW ships are now steady.
  • Silent hunters: new SSBNs and the final Scorpene; the shallow-water craft counter submarines near the coast.
  • Air: Rafale M and MH-60R fill gaps in carrier and ship-borne aviation.
  • Last imports: Tushil and Tamal close the era of buying finished warships from abroad.

Caveats

  • Launch, delivery and commissioning are three different dates; news reports often mix them.
  • A ship may be commissioned before every weapon and sensor is fully fitted or proven.
  • Some figures here come from news reports, not official summaries.

The pipeline and what comes next

  • Fleet size: open sources put the fleet at about 130 surface combatants and about 20 submarines in October 2026. Navy statements give a target of 150 to 160 ships by about 2030 and over 200 by 2035. About 45 warships were reported under construction in May 2026, and approvals in principle were reported for roughly 195 more vessels. Verify
  • Submarines: the Project 75I deal for six AIP boats with a German partner was reported close to signature through 2026. Two indigenous nuclear attack submarines were cleared in 2024. Verify status
  • Surface ships: a request for proposals for seven Project 17B stealth frigates was reported in September 2026, with competitive bidding among yards. New corvettes, next-generation offshore patrol vessels and missile vessels are under construction or planned.
  • Amphibious: a request for proposals for four landing platform docks was reported in October 2026, with a UK partnership on electric propulsion discussed. Verify
  • Support ships: INS Surya, the first indigenous fleet support ship, was launched at Hindustan Shipyard in October 2026, with five planned.
  • Air and unmanned: 31 MQ-9B drones (shared among the services) ordered in 2024; more medium-altitude drones, armed unmanned boats and underwater vehicles under development.
  • Weapons: a goal reported in 2025 to fit BrahMos across the destroyer and frigate fleet by 2030; work on land-attack missiles for conventional submarines.
  • Longer term: a 2025 technology roadmap mentions nuclear-propelled surface ships and a future carrier, reported as INS Vishal. Aspiration, not a programme
Check before quoting

This section moves fastest of all. Dates, contract values and approvals shift between announcement and signature. Quote the month and the source, and say "reported" for anything not in a PIB release.

Part VI · Industry and strategy

Who builds it, and what is missing

Shipbuilding and indigenisation

  • Mazagon Dock (Mumbai): destroyers, frigates, Scorpene submarines.
  • Garden Reach Shipbuilders and Engineers (Kolkata): frigates, corvettes, ASW craft.
  • Cochin Shipyard: the carrier Vikrant and support ships.
  • Goa Shipyard, Hindustan Shipyard, L&T and private yards: offshore vessels, fleet support and small craft.
  • Design: the Directorate of Naval Design, a long-standing in-house design organisation, produces most Indian warship designs.

Navy leadership has said that nearly all new ships and submarines on order are being built in Indian yards, and has quoted indigenous content figures that reach into the high double digits for hulls. Verify figures

The honest caveat

"Built in India" is not the same as "made in India". Gas turbines, diesel engines, many radars, missiles, sonars and some weapon systems still come from abroad. Indigenous hull is far ahead of indigenous heart (propulsion and weapons).

Gaps and debates

Capability gaps

  • Submarine numbers and AIP.
  • Mine-countermeasure vessels.
  • Fleet-wide shortage of modern helicopters and carrier fighters.
  • Long-range, wide-area anti-submarine capability.
  • Engines: dependence on foreign gas turbines and diesel engines.
  • Ageing Russian-origin ships needing spares.

Structural debates

  • Carriers versus submarines as the priority.
  • Fewer large ships versus more small ones.
  • Nuclear attack submarines versus more conventional boats.
  • The defence budget share: the Navy's share has been about 15 to 20 per cent of capital spending. Verify
  • Jointness: theatre commands, with a maritime theatre command often proposed. Interpretation

A balanced answer is that India needs both sea control and sea denial. Submarines deny the Indian Ocean to an adversary; surface ships and aircraft let India use it. The question is the mix, not either-or.

Knowledge toolkit

Analysis, practice and revision

Perspectives and facts

The optimist's view

The Navy is the most indigenised and best-planned service. It has two carriers, a nuclear deterrent at sea, a modern destroyer and frigate line, and nearly all new ships come from Indian yards. Time and steady budgets would close the gaps.

The sceptic's view

The fleet is too small for the task, the submarine arm is shrinking, the carriers lack enough modern fighters, and the Indian-built hulls still run on imported engines and weapons. Delays have outpaced the growth of the Chinese Navy in the Indian Ocean.

Lesser-known facts

  • A submerged submarine cannot use satellite navigation or GPS, and relies on inertial systems.
  • In 1971 Soviet-built Osa-class missile boats sank the Pakistani destroyer PNS Khaibar and other ships off Karachi. Established
  • India's earlier carriers: the first Vikrant (1961) and Viraat (1987) were both British-built.

The Navy's arsenal in one page

  • Carriers: Vikramaditya (2013, bought) and Vikrant (2022, built at Cochin); both STOBAR.
  • Escorts: Kolkata and Visakhapatnam destroyers; Shivalik and Nilgiri frigates; Talwar-class; Kamorta corvettes; ASW shallow-water craft.
  • Submarines: Kalvari (Scorpene) six boats, Kilo and Type 209 ageing, P-75I planned; SSBN Arihant, Arighaat and follow-ons; leased SSN.
  • Air: MiG-29K, Rafale M coming; P-8I, MH-60R, unmanned aircraft.
  • Weapons: BrahMos, Klub, Barak-8, Varunastra, Black Shark, K-15 and K-4.
  • Industry: Mazagon Dock, GRSE, Cochin, Goa, plus private yards; hulls ahead of engines and weapons.
