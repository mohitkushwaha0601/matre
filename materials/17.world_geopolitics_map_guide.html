Five map questions worth memorising

  1. What can this country reach easily? Land corridors, ports, air routes and nearby markets.
  2. What can easily reach this country? Trade, military pressure, migration, investment and ideas.
  3. What can it block? A chokepoint, river, border pass, cable route or resource corridor.
  4. What does it depend on? Fuel, food, shipping, technology, finance, water, minerals or allies.
  5. What can it supply that others need? Energy, minerals, food, industrial capacity, capital, technology or geographic access.

Do not confuse these concepts

Geography vs geopolitics

Geography is the physical and human spatial setting. Geopolitics studies how location, territory and spatial relationships affect power and statecraft.

GDP vs GDP (PPP)

Nominal GDP converts output at market exchange rates; PPP adjusts for local purchasing power. Use nominal GDP for many financial comparisons and PPP for some living-standard or real-resource comparisons.

Country vs economy

International datasets sometimes use “economy” because statistical reporting can cover territories or entities without treating them as sovereign states.

Alliance vs partnership

An alliance may include formal mutual commitments; partnerships can be looser, issue-specific or non-binding. Read the actual treaty or official description.

Continents as geopolitical systems

Do not study continents as isolated boxes. Study the seams: Mediterranean-North Africa, Europe-Russia, Gulf-South Asia, China-Southeast Asia, Arctic-North America, Red Sea-Horn of Africa and Pacific island chains.

Strategic chokepoints & corridors

A chokepoint matters when a geographically constrained passage carries a large amount of trade, energy or strategic movement. The correct question is not just “how important?” but “important to whom, for what, and with what alternatives?”

Maritime

Suez → Red Sea → Bab el-Mandeb

Connects the Mediterranean and European markets with the Indian Ocean and Asian trade routes.

Energy

Persian Gulf → Strait of Hormuz

Geography compresses Gulf energy flows into a narrow maritime gateway before they enter the Gulf of Oman.

Indo-Pacific

Malacca → South China Sea

A core maritime bridge between the Indian Ocean and East Asian manufacturing centers.

Europe

Turkish Straits → Black Sea

A strategic hinge between the Black Sea basin and the Mediterranean.

Americas

Panama Canal

A constructed chokepoint that shortens Atlantic-Pacific routes and links two ocean systems.

Alternate route

Cape of Good Hope

A longer but geographically unconstrained alternative to canal routes around southern Africa.

History that actually helps you read geopolitics

The goal is not to memorise dates. Learn how political geography accumulated layer by layer.

The six historical layers

Ancient River valleys, trade routes, city-states, empires.
Medieval Religious networks, steppe powers, dynastic states and trade.
Early modern Oceanic empires, gunpowder states, mercantilism.
Industrial Railways, ports, factories, finance and mass armies.
World wars Total war, borders, colonial collapse and new institutions.
Cold War → today Nuclear deterrence, blocs, globalization, strategic competition.

Use history to answer “why here?”

A border often makes more sense after you know the empire, colonial administration, war, treaty or partition that produced it. Geography constrains choices; history explains why today's institutions, borders and identities look the way they do.

The global economy as a map

Economic power is spatial. Factories, ports, oil fields, mines, data centers, cables, banks and consumers are all located somewhere.

Output

GDP

Measures the value of production within an economy. Useful for scale; not the same thing as household prosperity or state capacity.

People

GDP per capita

Approximates average output per person. Useful for comparisons, but distribution and public services still matter.

Purchasing power

GDP (PPP)

Accounts for differences in price levels. Particularly useful when comparing domestic purchasing power.

External position

Trade + finance

Ask who buys the country's exports, who supplies its imports, who finances it and what currency is used.

Resource geography

Energy and minerals can create strategic leverage, but abundance does not automatically create prosperity. Institutions, infrastructure, market access, human capital and diversification influence how resource endowments translate into state power.

Oil & gas
Gulf, Russia, North America, Africa, Latin America
Copper
Andes, Central Africa, North America and other producers
Critical minerals
Lithium, cobalt, nickel, graphite, rare earths

Supply-chain geography

A supply chain can be geographically concentrated at extraction, refining, manufacturing, shipping or final demand. The strategic weak point may be the processing stage rather than the mine itself.

IEA's 2026 critical-minerals analysis highlights rising concentration in refining and the growing use of export restrictions as an economic-security tool.

World Bank income groups — learn the bands, not the stereotypes

For FY2027, the World Bank classifies economies using GNI per capita (Atlas method): low income ≤ US$1,175; lower middle income US$1,176-4,635; upper middle income US$4,636-14,375; high income > US$14,375.

Income classification is a statistical grouping, not a complete description of political stability, state capacity, military power, inequality or living standards.

A practical 30-day geopolitics study loop

Spend 25-40 minutes a day. The map is the notebook.

Days 1-5

World geography

Continents, oceans, seas, mountain ranges, major rivers, deserts, canals, straits, peninsulas and island chains.

Days 6-12

Nations

Capitals, borders, neighbors, population centers, currencies, key exports and geographic constraints.

Days 13-20

History

Empires → colonialism → world wars → Cold War → post-1991 order. Always locate events on the map.

Days 21-30

Current affairs

Read one headline, then explain it using location, economic dependence, institutions, interests and alternatives.

Daily drill: Pick one country and one chokepoint. Without searching, explain why each matters, who depends on it, what its vulnerabilities are, and what geographic alternatives exist. Then verify yourself.
