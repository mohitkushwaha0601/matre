A Sailor's Life at Sea: Day, Night, Tools and Water Knowledge

If you were a sailor in the Indian Navy, what would your day and night look like, what would you work with, and what would you need to know about the water? This handbook follows one enrolled sailor from entry to the watch bill, through the instruments (compass, ship's log, echo sounder, radar, sonar), the engine room, seamanship, weather, tides and drills, with SSB-style practice material.

Using this handbook

A sailor is an enrolled member of the Navy: the person who steers, keeps watch, runs the machinery, fires the weapons and keeps the ship alive. This handbook shows that work from the inside, then explains the water it happens on.

Read it in three passes. First, the day and night routine (Parts I and II). Second, the instruments and machines (Part III). Third, the water, weather and safety knowledge (Parts IV and V), which is what converts a trained hand into a seaman. Handbook 1 covers the Navy as an institution (commands, fleets, bases); this one stays at deck level.

The idea that organises everything

A ship never sleeps. Every system in this handbook (watches, bells, instruments, drills) exists so that someone competent is always awake, always measuring, and always able to act.

Caveats

  • Established Nautical units, instrument principles, the Beaufort scale, the watch system and standard safety drills are universal seafaring practice.
  • Interpretation Daily timetables vary with the ship, the operational tempo and the commanding officer. The routines here are illustrative.
  • Re-verify Entry rules, trade names and equipment fits change with policy and ship class; check the official Navy recruitment portal and current notices.

Part I · Who and where

The sailor and the ship

The sailor: entry, branches, ranks

Indian Navy personnel are either commissioned officers or sailors (also called ratings). Sailors form the large majority of the workforce and carry out the hands-on technical, seamanship and support tasks.

Since 2022, sailors have been recruited through the Agnipath scheme as Agniveers: a four-year engagement, after which a limited share (announced as up to 25 per cent) are retained in the regular cadre. Re-verify Recruits train at INS Chilka in Odisha, the Navy's main sailor training establishment, before specialist trade training in other schools. Women have been inducted as sailors since the first Agniveer intake.

The rank ladder for sailors rises from Seaman II and Seaman I, to Leading Seaman, Petty Officer, Chief Petty Officer, and Master Chief Petty Officer II and I, with selected sailors later commissioned as officers. Chiefs and petty officers are the technical and supervisory backbone: they train juniors, run work parties and keep standards.

Same Navy, different seat

An officer decides what the ship should do; sailors make it happen. The Navy Act, 1957 and the chain of command bind both, but a sailor's competence at the controls is what safe ship-handling rests on.

The ship as a workplace

A warship is a floating town, power station and weapons platform in one steel hull. The first thing a sailor learns is its geography.

Directions on a ship do not use left and right. Everything is described relative to the bow (front), stern (back), port (left when facing forward) and starboard (right). Floors are decks, walls are bulkheads, ceilings are overheads, stairs are ladders, doors are doors or hatches (watertight doors when fitted), kitchen is the galley, and the sailors' dining and living area is the mess or messdeck. The ship is divided into watertight compartments so that damage can be confined.

Ships a sailor may serve on include destroyers, frigates, corvettes, patrol vessels, fleet tankers and support ships, and submarines (see Handbook 1 for classes). Life differs sharply between a large ship with crowded but spacious mess decks and a submarine where space is the scarcest resource.

Part II · Day and night

The rhythm of a ship

A day on board

A sailor's day is built from two overlapping clocks: the fixed daily routine everyone follows, and the watch schedule that keeps part of the crew on duty around the clock.

In harbour the ship runs a regular working day with ceremonial moments: Colours at 0800 (the naval ensign is hoisted) and Sunset (it is lowered). At sea, the same working day continues for those off watch, wrapped around the watch rotation. Orders are passed by the ship's broadcast, and traditional calls are made with the bosun's call (pipe). Everything below is an illustrative pattern.

What "work" actually means

  • Preservation: chipping rust, painting, greasing; salt water attacks steel constantly.
  • Maintenance: planned checks of pumps, valves, motors, radars, guns and boats.
  • Training: trade courses, seamanship practice, swimming and fitness.
  • Evolutions: anchoring, mooring, boat launch, towing, refuelling at sea, man-overboard recovery.

Duties beyond the working day

  • Duty watch: a part of the crew stays on board in harbour for fire, security and emergencies.
  • Gangway watch: controls access to the ship and records arrivals.
  • Rounds: patrols that check for fire, flooding, loose gear and unsafe conditions.

Watches, bells and the night

The watch system divides the 24 hours so that someone is always alert on the bridge, in the machinery spaces and in the operations room, while everyone else gets rest.

Ship's bells mark time within a watch: one bell for each half hour, rising to eight bells at the end of a four-hour watch (for example, 0030 is one bell, 0100 two bells, 0400 eight bells). In the dog watches the pattern restarts so that watches rotate. In the common three-watch system, each group stands four hours on and eight hours off. Because the dog watches make seven watches a day (an odd number), the crew's hours rotate and no group is stuck with the middle watch every night.

A sailor's sample day on a three-watch rhythm

A helmsman in the "A" watch stands the middle watch (0000-0400) and the afternoon watch (1200-1600). In between he sleeps after 0400, has breakfast, takes his turn in cleaning stations, and does maintenance or training in the forenoon. After the afternoon watch he eats, rests and attends a short drill. The eight hours "off" are never wholly free: drills, maintenance and ship-wide evolutions overlap them. Interpretation Actual rotas depend on the ship's manning and the operation.

Who stands the watch

  • Bridge: the Officer of the Watch commands; sailors serve as helmsman (steers on ordered courses), lookouts (port and starboard), and messenger or signalman.
  • Operations room: radar, sonar and communications operators maintain the picture.
  • Machinery spaces: engineering watchkeepers read gauges, tour machinery, respond to alarms.
  • Harbour: gangway sentry, fire and security patrols, boat crews.

Night at sea

At sunset the ship darkens: external lights are reduced to the navigation lights and interior lighting is shielded. On the bridge, dim red light is used so that eyes stay dark-adapted; full adaptation takes about half an hour, and a flash of white light resets it. Lookouts use averted vision: looking slightly to one side of a faint object rather than directly at it, because the edge of the retina is more sensitive in low light.

A lookout reports what he sees by type, bearing and distance, in the form "light, bearing green three zero, one mile": a light 30 degrees to starboard of the bow. At night, fatigue is as dangerous as darkness, so lookouts are rotated at short intervals, kept moving, and relieved on time.

Why rules about lights matter

Red on the left, green on the right. Seeing red means her port side faces you; seeing both red and green means she is approaching almost head on. A lookout who misjudges these colours can misjudge a collision.

Part III · Tools and machines

What a sailor measures, steers and maintains

Where am I: compass, charts, GNSS

Navigation answers three questions: where am I, where am I going, and how do I get there safely. Every instrument in this section feeds one of them.

The classic approach is dead reckoning: start from a known position and add your course, speed and time. Steaming on course 090 at 12 knots for 2.5 hours takes the ship 30 nautical miles east. Modern instruments check and refine this continuously, and the navigator keeps the chart as the master record.

Three kinds of north

True north is the geographic pole. Magnetic north differs from it by the local variation. Compass north differs again by the ship's deviation. A navigator corrects from compass to magnetic to true.

Courses and bearings

Directions are given in three figures from north through east: 000 is north, 090 east, 180 south, 270 west. A relative bearing measures from the ship's bow, in the red/green form at night ("green 30").

The layered-safety principle

No single source of position is trusted alone. GNSS is cross-checked against radar ranges, visual bearings, depth readings and dead reckoning. A sailor on watch is trained to notice when two sources disagree.

How fast and how deep: log and echo sounder

The "odometer" of a ship is its log: it reports speed through the water and the distance run. The "depth gauge" is the echo sounder: it times a sound pulse to the seabed and back.

The ship's log (speed and distance)

Early sailors threw a wooden board on a knotted line over the stern and counted the knots paying out in a set time. That is where the word knot comes from. Modern ships use an electromagnetic log (measures flow past the hull) or a Doppler log (sends acoustic beams and measures frequency shift, giving speed through the water or over the seabed). The log also keeps a running distance count, just like a car's odometer, and feeds the navigation system.

The echo sounder (depth)

A transducer under the hull sends a pulse downward; the time for the echo to return gives the depth. Sound travels at about 1,500 metres per second in seawater, so depth = (speed of sound × time) ÷ 2. A return time of 0.4 seconds means 1,500 × 0.4 ÷ 2 = 300 metres. Before electronics, sailors used the hand lead line: a lead weight on a line marked in fathoms, cast ahead and read by feel and call.

Units every sailor uses

  • 1 nautical mile = 1,852 m = 1 minute of latitude. 1 knot = 1 nautical mile per hour = 1.852 km/h.
  • 1 fathom = 6 feet (about 1.83 m). 1 cable = one-tenth of a nautical mile (about 185 m).
  • Speed × time = distance. At 15 knots for 4 hours, the ship covers 60 nautical miles, or about 111 km.

Sensors, communications and weapons

Sailors in the operations room, signals department and weapons teams work with the instruments that see, hear, talk and strike.

Radar

Sends radio pulses and times the echoes to show range and bearing of ships, aircraft and land. Navigation radar helps in fog and at night; surveillance and fire-control radars track targets. Operators use ARPA (automatic radar plotting aid) to compute other ships' course, speed and closest approach.

Sonar

Uses sound underwater. Active sonar sends a ping and listens for the echo; passive sonar listens silently. Hull-mounted and towed arrays detect submarines and surface ships. Water temperature layers bend sound and shape what operators can hear.

AIS and electronic support

AIS broadcasts ship identity, position, course and speed by VHF radio and is widely used by merchant ships. Electronic support measures listen for radar and radio emissions, helping to identify what is nearby without transmitting.

Communications

  • VHF and HF radio: short-range tactical and long-range voice and data.
  • Satellite links: long-distance secure links; the Navy uses a dedicated naval communications satellite, GSAT-7 (Rukmini), launched in 2013 Re-verify and successors.
  • Visual signalling: signal flags (International Code of Signals), signal lamp (Morse code), semaphore. Used when radio silence is ordered or equipment fails.

Weapons (overview)

Depending on the ship class, a sailor in the weapons or electrical branch may operate and maintain a medium-calibre naval gun, a close-in weapon system for anti-missile defence, surface-to-surface and surface-to-air missiles (examples include BrahMos and Barak-8 on selected ships), torpedoes and anti-submarine rockets, and small arms for boarding and security teams. Re-verify Weapon fits vary by ship; see Handbook 1 for system-level detail.

Seamanship: lines, knots, anchors

Seamanship is the craft that keeps a ship secured, moving and safe in a harbour or in rough water. It is the oldest part of a sailor's skill.

On a ship, ropes are lines (or hawsers or cables, depending on size and use). Moorings use several lines: head and stern lines hold the ship fore and aft, breast lines hold it close to the quay, and springs stop it moving ahead or astern. A heaving line with a weighted knot (a monkey's fist) is thrown ashore first and draws the heavy mooring line after it. Fenders cushion the hull; bollards and capstans take the strain; the windlass or anchor cable gear handles the anchor on the forecastle.

Safety around lines

  • Never stand in the bight (loop) of a line under strain.
  • A parting line whips back violently; keep clear of the snap-back zone.
  • Wear gloves, helmets and safety footwear for deck work.

Boats and survival craft

  • Sailors crew the ship's boats, rescue boats and rafts.
  • Lifebuoys, life jackets and rafts are inspected on a routine schedule.
  • Every sailor learns to swim and to handle a boat.

Engine room and ship's water

The engine room turns fuel into motion, electricity and fresh water. Engineering sailors keep all three flowing, usually in a hot, loud and unforgiving space.

Warship propulsion generally uses gas turbines, diesel engines, or a combination, driving the shafts and propellers directly or through gearboxes; older ships used steam turbines, conventional submarines use diesel-electric drive, and nuclear submarines use reactor-driven propulsion. Separate generators supply electricity for lighting, radars, weapons and pumps. A watchkeeper takes readings on gauges (temperature, pressure, RPM, oil level, vibration), listens for abnormal noise, checks for leaks, and reports to the control room.

Making fresh water from the sea

Seawater cannot be drunk or used in boilers. Evaporators boil seawater under reduced pressure and condense the vapour into fresh water; reverse osmosis plants force seawater through membranes that hold back the salt. Fresh water is a managed resource: it is produced, stored, tested and sometimes rationed. A ship that loses its water plants for days must restrict washing, laundry and cooking water.

Part IV · Water knowledge

Seawater, weather, tides and charts

The nature of seawater

Sailors work on water, in it, with it and against it. A few physical facts explain most of what they see at sea.

Indian waters have their own pattern. Surface water in the Indian Ocean is warm, often around 28 to 30°C, which feeds the monsoon and fuels cyclones. The Arabian Sea is generally saltier than the Bay of Bengal, which receives large river discharge. Interpretation These are broad averages; local salinity varies with season and rainfall.

Why a ship floats

A ship floats because the weight of water it displaces equals its own weight (Archimedes' principle). Stability depends on how weight is distributed. Sailors protect it by keeping watertight doors shut at the right times, controlling fuel and ballast, and never letting water collect free on a deck.

Indian seasons at sea

  • South-west monsoon (June to September): strong winds and heavy seas, especially off the west coast.
  • Cyclone seasons: mainly before the monsoon (April to June) and after it (October to December), in the Arabian Sea and the Bay of Bengal.
  • North-east monsoon (winter): calmer on the west, rain on the south-east coast.

Working in rough weather

  • Secure for sea: lash down gear, close watertight doors.
  • Wear lifelines or harnesses for work on open decks.
  • Reduce speed or alter course to ease rolling and pitching.
  • Manage seasickness: fresh air, a fixed horizon, light food, keeping busy.

Ships receive forecasts and warnings by radio and satellite from meteorological and maritime authorities. India's National Hydrographic Office coordinates maritime safety information for its navigational area (NAVAREA VIII). Re-verify In fog or heavy rain, a ship reduces speed, sounds fog signals, posts extra lookouts and relies on radar.

Tides, charts and buoyage

Tides raise and lower the sea twice a day on most Indian coasts. They decide when a ship can enter a port and how much water lies under her keel.

Tides arise mainly from the Moon's gravity, with a smaller effect from the Sun. Spring tides (largest range) occur near new and full moon, when the Sun and Moon line up. Neap tides (smallest range) occur near the quarter phases. High waters recur about every 12 hours 25 minutes in a semi-diurnal pattern. Some parts of India, such as the Gulf of Khambhat and Gulf of Kutch, have very large tidal ranges of several metres, while other stretches see far smaller ranges. Re-verify

Rule of twelfths (rough estimate of the tide's rise or fall)

In the six hours between low and high water the tide rises 1/12 of its range in the first hour, 2/12 in the second, 3/12 in the third, 3/12 in the fourth, 2/12 in the fifth and 1/12 in the sixth. So for a 6 m range, the sea rises 3 m in the first three hours and 4.5 m by the end of the fourth hour. It is an estimate, not a substitute for the tide table.

Charts and the chart datum

A nautical chart shows depths (soundings), the coast, hazards, lights, buoys, channels and tidal data. Depths are measured from a reference level called chart datum; the actual depth at any moment is the charted depth plus the height of the tide. Latitude is read from the side scale: one minute of latitude is one nautical mile. In India, charts and tide tables are produced by the Navy's National Hydrographic Office at Dehradun, with survey vessels collecting the data. Re-verify

Under-keel clearance, in practice

Water under the keel = charted depth + height of tide − ship's draught. A ship with a draught of 6 m, entering a channel charted at 5 m with a tide of 2.5 m, has 5 + 2.5 − 6 = 1.5 m under the keel, a margin the navigating team will judge against swell, squat and the accuracy of the data.

Part V · Safety and life

Drills, survival and life on board

Drills, damage control and survival

A ship at sea cannot call for outside help in minutes. So every sailor trains until the response to fire, flooding or a man overboard is automatic.

If you are in the water

  • Float and conserve energy; do not thrash.
  • Use the life jacket light and whistle to signal.
  • Stay with other survivors; a group is easier to spot.
  • In warm Indian waters, the main risks are exhaustion, dehydration and exposure rather than extreme cold.

Habits that save ships

  • Keep watertight doors and hatches in their correct state.
  • Report a small leak or a hot bearing immediately.
  • Know two routes out of every compartment you work in.
  • Treat drills as the real thing.

Life aboard and naval customs

Life aboard is crowded, disciplined and communal. Sailors share small spaces, eat in relays, and build a loyalty that shore jobs rarely require.

Sailors sleep in bunks in messdecks or small cabins, store belongings in lockers and keep their space spotless. Meals are cooked in the galley by catering sailors, and fresh water is used carefully. Communication with family is limited by the ship's connectivity and the operational situation. Deployments can last weeks or months, with port calls for refuelling, repairs and rest. Interpretation Conditions vary greatly: a large ship has more space and amenities; a submarine has strict space and schedule limits, and bunks may be shared by crew on different watches.

Customs and ceremonies

  • Colours and Sunset: daily hoisting and lowering of the naval ensign in harbour.
  • Bosun's call (pipe): used to pass orders and to honour visitors; piping the side is a mark of respect.
  • Quarterdeck: saluted when stepping on board or leaving.
  • Navy Day: 4 December, marking Operation Trident in 1971.
  • Naval ensign: a new design replaced the earlier St George's Cross version in September 2022.

Keeping body and mind in order

  • Regular exercise, adequate sleep (a hard but vital discipline on watch systems) and balanced food.
  • Hobbies, reading, sports, small group activities, and talking to seniors and shipmates when stressed.
  • Hygiene: clean clothes, clean mess, clean hands. A sick sailor on a small crew is a risk to everyone.
Why the Navy emphasises teamwork

On a ship, individuals cannot walk away from each other. A sailor who is reliable on watch, tidy in the mess and quick to help someone else strengthens the whole crew. These are exactly the qualities the SSB looks for as cooperation, responsibility and social adjustment.

Myths, perspectives and facts

Popular images of navy life are often romantic or one-dimensional. Test them against how a ship actually works.

Common ideas about a sailor's life and a measured reading
IdeaStatusMeasured reading
A ship is always on watch, day and night.EstablishedThe watch system ensures continuous bridge, machinery and operations coverage.
Sailors only do cleaning and painting.InterpretationPreservation is vital, but sailors also operate sensors and weapons, run machinery, steer, handle boats and respond to emergencies.
Modern electronics have made seamanship unnecessary.DisputedElectronic systems fail, are jammed or lose power; backups such as magnetic compass, lead line and visual signals remain core skills.
Life at sea is all adventure.InterpretationMuch of it is routine, repetitive and demanding; the adventure comes from the work and the places, not from every hour.
Ship routines are identical across all vessels.Re-verifyTimetables vary with ship type, role and the commanding officer's orders.

View 1: Tradition keeps ships safe

Bells, watches, drills and rituals create shared habits that work under stress. Standard routines reduce errors when people are tired or frightened.

View 2: Technology is changing the sailor's job

Automation, integrated bridge systems and unmanned vehicles reduce manual work and shift roles toward technical supervision and data handling. Training must keep up while preserving fallback skills.

Scenarios and options

Systems fail. A good sailor knows what the fallback is before the failure.

Practice bank: questions and motions

Fourteen questions to answer aloud in about ninety seconds each, then seven motions for group discussion.

  1. Describe a sailor's day at sea, from the morning watch to the first watch, and explain why the day is divided into watches.
  2. What are the dog watches, and why do they exist?
  3. Explain how a ship's log works and why it is often called the ship's odometer.
  4. An echo sounder returns an echo after 0.6 seconds. What is the depth, and what assumption did you use?
  5. Distinguish true, magnetic and compass north, and explain variation and deviation.
  6. Why is a gyrocompass preferred over a magnetic compass, and why is the magnetic compass still carried?
  7. You see a green light 30 degrees on your bow at night. What does it tell you, and how would you report it?
  8. Why does a ship sit lower in a river than in the open sea?
  9. Describe how a ship makes fresh water and why it must be managed carefully.
  10. A tide has a range of 6 metres. How much does it rise in the first three hours after low water?
  11. Name the stages of a man-overboard response and the sailor's role in each.
  12. What backups would you use if GNSS and radar were both unavailable?
  13. Why do sailors preserve and paint steel so often?
  14. How does teamwork on a ship connect to the Officer Like Qualities assessed at the SSB?

Debate motions

  1. Seamanship skills remain essential even in the age of satellite navigation.
  2. Automation will reduce, not increase, the need for skilled sailors.
  3. Strict routine and ritual make ships safer rather than rigid.
  4. Fatigue is a greater threat at sea than weather.
  5. Every young Indian should learn to swim and handle a boat.
  6. Short-service engagements, like Agnipath, serve the Navy better than long-service enrolment.
  7. A sailor's technical expertise matters more than his physical toughness.

Glossary

Bow / stern
Front / back of the ship.
Port / starboard
Left / right when facing forward.
Deck, bulkhead, overhead, ladder
Floor, wall, ceiling and stairs of a ship.
Galley / mess
Ship's kitchen / dining and living area for sailors.
Knot
One nautical mile per hour (1.852 km/h).
Nautical mile
1,852 metres; one minute of latitude.
Fathom
Six feet; unit of depth.
Cable
One-tenth of a nautical mile.
Draught
Depth of the hull below the waterline.
Freeboard
Height of the deck above the waterline.
Heading / bearing
The direction the ship points / the direction of an object from the ship.
Variation / deviation
Difference between true and magnetic north / the ship's own magnetic effect on the compass.
Dead reckoning
Estimating position from course, speed and time since a known position.
Log
Instrument measuring a ship's speed and distance through water.
Echo sounder
Instrument measuring depth by timing a sound pulse to the seabed and back.
Gyrocompass
A gyroscope-based compass that finds true north.
ECDIS
Electronic Chart Display and Information System.
ARPA
Automatic Radar Plotting Aid; computes other ships' courses and closest approach.
AIS
Automatic Identification System; ships broadcast identity and position.
Watch
A period of duty, normally four hours, during which part of the crew keeps the ship operating.
Dog watch
One of the two two-hour watches from 1600 to 2000.
Bosun's call
A whistle used to pass orders and honour visitors.
Chart datum
The reference level from which charted depths are measured.
Spring / neap tide
Larger / smaller tides near new and full moon / near the quarter phases.
Agniveer
A sailor recruited under the Agnipath scheme.

Rapid revision sheet

One-page summary

  • Sailor: enrolled member of the Navy; branches include seaman, engineering, electrical, air, supply and medical; entry via Agnipath (Agniveer); basic training at INS Chilka.
  • Ship directions: bow, stern, port (left), starboard (right); deck, bulkhead, overhead, ladder, galley, mess.
  • Watches: middle 0000-0400, morning 0400-0800, forenoon 0800-1200, afternoon 1200-1600, two dog watches 1600-2000, first 2000-0000; eight bells ends a watch.
  • Night: darken ship, red light for dark adaptation, averted vision; green on starboard, red on port; masthead and stern white lights.
  • Instruments: magnetic and gyro compass, sextant, GNSS, ECDIS, ship's log (speed and distance), echo sounder (depth = speed of sound × time ÷ 2), radar, sonar, AIS.
  • Units: 1 nautical mile = 1,852 m; 1 knot = 1.852 km/h; 1 fathom = 6 ft; 1 cable = 0.1 NM.
  • Water: salinity about 35 per mille, density about 1,025 kg/m³, sound about 1,500 m/s; fresh water made by evaporators or reverse osmosis.
  • Weather: Beaufort 0 to 12; monsoon June to September; cyclone seasons before and after the monsoon.
  • Tides: spring at new and full moon, neap at quarters; rule of twelfths; under-keel clearance = depth + tide − draught.
  • Safety: fire, flood, man overboard, abandon ship, action stations; contain, report, act.
