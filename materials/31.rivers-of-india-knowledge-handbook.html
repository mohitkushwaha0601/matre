Snapshot and how to read

India's rivers feed most of its farmland, hold most of its pilgrimage geography, carry its largest cities' wastewater, and cross the borders of five neighbours. Three Himalayan systems (Indus, Ganga, Brahmaputra) and a set of Peninsular rivers carry the country's surface water. Their management raises questions of federalism, ecology, treaty law and defence all at once.

Part I · The river systems and their civilisation

Divides, basins, great rivers and what they meant

River systems, divides and basins

Indian rivers fall into two broad groups. The Himalayan rivers (Indus, Ganga, Brahmaputra) are fed by both snow and rain, so they are perennial. They are old rivers that cut deep gorges through a rising range (antecedent drainage) and build huge floodplains and deltas. The Peninsular rivers depend mainly on the monsoon, so they are seasonal, flow in shallow valleys and have more stable courses.

The Western Ghats form the main water divide of the Peninsula. Short, fast rivers (Sharavati, Periyar, Bharathapuzha) drop west to the Arabian Sea, while the long rivers (Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri, Mahanadi) flow east across the plateau and form deltas. The Narmada and Tapi are exceptions: they run west through rift valleys. Rajasthan has inland drainage, including the Luni, which ends in the Rann of Kachchh, and the dry Ghaggar-Hakra channel.

Himalayan

  • Perennial: snowmelt plus monsoon
  • Long, with deep gorges and large floodplains
  • Navigation, irrigation, hydropower
  • High flood and glacial-hazard exposure

Peninsular

  • Seasonal: rain-fed
  • Shorter, with graded profiles and deltas on the east
  • Heavily dammed and irrigated
  • Dispute-prone because water is scarce relative to demand

India's average annual water resource is roughly 2,000 billion cubic metres, of which about 1,100 BCM is usable, and per-capita availability has dropped below the 1,700 m³ conventional "stress" threshold. Re-verify figures The Central Water Commission treats about a dozen river systems as major basins (catchment above 20,000 km²), and the Ganga, Godavari, Krishna and Indus (Indian portion) are among the largest by area. The Indus and Ganga systems

Indus system

The Indus rises near Mansarovar in Tibet, enters India in Ladakh and flows through Gilgit-Baltistan into Pakistan. Its Indian tributaries, from west to east, are the Jhelum (Verinag, Kashmir), the Chenab (Chandra and Bhaga meet at Tandi in Himachal), the Ravi, the Beas and the Sutlej (which enters India through Shipki La). They come together as the Panjnad before joining the Indus in Pakistan. The Siachen and other Karakoram glaciers feed the Nubra-Shyok and so the Indus. Established

Ganga system

The Ganga is formed by the Bhagirathi (from Gangotri) and Alaknanda at Devprayag, with four other prayags upstream on the Alaknanda. It enters the plains at Haridwar and is joined by the Yamuna at Prayagraj, the Ghaghara, Gandak and Kosi from the north (Nepal) and the Son from the south. Chambal, Betwa and Ken reach it through the Yamuna. Below the Farakka Barrage, the Bhagirathi-Hooghly takes a share of water toward Kolkata, while the main stream enters Bangladesh as the Padma and meets the Brahmaputra (Jamuna) and then the Meghna. Established

Why Ganga's basin dominates

The Ganga basin holds roughly a quarter of India's land and a very large share of its population, so its water quality, flows and floods shape national policy. Its delta with the Brahmaputra, including the Sundarbans (a UNESCO World Heritage Site), is the world's largest. Re-verify shares

The Brahmaputra and Barak systems

The Brahmaputra begins as the Yarlung Tsangpo in Tibet, runs east for more than a thousand kilometres, and then turns sharply around Namcha Barwa at the Great Bend. After a steep fall through the gorge it enters Arunachal Pradesh as the Siang (Dihang). It is joined by the Dibang and Lohit to become the Brahmaputra in Assam. In Bangladesh it is called the Jamuna. Established

  • Character: a very high discharge, heavy silt load and a braided course with shifting channels. Floods and bank erosion are annual events in Assam.
  • Tributaries: Subansiri, Kameng, Manas and Teesta (the last joins in Bangladesh).
  • Landmarks: Majuli (among the world's largest river islands), Kaziranga floodplains, and bridges at Dhola-Sadiya (2017) and Bogibeel (2018). Re-verify
  • Barak: rises in the Manipur hills, flows through Cachar in Assam and enters Bangladesh as the Surma and Kushiyara, which form the Meghna.
An upstream-downstream chain

China is upstream on the Yarlung Tsangpo, India is a middle riparian, and Bangladesh is the lowest. Each state tends to see the others as either threat or pressure point, so any engineering change on the main stem is read strategically. See the China section later in this handbook.

These rivers are more heavily regulated than the Himalayan ones. Almost every major Peninsular river has several large reservoirs, and in dry years the demand from farms, cities and industry exceeds the supply. That is why most of India's inter-state water disputes are Peninsular. The Godavari-Krishna-Kaveri axis also forms the proposed southern link in India's river-linking plans, discussed later.

Rivers in civilisation, religion and culture

Early cities grew on rivers. The Indus Valley Civilisation had major sites on the Indus (Mohenjo-daro), Ravi (Harappa) and the Ghaggar-Hakra (Kalibangan). The Rigveda praises the Sapta Sindhu and a river called Saraswati; many scholars link it to the Ghaggar-Hakra, but the identification is Disputed. Later, Patliputra (Patna) rose at the Ganga-Son confluence and became the Mauryan capital.

  • Sacred rivers: the tradition lists seven (Ganga, Yamuna, Godavari, Saraswati, Narmada, Sindhu, Kaveri). The Ganga is revered as a goddess, and the Narmada is circumambulated in a parikrama. Interpretation: lists vary by tradition
  • Kumbh: the four sites are Haridwar (Ganga), Prayagraj (Ganga-Yamuna-Saraswati), Nashik (Godavari) and Ujjain (Kshipra). See handbooks 27 and 30.
  • Festivals and practices: Ganga Aarti, Chhath on riverbanks, Pushkaram festivals, and immersion and cremation rites that also create pollution problems.
  • Legal personhood: in 2017 the Uttarakhand High Court declared the Ganga and Yamuna legal persons; the Supreme Court later stayed that order. Re-verify
Why culture matters for policy

Because rivers are sacred, pollution is both an environmental and a cultural-political issue. Governments can mobilise support for cleaning them, but religious practice at the water's edge also complicates enforcement.

Part II · Law, economy and the environment

Who governs water, who builds on it, and who cleans it

Water in the Constitution and law

Water is primarily a State subject: Entry 17 of the State List covers water supply, irrigation, canals, drainage, embankments, storage and water power. That entry is "subject to" Entry 56 of the Union List, which lets Parliament regulate and develop inter-state rivers and river valleys to the extent it declares by law to be expedient in the public interest. Established

  • Article 262: Parliament may provide by law for the adjudication of disputes over the waters of inter-state rivers and may bar the courts from hearing them.
  • Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956: the basis for ad hoc tribunals. A 2019 amendment bill proposed a single standing tribunal with multiple benches and time limits. Re-verify the bill's status
  • River Boards Act, 1956: allows river boards for inter-state basins, but it has hardly been used. Re-verify
  • Other laws and bodies: Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act 1974, the Environment (Protection) Act 1986, Dam Safety Act 2021, the Central Water Commission, and the Ministry of Jal Shakti (formed 2019).
  • Policy: National Water Policy (2012 version) treats water as a scarce national resource and promotes basin-level planning.
The structural tension

Rivers do not follow state boundaries, but the main powers over water sit with the states. This gap between hydrology and politics explains why disputes drag on and why the Centre's leverage is mainly through tribunals, funding and new authorities.

Irrigation, dams and hydropower

Agriculture takes the bulk of India's freshwater withdrawals, with groundwater now the largest source for irrigation. India extracts more groundwater than any other country. Re-verify shares Post-independence planning treated dams as "temples of modern India". Bhakra on the Sutlej (completed in the early 1960s) and Hirakud on the Mahanadi (1957) are early examples.

  • Tehri (Bhagirathi): among the tallest dams in India, built in the Himalaya despite seismic and displacement objections.
  • Sardar Sarovar (Narmada): filled to full reservoir level in 2017 after decades of litigation and protest by the Narmada Bachao Andolan. Re-verify
  • Polavaram (Godavari): a national project with large submergence and rehabilitation questions.
  • Hydropower: India's large hydro capacity is under 50 GW, and the largest untapped potential is in the Himalayan north-east, especially Arunachal Pradesh. Re-verify
  • Dam safety: the Dam Safety Act (2021) sets up national and state authorities to oversee safe operation.
The trade-off

Dams give storage, irrigation and power, and cut flood peaks, but they displace people, trap sediment, alter ecology and can create downstream disputes. Large Himalayan projects add seismic and glacial-lake flood risk (see the climate section).

Pollution, Namami Gange and conservation

Untreated sewage, industrial effluent, solid waste and agricultural runoff pollute most urban river stretches. The Ganga Action Plan began in 1985 and the broader National River Conservation Plan followed in 1995, but results were limited. The National Ganga River Basin Authority was set up in 2009. The Namami Gange programme, launched in 2014 under the National Mission for Clean Ganga (NMCG), gives priority to sewage treatment, riverfront development, biodiversity and the "Arth Ganga" economic model. Re-verify budgets, targets and extensions

  • Environmental flow: a 2018 notification set minimum flow norms for the Ganga, which hydropower developers and conservationists have debated. Re-verify
  • Yamuna: the stretch through Delhi is a small part of its length but contributes a large share of its pollution load. Re-verify
  • Gangetic dolphin: declared the national aquatic animal in 2009; an indicator species for river health.
  • Courts: the Supreme Court and the National Green Tribunal have issued many orders, but enforcement is uneven.
Two measurements

Infrastructure indicators (sewage treatment capacity, plants built) and ecological indicators (dissolved oxygen, biological oxygen demand, fish and dolphin counts) can tell different stories. A balanced answer cites both and notes that capacity built is not the same as capacity operating.

River linking, floods and climate

River linking

The National Perspective Plan (1980) outlined Himalayan and Peninsular link components, with the National Water Development Agency studying them. The first major project to move ahead is the Ken-Betwa Link, with a funding and sharing agreement among the Centre, Madhya Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh signed in 2021 and construction launched in December 2024. Supporters cite water for the Bundelkhand region, while critics point to the loss of forest and tiger habitat in the Panna reserve. Disputed Re-verify Large links such as those between Himalayan and Peninsular basins also raise cost, ecological and diplomatic issues.

Floods

Assam and Bihar flood almost every monsoon. The Kosi is called the "sorrow of Bihar", and its 2008 embankment breach in Nepal displaced millions. Flood forecasting by the Central Water Commission, embankments, and basin-level cooperation with Nepal and China are the main tools. Re-verify

Climate and glaciers

Himalayan glaciers feed the Indus, Ganga and Brahmaputra, so warming changes seasonal flows and raises the risk of glacial-lake outburst floods. Examples include the 2013 Kedarnath disaster, the Chamoli flood in February 2021 and the October 2023 South Lhonak lake flood in Sikkim that destroyed the Teesta-III dam. Re-verify details Interpretation: long-term effect on river flows is debated; many studies expect higher flows first and then a decline

Part III · Transboundary and strategic dimensions

Treaties, upstream power and rivers in uniform

The Indus Waters Treaty

The Indus Waters Treaty (IWT) was signed in Karachi on 19 September 1960 by Prime Minister Nehru and President Ayub Khan, with the World Bank as a party to certain provisions. It divided the basin: the three eastern rivers (Ravi, Beas, Sutlej) go to India for unrestricted use, and the three western rivers (Indus, Jhelum, Chenab) go to Pakistan, with India allowed limited uses such as run-of-river hydropower, domestic use, limited irrigation and capped storage. Established

  • Machinery: a Permanent Indus Commission with a commissioner from each side. Disputes move in steps: a "question" to the Commission, a "difference" to a Neutral Expert, and a "dispute" to a Court of Arbitration.
  • Past disputes: Baglihar (Neutral Expert, 2007) and Kishanganga (Court of Arbitration, 2013). Both sides also argue over Ratle and other design features. Re-verify
  • Pressure on the treaty: India issued notices seeking modification and review in 2023 and 2024, citing changed circumstances and Pakistan's conduct. Re-verify
  • April 2025: after the Pahalgam attack (22 April), India announced that the treaty was being held "in abeyance". Pakistan rejected this and called any stoppage of water an act of war. A Court of Arbitration continued proceedings, which India does not accept. Disputed Re-verify the legal position and current status
What the abeyance can and cannot do

India has little large storage on the western rivers, so the immediate effect on flows is limited and mainly operational: reservoir flushing, data sharing and design reviews. Over time, new storage and canal projects (for instance on the Chenab, and proposals to move water toward Punjab, Haryana and Rajasthan) could change the picture, but they need years and large investment. Interpretation Re-verify project status

The treaty survived the wars of 1965 and 1971 and the 1999 Kargil conflict, and was often called one of the world's more durable water agreements. Pakistan relies on the Indus system for most of its irrigated agriculture, so it treats any change as an existential concern. See handbook 7 for the wider India-Pakistan context.

The Brahmaputra and China

China is the upstream state on the Brahmaputra and Sutlej. There is no water-sharing treaty. The two countries have an Expert Level Mechanism (set up in 2006) and used to share flood-season hydrological data under periodic memoranda, which have lapsed or been interrupted in recent years. Re-verify the data-sharing status

  • Chinese projects: Zangmu (a run-of-river dam in Tibet) is operating. In July 2025, China formally began construction of a very large hydropower scheme on the lower Yarlung Tsangpo near the Great Bend, near Medog. Reported capacity and cost are very large. Re-verify capacity and cost figures
  • Indian concerns: flow regulation, sediment, flood warning data, and a strategic lever near a disputed border. Arunachal Pradesh is claimed by China, which adds a territorial dimension.
  • Chinese position: projects are run-of-river, do not reduce downstream flow, and serve clean energy goals. Interpretation: Indian analysts doubt that a huge scheme leaves flows entirely unaffected
  • Indian response: the proposed Siang Upper Multipurpose Project in Arunachal, intended to store water and generate power, which has met local opposition; and efforts to restore regular data sharing. Re-verify
An asymmetry

China is not bound by a treaty and holds the head of the river, while India holds the head of the Indus tributaries and is a lower riparian on the Brahmaputra. Critics of India's 2025 treaty move note this reverses the position India says it fears from China. Supporters reply that the two cases differ legally and politically. Interpretation See handbook 6 for the LAC context.

Rivers and military affairs

  • Rivers as obstacles: the Sutlej, Beas, Ravi and Brahmaputra with its tributaries are major obstacles to movement. In the Punjab sector, canals such as the Ichhogil (Lahore) and the BRB form defensive lines, and bridging, crossing and engineer support are decisive in any plains offensive.
  • Logistics: the Brahmaputra splits Assam and the Northeast. The Dhola-Sadiya and Bogibeel bridges improved movement toward eastern Arunachal. Roads and bridges built by the Border Roads Organisation and the Army's Corps of Engineers support forward areas. Re-verify
  • Inland waterways: National Waterway 1 (Ganga) and National Waterway 2 (Brahmaputra) matter for bulk freight and, potentially, for civil-military movement; the Indo-Bangladesh Protocol route links Kolkata and Assam. Re-verify
  • Relief: the Army, Air Force, Navy and NDRF respond to floods (Kashmir 2014, Uttarakhand 2013, Assam each year). Humanitarian assistance and disaster relief is a core peacetime role.
  • Riverine forces: the BSF Water Wing and Army engineers operate in delta and riverine terrain, including the Sundarbans. Re-verify unit details
  • Dams and law of war: Additional Protocol I to the Geneva Conventions (Article 56) protects dams and dykes from attack when this would cause severe civilian losses. India has not ratified that protocol. Re-verify
Water as a strategic instrument

Control of rivers is not just a military or legal matter. Data, storage, treaty design and diplomacy are the main levers, and the risk that water becomes an instrument of coercion shapes both Indian and neighbouring planning. Interpretation

Analysis, practice and revision

Perspectives, controversies and facts

Interpretation Dams: development or damage. Supporters point to irrigation, power and flood control; critics point to displacement, sediment loss, seismic risk and ecology. The Narmada and Tehri debates show both sides.

Interpretation River linking. Advocates see a way to move water from surplus to deficit basins; sceptics argue demand management, groundwater recharge and efficiency are cheaper and less risky. Disputed

Interpretation The Indus abeyance. One view holds that the treaty gave Pakistan a generous share and could not survive sustained terrorism. Another holds that unilateral suspension weakens India's case as an upstream state elsewhere, and invites China to do the same on the Brahmaputra. A balanced answer sets out the legal, strategic and reputational costs of each position.

Interpretation Cleaning the Ganga. Large sums have been spent and treatment capacity has grown, yet stretches remain heavily polluted. Disagreement centres on measurement, operation and maintenance, and environmental flow rather than on intent.

Lesser-known facts

  • Established The Grand Anicut (Kallanai) on the Kaveri, built in the Chola period, is still in use.
  • Established The Ganga was declared India's National River in 2008 and the Gangetic dolphin the national aquatic animal in 2009.
  • Established The Brahmaputra's Majuli is among the world's largest river islands and became India's first river-island district in 2016.
  • Established Three of the four Kumbh sites (Haridwar, Prayagraj, Nashik) lie on the Ganga, Yamuna or Godavari and the fourth, Ujjain, lies on the Kshipra.

Scenarios and strategic options

Presented neutrally, without recommending a political position:

Indus abeyance continues

India builds storage and diversions on the western rivers over many years, while Pakistan pursues legal, diplomatic and international routes. Risk of escalation is tied to the wider security relationship.

Upstream squeeze on the Brahmaputra

Large Chinese storage or diversion would affect flows and flood warning. India would need data agreements, its own storage (Siang) and diplomatic leverage.

Ganga treaty renewal

Renewal or renegotiation of the 1996 treaty with Bangladesh ties together water sharing, Teesta, security of the Siliguri corridor and regional politics.

Domestic water stress

Groundwater depletion, urban demand and a warming climate make allocation disputes between states more frequent and harder to settle.

Answer framework and worked example

For any "Why do rivers matter for India's security?" question, move through definition, location, evidence, contest, analysis, test, position, concession and close. One compressed example follows.

Worked example: "Does India's decision to hold the Indus Waters Treaty in abeyance serve its interests?"

Define
The IWT is a 1960 treaty allocating the three western rivers to Pakistan and three eastern rivers to India; abeyance is India's April 2025 suspension of its obligations.
Locate
Indus basin across J&K, Punjab and Pakistan; Chenab and Jhelum projects in J&K.
Establish
India announced it after the Pahalgam attack; Pakistan rejected the move; India has little storage on the western rivers.
Contest
Critics say it is legally contested, has limited immediate effect and sets a precedent China can invoke.
Analyse
Value lies in leverage, design flexibility and future projects, not in cutting flows soon.
Test
Compare effects on Pakistan, on Indian projects and on India's upstream-downstream positions.
Position
A pressure instrument with real long-term options, but with costs that must be managed.
Concede
Legal positions and project timelines are uncertain. Re-verify
Close
Water policy needs to be paired with storage, diplomacy and credible rules for upstream and downstream behaviour.

Rapid revision sheet

Systems and culture

  • Himalayan: perennial, antecedent, deltas. Peninsular: seasonal, mostly east-flowing; Narmada and Tapi flow west.
  • Indus: Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas, Sutlej. Ganga: Yamuna, Ghaghara, Gandak, Kosi, Son. Brahmaputra: Siang, Dibang, Lohit, Subansiri.
  • Kumbh sites: Haridwar, Prayagraj, Nashik, Ujjain; the Ganga is the National River (2008).

Law and economy

  • State List Entry 17; Union List Entry 56; Article 262; 1956 Acts; Dam Safety Act 2021.
  • Disputes: Kaveri, Krishna, Godavari, Ravi-Beas (SYL), Mahadayi, Narmada.
  • Namami Gange (2014); Ken-Betwa Link; Sardar Sarovar, Tehri, Polavaram, Bhakra, Hirakud.

Strategic

  • IWT 1960: east to India, west to Pakistan; in abeyance since April 2025; legal position disputed.
  • China: Medog scheme, Zangmu, no treaty, data-sharing lapses; India's Siang project.
  • Bangladesh: Ganga treaty (1996) due for renewal in 2026; Teesta unresolved. Nepal: Kosi, Gandak, Mahakali. Bhutan: hydropower.
  • Rivers as obstacles, logistics routes and relief theatres; always tag uncertain figures for re-verification.
