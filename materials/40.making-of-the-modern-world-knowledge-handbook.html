The Making of the Modern World, 1500-2025

Five centuries compressed into the developments that actually shaped the present: how oceanic trade linked the continents, how industry and the nation-state gave a small part of Europe two centuries of advantage, how that advantage was lost between 1914 and 1960, how the 1945 institutions and the Cold War organised the world that followed, and how economic weight has moved back towards Asia since 1978. Structure and causation first; contested figures and interpretations are tagged rather than asserted.

Part I · The first global age, 1500-1800

How the continents were joined into one economy

Snapshot: five centuries in outline

The modern world was made by four linked processes: the joining of the continents into a single economy after 1500, the industrialisation of a small part of Europe after 1750, the spread of the nation-state as the only legitimate political unit, and the organised ideological contest over how economies should be owned. European dominance was the result of these processes, not their cause; it peaked around 1914 and was effectively over by 1960.

Four questions that organise everything

  • Why did Europe, not Asia, dominate after 1750? Not because it was richer in 1500, but because industrialisation, state competition and access to colonial resources compounded a small lead into a large one.
  • Why did that dominance end so fast? Because two European civil wars, 1914-18 and 1939-45, destroyed the capital, manpower and legitimacy that empire required.
  • What replaced it? A rules-and-institutions order designed by the United States in 1944-49, run inside a bipolar military confrontation until 1991.
  • Where is it going? Towards a world where economic weight is distributed roughly as it was in 1750, but inside institutions written in 1945. That mismatch is the core of today's politics.

The world in 1500

In 1500 Europe was a peninsula on the edge of a world whose centres of wealth and population lay in Asia. China under the Ming and India under the emerging Mughals together produced perhaps half of world output; the Ottoman Empire sat astride the land routes between them and the Mediterranean. Europe's advantages were not size or wealth but ocean-going ships, cheap cannon, competitive state-building and, crucially, a motive: the Ottoman and Venetian hold on the eastern trade made an Atlantic route attractive.

The point that matters

Nothing about 1500 predicted European dominance. The question historians argue over is not why Europe was ahead — it was not — but why a modest lead in shipping, guns and state competition turned into an overwhelming one three centuries later. Interpretation

Oceans, empires and the first global economy

Between 1492 and 1571 the world's trading systems were stitched into one. Columbus reached the Caribbean in 1492, Vasco da Gama reached Calicut in 1498, and Magellan's expedition circumnavigated the globe in 1519-22. The decisive year is arguably 1571, when the Manila galleon began carrying American silver directly to China in exchange for silk and porcelain, closing the loop. For the first time a harvest failure or a currency change on one continent could move prices on another.

  • 1492Columbus reaches the Caribbean; Spain begins conquest of the Americas.
  • 1498Da Gama reaches India; Portuguese armed trading posts from Goa to Malacca.
  • 1519-22Magellan-Elcano circumnavigation proves a single connected ocean.
  • 1571Manila galleon links American silver to Chinese manufactures.
  • 1600 / 1602English East India Company and Dutch VOC chartered: private companies with the power to wage war.
  • 1757Plassey: the EIC becomes a territorial power in Bengal, turning trade into rule.

The Columbian exchange

  • To the Old World: potato, maize, cassava, tomato, chilli, tobacco, rubber, quinine. Potato and maize raised European and Chinese population ceilings substantially.
  • To the Americas: wheat, rice, sugar cane, horses, cattle — and smallpox, measles and influenza.
  • Demographic catastrophe: indigenous American populations fell by an estimated 50-90 per cent within a century, overwhelmingly from disease. Disputed

Mercantilism and the chartered company

  • States treated trade as zero-sum: maximise exports, hoard bullion, monopolise colonial commerce.
  • Chartered companies — the VOC, the EIC — held monopolies plus the right to raise armies, coin money and make treaties.
  • This blurred the line between commerce and conquest, and explains how a trading company ended up governing India. Interpretation
The silver loop

Roughly 150,000 tonnes of silver were mined in Spanish America, above all at Potosí, and a large share of it ended in China, where silver was the fiscal base. European traders had little else Asia wanted: for three centuries the Atlantic economy bought Asian goods with American metal. That dependency, not European manufacturing superiority, defined early modern trade. Re-verify

Slavery, plantations and abolition

The plantation complex of the Atlantic was the first mass-production system of the modern age, and it ran on forced labour. Roughly 12.5 million Africans were embarked on slave ships between the sixteenth century and the 1860s; about 10.7 million survived the crossing. Established

Abolition, and what replaced it

  • 1791-1804Haitian Revolution: the only successful large-scale slave revolt, producing the second republic in the Americas.
  • 1807 / 1833Britain abolishes the slave trade, then slavery in most of its empire; the Royal Navy's West Africa Squadron enforces suppression.
  • 1848France abolishes slavery in its colonies for the second and final time.
  • 1861-65American Civil War; the Thirteenth Amendment ends slavery in the United States.
  • 1888Brazil's Lei Áurea: the last major abolition in the Americas.
  • 1834-1917Indian indentured emigration — roughly 1.3 million people to Mauritius, the Caribbean, Natal, Fiji and Malaya — supplies plantation labour after abolition.

The argument about slavery and industry

  • Eric Williams (1944) argued that slave-trade and plantation profits financed British industrialisation, and that abolition came when slavery ceased to be economically central. Disputed
  • Critics reply that the quantitative contribution to British capital formation was modest, and that abolition was driven substantially by religious and popular campaigning against economic interest.
  • A defensible middle position: the Atlantic system was not the sole cause of industrialisation, but it supplied cheap raw cotton, protected markets and commercial institutions that mattered at the margin. Interpretation

Part II · Revolution, industry and empire, 1750-1914

Where the modern state and the modern economy came from

Revolutions, nationalism and the nation-state

Between 1776 and 1871 the basic political unit of the world changed from the dynastic realm, where people were subjects of a ruler, to the nation-state, where legitimacy came from a people. The Enlightenment supplied the argument — sovereignty rests with the governed — and a chain of revolutions turned it into institutions: written constitutions, citizenship, conscript armies, national education and national markets.

  • 1776-83American Revolution: the first colonial state to break away and write a republican constitution.
  • 1789-99French Revolution: the Declaration of the Rights of Man, the end of legal privilege, the levée en masse, and the Terror.
  • 1804-15Napoleon spreads the Civil Code and the administrative state across Europe by conquest; the Congress of Vienna (1815) restores monarchy but not the old order.
  • 1810-25Latin American independence under Bolívar and San Martín dissolves the Spanish American empire.
  • 1848Revolutions across Europe: liberal and national demands defeated almost everywhere, but their programme becomes the agenda of the next generation.
  • 1861-71Italian and German unification; the United States settles its own union question by civil war; Japan's Meiji Restoration (1868) begins defensive modernisation.

Two kinds of nationalism

Civic: the nation as everyone who accepts a common constitution and citizenship — the French and American template. Ethnic: the nation as a people defined by language, descent or religion — the template that drove German and Italian unification, and then the break-up of the Habsburg and Ottoman empires. Most real nationalisms mix the two, and which element dominates largely determines how a state treats its minorities. Interpretation

Why it matters for the rest of the story

Nationalism built states in Europe, pulled empires apart in the Balkans, supplied the mobilising energy of 1914, and was then taken up by colonised peoples who used the coloniser's own principle — self-determination — against him. Anti-colonial nationalism from Dublin to Delhi is the same idea turned around.

The Industrial Revolution and the great divergence

Industrialisation is the single largest break in human economic history: for the first time output per person grew faster than population, continuously, for generations. It began in Britain around 1760-80 and it is the proximate reason a handful of European states could dominate the rest of the world by 1900.

Why Britain first

  • Cheap coal beside water transport, which made steam power economic earlier than anywhere else.
  • High wages and cheap energy, which made labour-saving machinery worth inventing.
  • Agricultural productivity that released labour to towns.
  • Institutions: secure property, patents, a national market without internal tariffs, and a state that could borrow cheaply.
  • Empire and the Atlantic economy: raw cotton, captive markets and commercial finance. Weight contested. Disputed

The great divergence

The consequence was a gap between industrial and non-industrial economies that had not existed before. On Bairoch's and Maddison's widely used reconstructions, India and China together accounted for roughly half of world manufacturing output in 1750 and for a small fraction of it by 1900, while Britain's share rose steeply. India's position reflected both the collapse of its handloom export trade in the face of machine-made cloth and the tariff and revenue policy of colonial rule. Both the figures and the weight given to each cause are argued over. Disputed

What industrialisation changed besides output

Urbanisation and a working class; the factory clock and wage labour; mass literacy because industry needed it; the modern army, since railways and steel decided wars; and a permanent connection between economic capacity and military power that still determines which states matter.

Capitalism, socialism and the communist idea

Industrial society produced both a new economic system and an organised argument against it. Capitalism — private ownership of productive assets, wage labour, competitive markets, profit reinvested as capital — was described and defended by Adam Smith in The Wealth of Nations (1776). Within two generations its costs in the industrial towns had produced socialism, and by 1848 Marx and Engels had turned that into a theory of history in which class conflict drives change and capitalism ends in revolution.

Three distinctions worth keeping straight

  • Socialism is a family of positions about social ownership and equality; communism in practice means the Marxist-Leninist one-party state; social democracy accepts markets and seeks to redistribute their output through democratic politics.
  • Marx predicted revolution in the most industrialised countries. It happened in agrarian Russia and China instead — the central empirical problem for his theory. Interpretation
  • Fascism defined itself against both liberalism and communism, which is why "left" and "right" labels explain less about the 1930s than questions about who owns what and who may rule.

High imperialism and the fall of the land empires

Between 1870 and 1914 industrial states partitioned most of the world that was not already claimed. The Berlin Conference of 1884-85 set the rules for the scramble for Africa; by 1914 only Ethiopia and Liberia remained independent on the continent. At the same time the great land empires that had dominated Asia for centuries — Mughal, Ottoman, Qing — either fell or were reduced to dependence.

  • 1839-42, 1856-60Opium Wars force China open; the beginning of what Chinese historiography calls the century of humiliation.
  • 1857-58The Indian uprising ends Company rule and the Mughal title; India passes to the British Crown.
  • 1869Suez Canal opens, shortening Europe's route to Asia and making Egypt strategically decisive.
  • 1884-85Berlin Conference regulates the partition of Africa among European powers.
  • 1895 / 1905Japan defeats China, then Russia — the first modern defeat of a European power by an Asian state.
  • 1898Spanish-American War: Spain's remaining empire passes largely to the United States.
  • 1911-12 / 1922The Qing dynasty falls; a decade later the Ottoman sultanate is abolished and Turkey becomes a republic.

Why they did it

  • Economic: raw materials, protected markets and outlets for investment — the Hobson-Lenin explanation.
  • Strategic: coaling stations, canals and buffer zones; much of Africa was taken to deny it to a rival rather than for its own value. Interpretation
  • Technological: steamships, quinine, the telegraph and the machine gun made conquest cheap.
  • Ideological: the "civilising mission" and racial theory supplied the justification.

What it did to the colonised

  • Economies reoriented to export single commodities, with manufacturing displaced by imports.
  • Borders drawn for administrative convenience, which later states inherited.
  • Recurrent famines under export-oriented and revenue-first policy, in India and elsewhere.
  • The "drain of wealth" argument advanced by Dadabhai Naoroji and R. C. Dutt, against claims that railways, law and administration were compensating gains. Both the accounting and the counterfactual are contested. Disputed

Part III · Collapse and reconstruction, 1914-1991

How Europe lost the world and two superpowers divided it

The thirty-one years' crisis, 1914-1945

The two world wars and the Depression between them are best read as one connected crisis: the first war destroyed the political and economic order of the nineteenth century, the settlement failed to replace it, and the second war finished what the first had begun. Handbooks 38 and 39 cover each war in detail; what follows is only what the five-century story requires.

  • 1914-18First World War: four empires — German, Austro-Hungarian, Russian and Ottoman — cease to exist.
  • 1917Russian Revolution brings the first communist state to power, creating a permanent ideological division in world politics.
  • 1919-20Versailles and the League of Nations: a settlement without American participation or enforcement power.
  • 1929-33The Great Depression collapses world trade, discredits liberal governments and gives radical movements a mass base.
  • 1931-39Japan in Manchuria, Italy in Abyssinia, Germany in the Rhineland, Austria and Czechoslovakia: aggression goes unpunished.
  • 1939-45Second World War; roughly 70-85 million dead, a majority of them civilians. Disputed
  • 1945Nuclear weapons used; the United Nations founded; Europe ceases to be the centre of world politics.
What the crisis settled

Three things, permanently. European empires lost the money, manpower and moral claim needed to hold their colonies. The United States abandoned isolation and chose to build and underwrite an international order. And the Soviet Union emerged with an army in the centre of Europe and a competing model of modernity — which is the Cold War in one sentence.

Building the post-1945 order

The institutions of 1944-49 were designed as explicit corrections of the failures of 1919-39. The League had lacked enforcement, so the United Nations gave the five principal victors permanent seats and a veto. The 1930s had been ruined by competitive devaluation and tariff walls, so Bretton Woods created fixed exchange rates, the IMF and the World Bank. The Holocaust and total war produced a body of codified law on genocide, human rights and the conduct of hostilities.

The 1945 bargain, and why it is contested

Permanent seats and vetoes bought the great powers' participation at the price of paralysis whenever they disagree. The membership reflects the distribution of power in 1945, not 2025: no permanent seat for India, Africa, Latin America or Japan. Reform proposals have been debated since the 1990s without agreement, because enlarging the council requires the consent of those whose relative weight it would reduce. Interpretation

Decolonisation and the new states

In 1945 roughly a third of the world's people lived in territories governed by others. By 1975 almost all of them lived in sovereign states. The UN had 51 members at its founding and 193 today; most of that growth is decolonisation.

Why it happened when it did

  • Europe was bankrupt and exhausted after 1945; holding colonies had become unaffordable.
  • Mass nationalist parties with organised leaderships had been built in the interwar decades.
  • Japan's victories in 1942 had destroyed the assumption that European rule was permanent.
  • Both superpowers opposed old-style colonialism, for different reasons; the Atlantic Charter's language of self-determination was used against its authors.

What the new states inherited

  • Borders drawn by outsiders, cutting across ethnic and linguistic lines.
  • Economies specialised in one or two export commodities.
  • Partition violence and refugee movements in India, Palestine, Cyprus and elsewhere.
  • Weak institutions, which in many states produced one-party rule or military government within a decade. Interpretation

The Cold War, 1947-1991

A global confrontation between the United States and the Soviet Union that never became direct war between them, because nuclear weapons made that unaffordable. It was fought instead through alliances, proxy wars in the decolonising world, economic blocs, intelligence services, ideological competition and a technology race. Its structure — two armed camps and a large non-aligned remainder — organised almost every regional conflict for forty years.

  • 1947-49Truman Doctrine and Marshall Plan; Berlin blockade and airlift; NATO founded; the Soviet bomb; the Chinese Communist victory.
  • 1950-53Korean War: the first hot proxy conflict, ending in a stalemate still unresolved.
  • 1955-62Warsaw Pact; Hungary and Suez in 1956; Sputnik 1957; Berlin Wall 1961; the Cuban missile crisis of 1962 brings the closest approach to nuclear war.
  • 1963-79Détente: the Partial Test Ban 1963, the NPT 1968, SALT I 1972, Nixon's opening to China 1972, the Helsinki Accords 1975 — alongside Vietnam, which the United States left in 1973-75.
  • 1979-85Second Cold War: the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan, the Iranian revolution, Euromissiles, and a sharp American military build-up.
  • 1985-91Gorbachev's glasnost and perestroika; the revolutions of 1989 and the fall of the Berlin Wall; German reunification 1990; the Soviet Union dissolves in December 1991.

How it actually worked

  • Nuclear deterrence. Mutually assured destruction made general war irrational and pushed competition into the periphery.
  • Proxy wars. Korea, Vietnam, Angola, the Horn of Africa, Central America and Afghanistan were local conflicts fought with superpower money and weapons.
  • Blocs and economics. NATO and the Marshall Plan against the Warsaw Pact and Comecon; two separate trading systems.
  • The non-aligned. Bandung 1955 and the Non-Aligned Movement from 1961 gave new states room to bargain with both sides rather than join either.
Why it ended — four explanations in contention

Soviet economic failure and the cost of matching American military spending; Western pressure and the arms build-up of the early 1980s; Gorbachev's own decisions, above all the refusal to use force in Eastern Europe in 1989; and societal pressure from within the bloc, from Solidarity in Poland to the Helsinki human-rights networks. Most accounts combine them; the disagreement is over which was decisive. Disputed

Communism in practice, and why it collapsed

Communist states shared a model: a single party holding a monopoly of political power, state ownership of industry, collectivised or state-directed agriculture, and central planning of output targets. It delivered rapid heavy industrialisation and large gains in literacy and basic health from a low base, at the cost of political freedom, consumer welfare and, in several cases, mass death from forced collectivisation and famine.

The comparison that explains the outcome

The Soviet Union liberalised politics while leaving a planned economy in place, and lost both. China liberalised the economy while leaving the political monopoly in place, and kept both. Whether the Chinese sequence is a general lesson or a product of specific conditions — a rural surplus population, overseas Chinese capital, and access to American markets — is one of the live arguments in political economy. Interpretation

Post-war capitalism: boom, crisis and the market turn

Western capitalism after 1945 was not the capitalism of 1900. Fixed exchange rates, capital controls, strong trade unions, high taxation and expanded welfare states produced roughly three decades of historically exceptional growth — the "golden age" of 1945-73, which included the West German and Japanese recoveries. It broke down in the 1970s, and what replaced it set the terms of the next forty years.

  • 1944-71Bretton Woods system of fixed rates anchored to a dollar convertible into gold.
  • 1971Nixon suspends dollar-gold convertibility; currencies float, and global finance expands.
  • 1973, 1979Oil shocks following the Arab-Israeli war and the Iranian revolution; stagflation discredits the post-war policy consensus.
  • 1978-91The market turn: Deng's reforms in China 1978, Thatcher in Britain 1979, Reagan in the United States 1981, and India's liberalisation in 1991.
  • 1995-2001WTO founded; China's accession in 2001 brings several hundred million workers into the world trading system.
  • 2008Global financial crisis ends the period's confidence and begins the return of industrial policy and state intervention.

Two trends that are often confused

  • Between countries, inequality fell sharply after 1990, driven almost entirely by growth in China and later India; extreme poverty worldwide fell faster than in any previous period.
  • Within countries, inequality rose in most rich economies and in China, concentrating gains at the top and stagnating middle incomes in parts of the West.
  • That combination — global convergence with domestic divergence — is the economic root of the political backlash against globalisation after 2008. Interpretation

Part IV · The world we have, 1991-2025

One pole, then several

Globalisation and the American moment

For roughly two decades after 1991 the United States had no military or ideological peer, and the integration of markets, supply chains and information moved faster than at any time since before 1914. The period produced the largest reduction in absolute poverty in history and, simultaneously, the conditions for its own political reversal.

  • 1991The Soviet Union dissolves; the Gulf War is fought by a broad UN-authorised coalition; India begins liberalisation.
  • 1992-99Yugoslav wars and the Rwandan genocide expose the limits of collective action; NATO enlarges eastwards.
  • 1995-2001WTO established; the internet becomes commercial; China joins the WTO in 2001.
  • 2001The 11 September attacks turn American strategy towards counter-terrorism; wars in Afghanistan from 2001 and Iraq from 2003.
  • 2008Financial crisis in the United States and Europe; China's relative standing rises sharply; the G20 becomes the crisis forum.

What drove integration

  • Containerisation and cheap shipping; just-in-time manufacturing split production across borders.
  • Capital account liberalisation and floating currencies.
  • The internet, which cut the cost of coordinating work across time zones to near zero.
  • A single set of trade rules that most of the world had joined by 2001.

What it cost, and where

  • Manufacturing employment fell in parts of the United States and Europe faster than new work appeared.
  • The 1997 Asian financial crisis showed how quickly capital flows could reverse on developing economies.
  • Supply chains optimised for cost proved fragile under shock, as 2020-22 demonstrated.
  • The assumption that economic integration would produce political convergence was not borne out. Interpretation

The return of Asia: China and India

The most consequential development of the last half-century is the recovery of Asia's share of world output towards something like its pre-industrial level. Two states account for most of it, by different routes: China through state-directed export manufacturing, India through a later and more services-led opening.

The present order and its stresses

The world of 2025 has multipolar economics, a sharply bipolar technology competition, and institutions designed for 1945. None of those three is adjusting quickly to the others, which is why the period feels unsettled without being a return to the Cold War.

The structural summary

Economic weight is returning to roughly its distribution of 1750, but inside a political and institutional structure written in 1945 by the winners of a European war. Every major dispute of the present — Security Council reform, trade rules, climate finance, technology standards — is a version of the same mismatch. Interpretation

Perspectives, controversies and facts

Interpretation Why did the West rise? One school credits institutions — property rights, representative government, competitive states, scientific method. A second credits geography and contingency: coal in the right place and the resources of the Americas, which Kenneth Pomeranz called the "ghost acreage" that let Britain escape a land constraint China could not. A third argues that the question itself is badly posed, since the divergence was late, narrow and reversible — as the last fifty years suggest. Disputed

Interpretation Was colonial rule economically net negative? The drain and de-industrialisation case points to transferred revenue, destroyed handloom exports, famines and tariff policy written for the metropole. The opposing case cites railways, irrigation, legal and administrative systems and integration into world markets. The honest answer separates what was built from who paid for it and who decided, and notes that the counterfactual — what India's own trajectory would have been — cannot be observed. Disputed

Interpretation Was the Cold War avoidable? The orthodox view blames Soviet expansion; the revisionist view blames American economic and nuclear assertiveness; the post-revisionist synthesis emphasises mutual misperception between two states with incompatible models and armies facing each other in central Europe. Disputed

Interpretation Did liberal democracy win in 1991? Francis Fukuyama's "end of history" argument held that no rival model remained. Thirty years later, competitive authoritarian states have delivered growth, democratic backsliding has occurred in established democracies, and the claim looks at best premature. Its defenders reply that no coherent universal alternative has yet been articulated either. Disputed

Facts worth holding

  • Established Chinese fleets under Zheng He reached East Africa in 1405-33, decades before Portuguese voyages; the Ming court then ended state voyaging as a matter of policy.
  • Established Haiti, founded in 1804 by a successful slave revolt, was required to pay France an indemnity for the loss of its "property", and finished servicing the resulting debt only in the twentieth century.
  • Established The United Nations had 51 members in 1945 and has 193 today; the growth is almost entirely decolonisation.
  • Established Seventeen African states became independent in 1960 alone.
  • Established The Bretton Woods system was negotiated in July 1944, while the war was still being fought — the post-war economic order predates the victory.
  • Established Japan was the world's second-largest economy from 1968 until China overtook it in 2010.

Counterfactuals, lessons and India's options

Counterfactuals test whether a stated cause was necessary. Change one variable, hold the rest, and state what it reveals.

If the Americas had never been colonised

No silver to pay for Asian goods, no plantation cotton, no land relief for European population pressure. Tests Pomeranz's claim that colonial resources, not European institutions alone, made the industrial escape possible.

If the Ming had continued ocean voyaging after 1433

Chinese fleets were larger and earlier than Portuguese ones. Tests whether European expansion reflected unique capability or simply the absence of competition, and how much state policy choices shape long-run outcomes.

If the United States had joined the League of Nations in 1920

Enforcement might have existed where it did not. Tests whether the interwar collapse was caused by the terms of the 1919 settlement or by the absence of a power willing to uphold it.

If the USSR had reformed its economy before its politics

The Chinese sequence applied in Moscow. Tests whether the Soviet collapse was structurally inevitable or a consequence of the order in which reforms were attempted.

What India takes from this history

Presented neutrally: manufacturing and energy capacity, not only diplomacy, determine a state's weight; technological dependence in one era becomes political dependence in the next; multi-alignment is a recognisable descendant of the non-aligned position of 1961, adapted to a world with more poles; and the case for institutional reform rests on the same mismatch between 1945 structures and present capability that the rest of the Global South also invokes.

Answer framework and worked example

For any long-run causation question, move through definition, location, evidence, contest, analysis, test, position, concession and close. One compressed example follows; use the same ladder for "Was colonialism's economic impact negative?" or "Why did the Cold War end peacefully?".

Worked example: Why did Europe industrialise first?

Define
Industrialisation means sustained growth in output per person driven by mechanised production and fossil energy, beginning in Britain around 1760-80.
Locate
Britain first, then Belgium, France, Germany and the United States; Japan from the 1870s; most of Asia and Africa only after 1950.
Establish
In 1750 China and India were comparable to Western Europe in living standards and ahead in total manufacturing output. The divergence is therefore late and requires explanation, not assumption. Disputed
Contest
Institutionalists stress property rights, patents, competitive states and scientific culture. Resource-and-contingency accounts stress accessible coal and colonial land and raw materials. A third view emphasises high British wages making machines worth building.
Analyse
These are complements, not rivals: cheap coal made steam economic, high wages made mechanisation profitable, colonies supplied cotton and markets, and institutions let capital be raised and invention rewarded. Remove any one and the escape is slower.
Test
If institutions alone sufficed, the Dutch Republic should have industrialised first; it had the institutions and the finance but lacked coal. If colonies alone sufficed, Spain and Portugal should have led. Neither did.
Position
Britain industrialised first because a specific combination — coal next to water transport, expensive labour, Atlantic raw materials and markets, and institutions that financed and protected invention — existed in one place at one time.
Concede
The relative weight of colonial resources against domestic institutions is genuinely unresolved, and the quantitative estimates behind both cases carry wide error bars.
Close
The useful conclusion is that the divergence was contingent rather than civilisational, which is why it has proved reversible.

Eight debate motions

  1. European dominance after 1750 was the result of contingency rather than superiority.
  2. The economic record of colonial rule was unambiguously negative for the colonised.
  3. The Industrial Revolution did more to shape the modern world than any war.
  4. Nationalism has done more harm than good since 1789.
  5. The Cold War was made inevitable by the outcome of the Second World War.
  6. Globalisation after 1991 benefited the world's poor more than it harmed the West's workers.
  7. The United Nations Security Council in its present form does more harm than good.
  8. The twenty-first century will be shaped more by technology than by territory.

Glossary

Mercantilism
Early modern doctrine treating trade as zero-sum, to be managed for bullion and national advantage.
Columbian exchange
The transfer of crops, animals, people and diseases between the hemispheres after 1492.
Chartered company
A private trading company granted monopoly rights and sovereign powers, such as the EIC and the VOC.
Triangular trade
The Atlantic circuit of manufactures to Africa, enslaved people to the Americas, and plantation produce to Europe.
Indenture
Contract labour migration, used extensively from India after abolition, 1834-1917.
Great divergence
The gap in income and industrial output that opened between Western Europe and Asia after about 1800.
Nation-state
A sovereign state whose legitimacy rests on representing a people rather than a dynasty.
Self-determination
The principle that peoples choose their own sovereignty; stated in 1918 and 1941, and used against empires thereafter.
Capitalism
An economic system based on private ownership of capital, wage labour and competitive markets.
Communism (Marxist-Leninist)
State ownership and central planning under a single-party monopoly of political power.
Social democracy
Market economy with high taxation, regulation and universal public services, pursued through electoral politics.
Imperialism
Control, formal or informal, by one state over the political and economic life of another.
Drain of wealth
Naoroji's argument that colonial India transferred resources to Britain without return.
Bretton Woods
The 1944 agreement creating fixed exchange rates, the IMF and the World Bank.
Cold War
The US-Soviet confrontation of 1947-91, conducted through blocs, proxies and deterrence rather than direct war.
Non-alignment
The refusal of newly independent states to join either Cold War bloc; formalised in 1961.
Globalisation
The integration of markets, production and information across borders, most intense between 1990 and 2008.
Multipolarity
A system with several centres of comparable power rather than one or two.
De-risking / friend-shoring
Reorganising supply chains towards politically aligned states, accepting higher cost for lower exposure.

Rapid revision sheet

Eras and hinges

  • 1500-1750 first global age · 1750-1870 revolution and industry · 1870-1914 high imperialism · 1914-1991 crisis and Cold War · 1991-2025 globalisation and after.
  • Hinge years: 1492, 1571, 1757, 1789, 1870, 1914, 1945, 1978, 1991, 2008.
  • In 1500 Asia led; by 1900 Europe led; since 1978 weight has moved back towards Asia.

Economy and ideology

  • Industrialisation: Britain c. 1760 → Germany and the US by 1900 → Japan 1870s → East Asia after 1950 → China after 1978.
  • Capitalism (Smith 1776) · socialism and communism (Marx 1848, 1867) · fascism (1922, 1933) · social democracy · developmental state.
  • Post-war: golden age 1945-73 → 1971 end of gold convertibility → 1973 and 1979 oil shocks → market turn 1978-91 → 2008 crisis.

Empire and independence

  • Atlantic slave trade: c. 12.5 million embarked; abolition 1807-1888; Indian indenture 1834-1917.
  • Land empires end: Mughal 1857, Qing 1911, Ottoman 1922.
  • Decolonisation: India and Pakistan 1947, Indonesia 1949, Ghana 1957, seventeen African states in 1960, Algeria 1962, Portuguese Africa 1975, Namibia 1990, South Africa 1994.

  • Cold War phases: formation 1947-49 · crises 1950-62 · détente 1963-79 · second Cold War 1979-85 · end 1985-91.
