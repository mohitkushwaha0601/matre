Snapshot & identity

The Indian Navy is the maritime arm of the Indian Armed Forces. Its job, stated simply, is to make sure that whatever happens on India’s 7,516 km of coastline and in the 2.37 million sq km of its Exclusive Economic Zone happens on India’s terms.

Interview edge

“Why the Navy and not the Army or Air Force?” is asked in almost every naval knowledge interview. The weak answer is “I like ships.” The strong answer connects a personal trait to the service’s character: the Navy sends a 25-year-old officer to sea for months with responsibility for a watch, a weapon system and a team, far from any headquarters. If you are drawn to independent responsibility, technology and a genuinely expeditionary life, say so — and give one example from your own life where you had to decide alone.

History in milestones

You do not need dates for every event. You do need a clean narrative: colonial marine force → wartime expansion → post-independence Indianisation → 1971 proof of concept → blue-water ambition → today’s builder’s navy.

  • 1612East India Company forms the Marine at Surat to protect merchant shipping — the ancestral root the Navy claims.
  • 1830Renamed Her Majesty’s Indian Navy; later Royal Indian Marine, then Royal Indian Navy (1934).
  • 1946The Royal Indian Navy Mutiny in Bombay — a significant push toward independence.
  • 1950Becomes the Indian Navy on Republic Day; ships re-designated INS.
  • 1958Vice Admiral R.D. Katari becomes the first Indian Chief of the Naval Staff.
  • 1961Operation Vijay — liberation of Goa; the Navy’s first major combat employment.
  • 1971Operations Trident & Python strike Karachi; INS Vikrant blockades East Pakistan; PNS Ghazi is lost off Visakhapatnam. The decisive proof that sea power changes land outcomes.
  • 1988Operation Cactus — Indian forces foil a coup in the Maldives; INS Godavari and Betwa intercept the fleeing mercenaries at sea. The textbook example of India as regional first responder.
  • 1999Operation Talwar during Kargil — the Navy bottles up Pakistan’s sea lanes and oil imports without firing a shot. Best single illustration of “deterrence by presence.”
  • 2004Indian Ocean tsunami — simultaneous relief to Sri Lanka, Maldives and Indonesia establishes the Navy’s HADR reputation.
  • 200826/11 Mumbai attacks arrive by sea; the Navy is made the authority responsible for overall maritime security. Anti-piracy escorts begin in the Gulf of Aden.
  • 2016INS Arihant, India’s first indigenous SSBN, enters service — the sea leg of the nuclear triad.
  • 2020Operation Samudra Setu repatriates Indians during COVID-19; Samudra Setu II ferries oxygen in 2021.
  • 2022INS Vikrant commissioned — India joins the small club that designs and builds its own aircraft carriers. New ensign adopted the same day.
  • 2024MARCOS retake the hijacked MV Ruen 1,400 nm from the coast; 35 pirates brought to Mumbai for trial.
  • 2025Operation Sindoor: a carrier battle group forward-deployed in the North Arabian Sea keeps the Pakistan Navy in harbour for the duration of the conflict.
  • 2026Record expansion year — 19 warships planned for commissioning; INS Aridhaman, the third SSBN, joins the fleet; India hosts IFR, MILAN and the IONS Conclave together at Visakhapatnam.

Organisation & commands

Chain of command

President (Supreme Commander) → Prime Minister and Cabinet Committee on Security → Raksha Mantri (Defence Minister) → Ministry of Defence → Chief of Defence Staff and the Department of Military Affairs → Chief of the Naval Staff at Naval Headquarters, New Delhi.

Under the CNS at NHQ sit the Principal Staff Officers: the Vice Chief of the Naval Staff (VCNS), Deputy Chief (DCNS), Chief of Personnel (COP), Chief of Materiel (COM), and Controllers for Warship Production & Acquisition, Logistics and Personnel Services. The Warship Design Bureau — formerly the Directorate of Naval Design — is the in-house body that has designed over 100 indigenous ships.

The three operational commands

Naval commands — memorise this table
CommandHeadquartersCharacter & responsibility
Western Naval CommandMumbaiThe “Sword Arm.” Arabian Sea, Pakistan front, Gulf approaches. Hosts the Western Fleet and India’s carrier operations. Includes Karwar (Project Seabird).
Eastern Naval CommandVisakhapatnamBay of Bengal, Malacca approaches, the China-facing theatre. Home of the Eastern Fleet, the SSBN force and the Ship Building Centre.
Southern Naval CommandKochiThe training command. Runs the Indian Naval Academy at Ezhimala, flying training, the Fleet’s work-up and the Lakshadweep islands.

Each is headed by a Vice Admiral titled Flag Officer Commanding-in-Chief (FOC-in-C).

Two more formations the Navy plugs into

  • Andaman & Nicobar Command (ANC), Port Blair — India’s only tri-service theatre command, created in 2001. Usually but not always led by a naval officer. It guards the Malacca approaches. A common trick question: ANC is not a naval command, it is a joint command.
  • Strategic Forces Command (SFC) — a tri-service command under the Nuclear Command Authority. SSBN crews are naval, but operational control of the deterrent patrol rests with SFC, not with a naval C-in-C.

Fleets and flotillas

  • Western Fleet (Mumbai) and Eastern Fleet (Visakhapatnam), each under a Rear Admiral titled Flag Officer Commanding.
  • Flag Officer Submarines (FOSM) — the submarine arm’s professional head, based at Visakhapatnam.
  • Local defence formations: Maharashtra, Gujarat, Karnataka, Kerala, Tamil Nadu & Puducherry, Andhra Pradesh and Odisha Naval Areas, each under a Flag Officer.
  • Specialist units: MARCOS (Marine Commandos, raised 1987), the Sagar Prahari Bal for base protection, and the Offshore Defence Advisory Group for offshore oil assets.
Interview edge

If asked about theatre commands, know the shape of the debate rather than picking a side. The proposal is to restructure 17 single-service commands into integrated theatres — with a Maritime Theatre Command likely headquartered at Karwar or Thiruvananthapuram, absorbing both fleets, ANC and naval air assets. The arguments for are unity of command and efficient use of scarce assets; the arguments against are the loss of service-specific expertise and the risk of diluting the Navy’s ocean-wide, non-geographic role. Anil Chauhan is the current Chief of Defence Staff driving the reform.

Overseas and partner facilities

India does not run foreign bases in the Western sense, but it maintains access and monitoring arrangements: coastal radar chains in Mauritius, Seychelles, Sri Lanka, Maldives and Bangladesh; an airstrip and jetty developed on Agalega (Mauritius); a logistics presence at Duqm (Oman) and access to Chabahar (Iran); a listening post in Madagascar; and berthing rights under logistics-exchange agreements with the US (LEMOA), France, Australia, Japan, Singapore and others.

In descending order: Master Chief Petty Officer I → Master Chief Petty Officer II → Chief Petty Officer → Petty Officer → Leading Seaman → Seaman I → Seaman II. Agniveers recruited under the Agnipath scheme serve a four-year tour, with up to 25% retained in regular service.

Currency check (May 2026): the Navy’s proposal to indigenise sailor rank titles — which would have affected more than 65,000 sailors — was shelved. The MoD preferred a coordinated tri-service approach. Existing nomenclature continues. Officer ranks were never part of the proposal. Earlier related reforms that did happen: the 2022 ensign and crest change and the 2023 introduction of new officer epaulettes inspired by Shivaji’s seal.

Interview edge

Be ready for “Which branch do you want and why?” and, immediately after, “What if you are given a different branch?” The IO is testing whether you have a genuine preference and whether your commitment survives not getting it. Have a reasoned first choice built on your academic strengths and temperament, and a sincere answer that you want to serve in the Navy — the branch is the Navy’s call, not a condition of your service.

Types of ships

Learn the hierarchy first, then hang specific names on it. A useful mental ladder by size and role: aircraft carrier → destroyer → frigate → corvette → offshore patrol vessel → fast attack craft, with a parallel family of amphibious, survey, support and special-purpose ships.

Aircraft carriers

  • INS Vikramaditya (R33) — 45,000 t, converted Kiev-class Admiral Gorshkov, commissioned 2013. STOBAR with a ski-jump. Operates MiG-29K.
  • INS Vikrant (R11) — 45,000 t, India’s first indigenously designed and built carrier, built at Cochin Shipyard, commissioned 2 September 2022. STOBAR. Roughly 76% indigenous content.
  • IAC-2 / INS Vishal — the proposed third carrier. Current thinking favours a repeat-Vikrant of roughly 45,000 t with a deck reworked for UAV operations, rather than the earlier 65,000 t CATOBAR concept. The Navy has been pushing for government clearance; as of 2026 the project remains unapproved.

STOBAR = Short Take-Off But Arrested Recovery (ski-jump plus arrestor wires). CATOBAR = Catapult-Assisted Take-Off But Arrested Recovery. The difference matters: CATOBAR allows heavier aircraft and fixed-wing AEW, which STOBAR cannot.

Frigates

  • Nilgiri class (Project 17A) — 6,670 t stealth frigates, the current mainstay of new construction, built in parallel at Mazagon Dock and GRSE. In service: Nilgiri (Jan 2025), Himgiri and Udaygiri (both 26 Aug 2025 — the first time two major combatants from two shipyards were commissioned together), Taragiri (Apr 2026), Dunagiri (Jun 2026). Note that Udaygiri was the 100th ship designed by the Warship Design Bureau.
  • Shivalik class (Project 17) — Shivalik, Satpura, Sahyadri. India’s first stealth frigates.
  • Talwar / Tushil class (Project 11356) — Russian-built. INS Tushil (Dec 2024) and INS Tamal (commissioned 1 July 2025 at Yantar Shipyard, Kaliningrad) are the newest; two more of the class are being built at Goa Shipyard with Russian assistance.
  • Brahmaputra and Godavari classes — older indigenous frigates, progressively retiring.

Corvettes and small combatants

  • Kamorta class (P-28) — dedicated anti-submarine corvettes: Kamorta, Kadmatt, Kiltan, Kavaratti.
  • Kora, Khukri, Veer (Tarantul) classes — missile corvettes and fast attack craft.
  • Arnala class ASW Shallow Water Craft — 900 t, waterjet-propelled, 16 planned, built by GRSE and Cochin Shipyard to hunt submarines in the littorals where big ships cannot go. Arnala (Jun 2025), Androth (Oct 2025), Anjadip (Feb 2026), Agray (Jun 2026) are in service; Mahe class craft are joining alongside.
  • Next Generation Missile Vessels and Next Generation Offshore Patrol Vessels — under construction at Cochin Shipyard and Goa Shipyard.

Amphibious, survey and support

  • INS Jalashwa — Landing Platform Dock (ex-USS Trenton), the Navy’s largest amphibious ship. A Landing Platform Dock (LPD) programme for four larger ships is planned.
  • Magar and Shardul class Landing Ship Tanks; LCU Mk-IV landing craft operating largely from the Andamans.
  • Survey Vessel (Large) — Sandhayak class: Sandhayak, Nirdeshak, Ikshak (Nov 2025), Sanshodhak (Jun 2026). Each carries four survey motor boats. India’s hydrographic survey service also charts waters for friendly navies — a quiet but powerful diplomatic tool.
  • INS Nistar — Diving Support Vessel commissioned July 2025, built by Hindustan Shipyard. With the Deep Submergence Rescue Vessel it gives India a submarine-rescue capability that only a handful of navies possess; it made its operational debut in a multinational rescue exercise in the South China Sea in October 2025.
  • Fleet tankers — Deepak, Shakti, Jyoti; five new Fleet Support Ships are being built at Hindustan Shipyard. These are what turn a coastal navy into a blue-water navy.
  • INS Dhruv — nuclear missile-tracking and ocean surveillance ship.

Pennant number logic, often asked: R = aircraft carrier, D = destroyer, F = frigate, P = patrol/corvette, K = corvette/missile vessel, S = submarine, A = auxiliary, L = landing ship, M = mine countermeasures.

Interview edge

“Difference between a destroyer and a frigate?” Do not fumble this. Historically: destroyers were built to screen a fleet against torpedo boats and submarines; frigates for escort and patrol. Today the practical distinction is size and role weighting — a destroyer is larger (7,000–8,000 t), carries more vertical launch cells and is optimised for area air defence of a task force, while a frigate (4,000–7,000 t) is a multirole escort weighted toward anti-submarine and anti-surface work. Corvettes are smaller still and mission-specialised.

Submarines

Know the three-letter codes cold: SSBN = nuclear-powered ballistic missile submarine; SSN = nuclear-powered attack submarine; SSK = conventional diesel-electric attack submarine. Nuclear propulsion buys unlimited endurance and high sustained speed; a conventional boat is quieter at low speed and cheaper, but must surface or snorkel.

India’s nuclear doctrine is No First Use with credible minimum deterrence and assured massive retaliation. That doctrine is only credible if some part of the arsenal survives a first strike — which is exactly what an SSBN hidden in the Bay of Bengal provides. This is the single most important point to make if the subject comes up.

Nuclear attack submarines

  • Project 77 (formerly Project 75 Alpha) — six indigenous SSNs of roughly 10,000 t. The Cabinet Committee on Security cleared the first two in October 2024; they will be built at the Ship Building Centre, Visakhapatnam, with Larsen & Toubro, with induction projected in the mid-2030s. Designed around vertical-launch land-attack and anti-ship weapons plus Varunastra torpedoes.
  • India previously operated two leased Russian Akula-class boats as INS Chakra; a further lease has been under discussion.
  • Strategic point: the Navy prioritised SSNs over a third carrier in its own internal debate — an SSN can hold a hostile carrier group or a chokepoint at risk at a fraction of the cost of a carrier battle group.

Conventional submarines

  • Kalvari class (Project 75) — six Scorpène-design boats built at Mazagon Dock with France’s Naval Group: Kalvari, Khanderi, Karanj, Vela, Vagir and Vagsheer (commissioned 15 January 2025, completing the project). They lack the originally planned air-independent propulsion; DRDO’s indigenous fuel-cell AIP plug is to be retrofitted during refits.
  • Sindhughosh class — Russian Kilo-class, ageing; Shishumar class — German Type 209, ageing.
  • Project 75I — six new AIP-equipped boats. Germany’s TKMS with Mazagon Dock emerged as the only compliant bidder, because India insisted on an AIP system already proven at sea. The Finance Ministry cleared the acquisition in May 2026 and the contract has been moving toward final Cabinet Committee on Security approval, at a headline figure of roughly ₹70,000 crore. Contractually the first boat arrives seven years after signature, so realistically the early 2030s, with one per year thereafter.
  • Three additional Scorpènes — a follow-on order of about ₹36,000 crore has been discussed with France but has not been concluded.
  • Project 76 — a future fully indigenous conventional submarine design.
Interview edge

The honest and impressive answer on submarines is that India has a capability gap. The 1999 thirty-year plan envisaged 24 conventional submarines by 2030; India has roughly 16 boats, many over 30 years old, and the first Project 75I boat will not arrive before the early 2030s. Say this plainly, then say what offsets it: the ASW Shallow Water Craft programme, P-8I and MQ-9B surveillance, MH-60R helicopters, and the SSBN/SSN force. Candidates who only recite good news sound rehearsed; candidates who can name a problem and its mitigation sound like officers.

  • MH-60R Seahawk “Romeo” — 24 ordered from the US in 2020 to replace the Sea Kings; 21 delivered by mid-2026. Unmanned systems

  • MQ-9B SeaGuardian — high-altitude long-endurance ISR. Operated on lease since 2020; additional airframes leased in 2026, and a much larger tri-service purchase of 31 MQ-9Bs (about $3.9 bn) has been progressing.
  • Drishti-10 Starliner (Adani/Elbit Hermes 900) — medium-altitude endurance UAV, first delivered January 2024.
  • Heron Mk-I, ship-launched Camcopter S-100, and a growing family of indigenous shipborne drones and underwater autonomous vehicles.
Interview edge

Be able to explain why the Rafale M was chosen over the F/A-18 Super Hornet: commonality with the IAF’s existing 36 Rafales (training, spares, weapons), demonstrated ski-jump compatibility with Indian carriers, and a deeper Indo-French strategic relationship. And be able to state the honest caveat: it is an interim bridge, not a permanent solution, because the indigenous TEDBF has slipped.

Weapons & sensors worth naming

Strike and anti-ship

  • BrahMos — Indo-Russian supersonic cruise missile, ~Mach 2.8, ship-, sub-, air- and land-launched. The Navy’s signature weapon.
  • Nirbhay — subsonic long-range land-attack cruise missile.
  • K-15 Sagarika (750 km) and K-4 (3,500 km) submarine-launched ballistic missiles; K-5 in development.

Air defence

  • Barak-8 / MR-SAM — Indo-Israeli medium-range SAM, ~70–100 km.
  • VL-SRSAM — DRDO short-range point defence.
  • AK-630 / SRGM 76 mm — close-in weapon systems and main gun.

Underwater

  • Varunastra — indigenous heavyweight torpedo; TAL Shyena lightweight torpedo.
  • RBU-6000 and the indigenous ERASR anti-submarine rockets.
  • USHUS submarine sonar, HUMSA-NG hull-mounted sonar, towed arrays; Maareech anti-torpedo decoy.

Sensors & networks

  • MF-STAR (EL/M-2248) AESA radar on P-15A/B and P-17A ships.
  • GSAT-7 “Rukmini” — the Navy’s dedicated communications satellite, ~2,000 nm footprint; GSAT-7R to follow.
  • Shakti electronic warfare suite.

Maritime security

The 26/11 Mumbai attacks of 2008 reorganised India’s entire coastal security architecture. Ten terrorists reached Mumbai by hijacking a fishing trawler. Nothing in the naval syllabus is more likely to be discussed.

The information backbone

  • IMAC — Information Management and Analysis Centre at Gurugram, the nerve centre that fuses the picture.
  • NC3I — National Command Control Communications and Intelligence Network links about 50 naval and Coast Guard radar stations into a single common operating picture.
  • Coastal Surveillance Network — chains of static radar stations along the coast and islands, extended to partner countries.
  • IFC-IOR — the Information Fusion Centre – Indian Ocean Region at Gurugram, opened in December 2018, now hosting international liaison officers from many navies. It is the single best example of India acting as a regional public-goods provider.
  • Automatic Identification System transponders mandated on fishing vessels; biometric ID cards for fishermen; colour-coded boat registration.
  • Joint Operations Centres at Mumbai, Visakhapatnam, Kochi and Port Blair; regular Sea Vigil national coastal defence exercises.

The threats, in the order the Navy ranks them

  1. State competition — the growth of the PLA Navy and its presence in the Indian Ocean.
  2. Sea-borne terrorism and infiltration.
  3. Piracy and armed robbery at sea.
  4. Trafficking — narcotics, arms, human.
  5. Illegal, unreported and unregulated fishing, which is both an economic and a sovereignty problem.
  6. Grey-zone activity — maritime militias, research vessels doing survey work, undersea cable interference, actions calibrated to stay below the threshold of war. This is the phrase to use if you want to sound current.
  7. Non-traditional threats: climate, disaster, marine pollution, pandemic.

The Indian Ocean Region

Geography is the whole argument. India sits astride the Indian Ocean like a pier jutting 1,000 nm into it, within reach of every major sea lane. About 95% of India’s trade by volume and 68% by value moves by sea, and roughly 80% of its crude oil is imported across it. Globally, around two-thirds of the world’s oil shipments and a third of bulk cargo transit these waters.

Chokepoints to know by name and location

  • Strait of Hormuz — between Iran and Oman; the Gulf’s only outlet, and India’s oil lifeline.
  • Bab-el-Mandeb — between Yemen and Djibouti; gateway to the Red Sea and Suez.
  • Strait of Malacca — between Malaysia, Singapore and Sumatra; over 80% of China’s oil imports pass through it. India’s Andaman & Nicobar position overlooks the western approach — China’s so-called “Malacca dilemma.”
  • Sunda and Lombok Straits — the alternatives to Malacca, through Indonesia.
  • Mozambique Channel and the Cape of Good Hope route.
  • India’s own: the Nine Degree Channel (Lakshadweep), Eight Degree Channel (between Minicoy and Maldives), Ten Degree Channel (between Andaman and Nicobar) and the Six Degree Channel (south of Great Nicobar).

The competition

China maintains a persistent presence: its first overseas base at Djibouti (2017), port access and investment at Gwadar (Pakistan), Hambantota (Sri Lanka), Kyaukpyu (Myanmar) and elsewhere — the pattern India describes as the “String of Pearls.” Chinese research and survey vessels operate regularly in the Indian Ocean, and PLAN anti-piracy task forces have rotated there since 2008. India’s counter, informally called the “Necklace of Diamonds,” combines partner access (Duqm, Chabahar, Sabang, Changi, Agalega), capacity-building for small island states, and persistent surveillance.

India’s framework: SAGAR to MAHASAGAR

  • SAGAR — “Security and Growth for All in the Region,” announced by Prime Minister Modi in Mauritius in 2015. Its pillars: safeguarding India’s own interests, deepening security cooperation with neighbours, collective action on maritime threats, sustainable regional development, and engaging extra-regional powers constructively.
  • MAHASAGAR — “Mutual and Holistic Advancement for Security and Growth Across Regions,” announced in Mauritius in March 2025. It widens SAGAR from the Indian Ocean neighbourhood to the wider Global South, adding trade, connectivity, capacity-building and technology. The rhetorical shift matters: from “net security provider” to “preferred security partner” — a change of vocabulary aimed at countries like Sri Lanka and the Maldives that are sensitive about sovereignty.

Institutions India works through

  • IONS — Indian Ocean Naval Symposium, founded by India in 2008; India holds the chair for 2025–27 and hosted the Conclave of Chiefs at Visakhapatnam in February 2026 with 33 countries represented.
  • IORA — Indian Ocean Rim Association, the economic and policy forum.
  • Colombo Security Conclave — India, Sri Lanka, Maldives, Mauritius, Bangladesh and Seychelles.
  • Quad — India, US, Japan, Australia; the Indo-Pacific Maritime Domain Awareness partnership is its most concrete maritime output.
  • BIMSTEC, and the Indian Ocean Commission where India is an observer.

Mission-Based Deployments

Since 2017 the Navy keeps ships permanently stationed at key locations — the Gulf of Aden, the Persian Gulf, the North Arabian Sea, the Malacca, Sunda and Lombok approaches, the Andaman Sea and the southern Indian Ocean — rather than surging them from home ports. It is the practical mechanism behind “first responder”: a ship is already there when something happens.

Anti-piracy operations

India has escorted merchant shipping in the Gulf of Aden continuously since October 2008. Over that period the Navy has deployed more than a hundred ships and safely escorted several thousand merchant vessels carrying tens of thousands of seafarers of all nationalities.

Operation Sankalp

Launched in June 2019 after tanker attacks in the Gulf of Oman, and substantially expanded from December 2023 when the Red Sea crisis pulled Western warships toward countering Houthi missile and drone attacks, leaving a vacuum that Somali pirates exploited. In that phase India deployed over 20 warships and 5,000 personnel, becoming the largest single-nation naval presence in the region. Over roughly 100 days the force responded to 18 incidents, conducted about 1,000 boarding operations, queried more than 450 merchant vessels, rescued over 110 people — Indian, Pakistani and other nationals — escorted around 15 lakh tonnes of critical cargo and seized more than 3,000 kg of narcotics.

The MV Ruen operation — the story to tell

The Maltese-flagged bulk carrier MV Ruen was hijacked off Somalia in December 2023, the first successful Somali hijacking in nearly seven years, and then used as a mother ship. In March 2024, about 1,400 nautical miles from the Indian coast, INS Kolkata intercepted her. An IAF C-17 air-dropped two combat boats and MARCOS teams into the open ocean. The Navy disabled the ship’s steering and communications, and after a roughly 40-hour operation all 35 pirates surrendered and 17 crew were rescued without a single hostage casualty. The pirates were brought to Mumbai and prosecuted under India’s Maritime Anti-Piracy Act, 2022.

Current situation (2026): Somali piracy has resurged. The Joint Maritime Information Centre raised its threat assessment to “severe” in May 2026. Pirate groups now operate more than 1,000 nautical miles from the Somali coast using hijacked vessels as mother ships. INS Kolkata foiled an attempted attack on the merchant vessel MV Mashallah 1 near the Gulf of Aden in May 2026. Overlaying this are continuing Houthi attacks in the Red Sea and heightened Iran-related tension — three simultaneous threat streams stretching every navy in the region.

Why India does it

  • Self-interest — Indians make up a large share of the world’s merchant seafarers, and Indian trade and energy move through these waters.
  • Reputation — escorting other countries’ ships is the cheapest, most credible way to be seen as a security provider.
  • Operational value — sustained deployments 3,000 km from home prove logistics, endurance and command that no exercise can.

Cyber & information warfare

A modern warship is a floating data centre. Its combat management system fuses radar, sonar, electronic support and datalink feeds into one picture, and every one of those links is an attack surface. The Navy is generally regarded as the most digitally mature of the three Indian services, largely because network-centric operations are unavoidable at sea.

Structures

  • Defence Cyber Agency (DCyA) — established 2019 under the Integrated Defence Staff as a tri-service agency for defensive and offensive cyber operations. Notably, its first head was a naval officer, reflecting the Navy’s lead in this domain.
  • The Navy maintains its own cyber-security organisation, a dedicated Information Technology cadre in the Executive Branch, and Communications & Electronic Warfare as a full officer specialisation.
  • Defence Space Agency and Armed Forces Special Operations Division are the sister tri-service agencies created at the same time — name all three together if asked.
  • Recruitment has followed: the Navy now runs dedicated SSC Executive (IT) entries for graduates in computer science, cyber security, AI and data analytics.

The domains it covers

Defensive

  • Hardening shipborne and shore networks, and the links between them
  • Supply-chain security for imported combat systems and chips
  • Protecting industrial control systems in dockyards and shipyards
  • Counter-espionage — social engineering of personnel and families has been a documented vector

Offensive & electronic

  • Electronic warfare: jamming, spoofing, deception; the indigenous Shakti EW suite
  • Navigation warfare — GPS jamming and spoofing at sea, a real and growing problem in the Gulf and Red Sea
  • Undersea domain awareness, including protection of submarine cables and seabed infrastructure
  • Information operations and counter-narrative work in the maritime space

Digital initiatives you can name

The Navy has moved administration and logistics onto indigenous platforms — its own private cloud Meghdoot, a digital library and knowledge network, an indigenous operating system Maya adopted across defence networks, and data-analytics cells for predictive maintenance of machinery. It has also stood up AI-enabled projects for maritime pattern-of-life analysis, and DRDO has been developing an AI-enabled autonomous mine-hunting system for the Navy.

Interview edge

If asked whether cyber is the “fifth domain,” give the full list — land, sea, air, space, cyber — and add the current thinking that cognitive or information warfare is emerging as a sixth. Then make it concrete: during recent India–Pakistan tension, a large part of the contest was fought in claims, deepfakes and disputed imagery on social media within hours of any kinetic action. A naval officer today has to be able to operate when the picture itself is contested.

Role in war and in peacetime

This is a near-certain interview question and the one most candidates answer thinly. The doctrinal framing — from the Navy’s own Ensuring Secure Seas: Indian Maritime Security Strategy — divides naval roles into four: military, diplomatic, constabulary and benign. Use that structure and you will sound like you have read something.

Military role — in war

  • Sea control — the ability to use a sea area at will for a period of time. Not permanent ownership; a time-and-place proposition.
  • Sea denial — preventing an adversary from using an area, the strategy of the weaker fleet. Submarines are the classic tool.
  • Power projection ashore — carrier air, cruise missiles, amphibious landings, special forces insertion.
  • Protection of sea lines of communication and convoy escort; conversely, blockade and interdiction of enemy trade.
  • Strategic deterrence — the SSBN patrol that guarantees second-strike capability.
  • Escalation control — the sea offers graduated options that land borders do not, because you can mass force visibly without crossing anyone’s territory.

Diplomatic, constabulary and benign roles — in peace

  • Naval diplomacy — port calls, joint exercises, training foreign officers, hydrographic surveys for partner states, ship handovers and capacity-building.
  • Constabulary — anti-piracy, counter-terrorism at sea, counter-narcotics, anti-poaching, EEZ policing, sanctions monitoring.
  • Benign — humanitarian assistance and disaster relief, search and rescue, evacuation of citizens from conflict zones, medical outreach, oceanographic research, pollution response.
  • Presence — the single most underrated peacetime function. A grey hull on the horizon changes calculations without anyone firing.

Operation Sindoor and the naval dimension

After the Pahalgam terror attack of 22 April 2025 killed 26 civilians, India launched Operation Sindoor on the night of 6–7 May against nine terrorist camps in Pakistan and Pakistan-occupied Kashmir. The Navy did not fire the opening strikes. What it did was arguably harder: within 96 hours of the attack it moved a carrier battle group, surface forces, submarines and aviation assets into the North Arabian Sea, conducted live weapon firings to validate readiness, and held a forward, openly deterrent posture with the declared ability to strike selected targets — Karachi among them — at a time of India’s choosing. MARCOS were inserted forward for reconnaissance and targeting support, and P-8I aircraft and MH-60R helicopters provided the surveillance picture.

The result was that the entire Pakistan Navy stayed in harbour or hugged the Makran coast for the duration. Merchant shipping avoided Pakistani ports and war-risk insurance premiums rose, imposing an economic cost. The Director General of Naval Operations, Vice Admiral A.N. Pramod, described the posture publicly; the then Navy Chief later noted the force was minutes from striking when a halt to kinetic action was requested.

Interview edge

The lesson to draw from Sindoor, if asked, is about deterrence without firing. A fleet that is visibly ready, forward-deployed and capable can shape an adversary’s decisions more cheaply than one that shoots. Add the second lesson — tri-service synergy — since the operation was coordinated across all three services and is being used as an argument in the theatre-command debate. Avoid triumphalism; describe it as an operation with objectives and outcomes.

Knowledge question bank & how to handle it

What the board is actually assessing

The Knowledge assessment tests Officer Like Qualities across four factors: planning and organising ability, social adjustment, social effectiveness, and dynamism. Your naval knowledge is not one of the fifteen OLQs. It matters because it evidences two of them — effective intelligence and liveliness/interest in the service. A candidate who knows ten things deeply and says “I don’t know, sir” to the eleventh scores better than one who bluffs on all eleven.

The single most important rule

Never invent a fact. The interviewing officer is very often a serving naval officer who will know immediately. Say “I’m not sure, sir, but I believe…” and mark the boundary of your knowledge honestly, or simply say you don’t know and that you will look it up. Honesty under pressure is being directly assessed.

Personal and motivational

  1. Why the Navy specifically? What did you do to find out what naval life is actually like?
  2. What have you read about the Navy in the last month?
  3. Which branch do you want, and what if you are assigned another?
  4. Are you prepared for six months at sea away from family? How do you know?
  5. If you are not recommended today, what will you do tomorrow?
  6. Name three officers you admire and say why — be ready for follow-up on each.

Factual and organisational

  1. Who is the current CNS? Name the three commands and their headquarters.
  2. Why is Andaman & Nicobar Command different from the three naval commands?
  3. Explain the Navy’s motto and when the ensign last changed, and why.
  4. Difference between a destroyer, a frigate and a corvette.
  5. What does STOBAR mean and why does it limit Indian carriers?
  6. Difference between SSBN, SSN and SSK. Which does India build indigenously?
  7. Name India’s aircraft carriers and say what makes Vikrant significant.
  8. What is the role of the Coast Guard, and how is it different from the Navy?

Strategic and current affairs

  1. What is the String of Pearls? How is India responding?
  2. Explain SAGAR and MAHASAGAR, and what changed between them.
  3. What was the Navy’s role in Operation Sindoor?
  4. Why does India patrol the Gulf of Aden when it is 3,000 km away?
  5. Name three chokepoints in the Indian Ocean and explain why each matters.
  6. Should India build a third aircraft carrier or more nuclear attack submarines?
  7. What are theatre commands and where does the Navy stand on them?
  8. How has Atmanirbhar Bharat changed Indian shipbuilding? Give evidence.
  9. What is grey-zone warfare at sea?
  10. Should women serve on submarines? (Women already serve on warships, fly with the Navy, and the first woman officers were deployed at sea in 2021.)

Group discussion and lecturette topics that recur

  • Indian Ocean: zone of peace or zone of competition?
  • Is Atmanirbhar Bharat in defence realistic, or does it slow modernisation?
  • Should India pursue a third aircraft carrier?
  • Quad — a security alliance or a talking shop?
  • Agnipath: strengths and weaknesses.
  • Blue economy and India’s maritime future.
  • Climate change as a national security threat.
  • Artificial intelligence and unmanned systems in warfare.
  • India–China relations in the maritime domain.
How to structure a lecturette in three minutes

Thirty seconds of definition and why it matters now. Ninety seconds of two or three specific points, each with one concrete example — a name, a date, a number. Thirty seconds of a balanced view that acknowledges the other side. Thirty seconds of conclusion and what India should do. Specifics are what separate you: “India commissioned 14 ships in 2025 and plans 19 in 2026” lands far harder than “India is building many ships.”

Traps to avoid

  • Rehearsed answers. Officers hear the same coaching-class phrasing dozens of times a day. Speak in your own words even if they are plainer.
  • Jingoism. Describing an adversary contemptuously reads as immaturity, not patriotism. Professionals respect capability.
  • Contradicting your PIQ. Everything you wrote on the Personal Information Questionnaire is fair game and will be cross-checked in the interview, in the group tasks and in your stories.
  • Over-preparing facts, under-preparing yourself. Most rejections have nothing to do with defence knowledge. Know your own hobbies, your school, your friends’ names, your weaknesses, and what you have actually done with your time.
  • Being a passenger in group tasks. Knowledge earns nothing if you cannot contribute in a group under time pressure.
