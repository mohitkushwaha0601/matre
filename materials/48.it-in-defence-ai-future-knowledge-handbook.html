Using this handbook

Defence IT is a fast-moving field where official detail is limited and press reports are mixed with advocacy. This handbook gives you the concepts, the structure and the arguments, and marks what is established and what you should re-verify.

Read Part I and II for the concepts, then Part III for the three services (the Navy gets three sections). Part IV and V look at AI, innovation and the future. The toolkit closes with practice questions, debate motions and a revision sheet. Related handbooks in this collection: NavIC and the Indian Navy (navigation), Global Navies and A Sailor's Life at Sea.

The one framing to keep

Technology serves a decision. For every system in this handbook, ask four things: what does it sense, what does it connect, what does it help decide, and what happens when it is attacked or switched off?

Scope and caveats

  • Established Institutions, programmes and dates marked as such are in the public record.
  • Re-verify Procurement figures, deployment status, project names and organisational details change quickly; check current official releases (MoD, PIB, service websites).
  • Interpretation Analysis of effects, strategy and the future is judgement, not fact. Capabilities of operational systems are classified, and nothing here describes them beyond what is public.

Part I · Foundations

What IT does for a military, and who runs it in India

Why IT matters in defence

Information technology turns a collection of platforms into a connected force: it moves information faster than the opponent can, and uses it better.

In defence, "IT" covers computers, networks, software, data, communications, cyber and the satellites and sensors that feed them. A tank, a frigate or a fighter is still the instrument of force, but its value now depends on what it knows (sensors), who it can talk to (networks) and how quickly a commander can decide (software and data). This is the idea of a force multiplier: a connected formation can achieve more than a larger, isolated one.

The classic way to see this is the decision loop: observe, orient, decide, act. IT shortens each step. Sensors observe, data fusion orients, decision-support tools help decide, and secure communications carry the order to the shooter. The side that completes the loop faster controls the tempo of an engagement.

What IT adds

  • Shared situational awareness across services
  • Speed of decision and targeting
  • Better logistics, maintenance and readiness
  • Realistic, low-cost training through simulation
  • Precision, which can reduce collateral damage

What it also creates

  • A new attack surface: networks can be penetrated, jammed or deceived
  • Dependence on satellites, power and supply chains
  • Information overload and over-trust in tools
  • A growing skills gap in uniformed services
Connected does not mean safe

Every link that carries an order can also carry an attack. A modern force must be able to fight when degraded: with its networks cut, its satellites jammed and its screens dark. That is why doctrine stresses redundancy and the ability to operate on commander's intent.

India's defence IT architecture

India's defence IT is a mix of service networks, tri-service agencies, defence research and national bodies. The trend since 2019 has been toward joint structures.

The creation of the Chief of Defence Staff and the Department of Military Affairs in 2019 gave the armed forces a central voice on joint matters, and the Integrated Defence Staff (IDS) became the home of several tri-service agencies. Below that sit the service networks (Army, Navy, Air Force), each with its own signals, IT and cyber branches, and the research and industry base that supplies them.

Why joint matters for IT

Three services buying three incompatible systems is the classic failure. Common standards, shared data formats and joint agencies are what make "jointness" real rather than rhetorical.

Part II · Security perspectives

Defending networks and fighting with information

Cyber threats and defence

Cyberspace is now a domain of warfare, alongside land, sea, air and space, and it is contested every day, not only in war.

Military networks face espionage by state-linked groups, criminal ransomware, supply-chain compromise, denial-of-service, insider threats and disinformation. Operational technology (the control systems on ships, aircraft and bases) is as exposed as office IT. Many incidents are below the threshold of war, which is why the activity is called "grey-zone".

Threat types and typical defences
ThreatWhat it doesTypical defence
Espionage (APT)Persistent, quiet theft of plans and dataNetwork monitoring, segmentation, counter-intelligence
RansomwareLocks systems for paymentOffline backups, patching, access control
Supply-chain attackCompromises hardware or software before deliveryTrusted vendors, testing, indigenous components
Denial of serviceFloods a service to disrupt itRedundancy, traffic filtering
Jamming and spoofingDisrupts or falsifies radio and satellite signals (including GNSS)Anti-jam design, alternative navigation, electronic protection
Insider threatMisuse or leak by a trusted personVetting, least-privilege access, awareness
DisinformationShapes perception and moraleVerification, rapid official communication, media literacy

Core defensive ideas

  • Defence in depth: several layers, so one failure is not fatal.
  • Zero trust: verify every user and device, every time.
  • Segmentation and air gaps: keep critical systems apart, knowing that air gaps can be bridged (as the 2010 Stuxnet case showed).
  • Red teaming: attack your own systems to find weaknesses first.

The Indian context

  • Malware was found on a Kudankulam nuclear plant network in 2019, which the operator acknowledged. Established
  • A 2021 private-sector report linked a China-based group to probes of Indian power-grid systems; Indian officials said no grid disruption resulted. Attribution disputed
  • Public advisories regularly warn services against unvetted apps and social-engineering approaches.
The weakest layer is usually human

Phishing, honey-traps on social media and careless handling of removable media cause more breaches than clever zero-day exploits. For an officer, personal digital discipline is an OLQ in practice: reliability, responsibility and self-control.

C4ISR, space and the kill chain

C4ISR stands for command, control, communications, computers, intelligence, surveillance and reconnaissance: the whole system that lets a commander see, decide and act.

Think in terms of the kill chain: find, fix, track, target, engage and assess. Modern warfare compresses this chain from hours to minutes, and the aim of "sensor-to-shooter" integration is to link whoever detects a target to whoever can strike it, whatever service they belong to. Satellites are central: they carry communications, imagery, signals intelligence and navigation signals, which is why space is both a force enabler and a vulnerability.

See

Radars, airborne early warning (the indigenous Netra platform is an example), maritime patrol aircraft, satellites, unmanned systems, signals intelligence.

Connect

Secure fibre and radio, tactical data links, military satellite communications. GSAT-7 (Navy, 2013) and GSAT-7A (Air Force and Army, 2018) are dedicated military communications satellites. Established

Decide and act

Command and control software, fusion engines, fire-control systems and, increasingly, AI decision-support that ranks options for a human commander.

  • Electronic warfare (EW): attacking and protecting the electromagnetic spectrum; the contest over radars, radios and GNSS signals.
  • Positioning, navigation and timing (PNT): GPS-type signals are weak and easy to jam, hence India's NavIC and the push for alternatives such as inertial and celestial navigation.
  • Counter-space: India's 2019 anti-satellite test showed capability; debris and norms remain concerns.
Degraded operations

Because networks and satellites will be attacked, training must include fighting without them. The best-connected force is not the one that never loses connectivity, but the one that keeps functioning when it does.

Part III · The tri-services

Army, Air Force and a special focus on the Navy

Indian Army

The Army's IT challenge is scale and terrain: millions of nodes, from a corps headquarters to a platoon on a glacier, all needing secure communications and shared pictures.

The Corps of Signals runs the Army's communications, with training at the Military College of Telecommunication Engineering, Mhow. The backbone has long been ASCON (a static switched network) and the radio-based AREN, with tactical command, control, communications and information (Tac C3I) systems for forward formations. The Shakti system for artillery and the Battlefield Surveillance System aim to cut the time between detecting a target and firing on it. Re-verify current fielding status.

  • Air defence: the indigenous Akashteer air-defence control and reporting system links radars and guns into one picture, and was reported to have been used in Operation Sindoor (2025). Re-verify
  • Drones and counter-drone: small drones for surveillance and strike, and the sensors and jammers to defeat them, are now a core infantry and artillery concern.
  • Transformation: the Army declared 2023 the "Year of Transformation" and 2023-32 the "Decade of Transformation", with technology absorption as a stated pillar. Established
  • Cyber and EW: cyber units and electronic-warfare capability sit alongside signals, with the Army Design Bureau linking field needs to industry.

Army-specific hard problems

  • Reliable communications in mountains, deserts and jungle, where line-of-sight and bandwidth are limited.
  • Securing a very large and varied network, including older equipment.
  • Giving a young soldier a usable device without exposing the unit's location.

Indian Air Force

An air force is the most network-dependent service: aircraft, radars and missiles must be fused into one picture within seconds.

The IAF's air-defence backbone is the Integrated Air Command and Control System (IACCS), which fuses military and civil radars, airborne early-warning aircraft and ground-based air defence into a single air situation picture, carried over its own secure optical network, AFNET. Established AFNET and IACCS were commissioned in the early 2010s.

Where IT shows up

  • Airborne early warning and control (AEW&C), including the indigenous Netra, extends radar coverage.
  • Fighters use sensor fusion and data links to share targets (the aim of aircraft such as the Rafale and upgraded Su-30MKI). Re-verify upgrade status
  • Long-range surface-to-air missile systems (the S-400, delivered from 2021) depend on cued, networked radars. Established
  • Simulators, data analytics for maintenance and mission-planning software raise readiness and cut cost.

Where it is heading

  • Manned-unmanned teaming: crewed fighters controlling "loyal wingman" drones, with Indian development programmes under way. Re-verify
  • Large armed and surveillance drones, including the planned MQ-9B purchase (31 for the three services, announced 2024). Established
  • AI-assisted target recognition and electronic-warfare management.
  • A resilient, space-linked air-defence picture across the whole country.
Why air defence is an IT problem

The hard part of air defence is not the missile; it is telling a hostile cruise missile or drone from a civilian airliner or a bird in seconds, and deciding which weapon to commit. That is data fusion, classification and command logic. It is also why the quality of software and the integrity of the network decide outcomes.

Navy I: networks and command

The Navy is the most IT-intensive service by nature: a ship is a floating network, and a fleet only works if its ships, aircraft, submarines and shore commands share a picture across thousands of kilometres of ocean.

At sea there is no cable and no tower. Everything travels by radio or satellite, so the Navy has led India's military-satellite effort. GSAT-7 (Rukmini), launched in 2013, gave the Navy dedicated satellite communications over the Indian Ocean, linking ships, submarines, aircraft and shore stations. A follow-on, GSAT-7R (CMS-03), was reported to have been launched in late 2025. Established GSAT-7; Re-verify GSAT-7R details.

Maritime Domain Awareness (MDA)

After the 2008 Mumbai attacks, India built a coastal surveillance and information architecture. The National Command Control Communication and Intelligence (NC3I) network, linked to the Navy's Information Management and Analysis Centre (IMAC) at Gurugram, fuses radar, AIS (ship-identification) and other data. The Information Fusion Centre for the Indian Ocean Region (IFC-IOR), set up at Gurugram in 2018, shares maritime information with partner navies through liaison officers. Established

Navy and navigation

A ship that loses satellite navigation or communications is blind and mute. Hence the Navy's interest in NavIC, inertial navigation and anti-jam design. The full story of NavIC is in the NavIC and the Indian Navy handbook.

SSB link

A ship's network works only if every sailor follows procedure: communication discipline, correct handling of classified material and prompt reporting. These are habits that show Reasoning, Organising Ability and Sense of Responsibility long before any technology is involved.

Navy II: sensors, unmanned systems and AI

Seas are huge and mostly empty; the problem is finding the few vessels that matter. This is where sensors, unmanned platforms and AI help the Navy most.

Surface and air sensors

Ship radars, electro-optical systems, long-range maritime patrol aircraft (the P-8I), helicopters such as the MH-60R, and shore-based radars. Space-based imagery and AIS tracking widen the view beyond the horizon. Combined, they give the commander a "recognised maritime picture".

Underwater awareness

Anti-submarine warfare depends on sonar and acoustic analysis. Indigenous sonars such as the HUMSA-NG series (developed by DRDO's NPOL, Kochi) equip ships. Seabed and fixed sensors, and signal-processing software that classifies underwater sounds, make the sea "transparent". Interpretation AI-based acoustic classification is an active area of work.

Unmanned systems

Aerial drones extend the fleet's eyes; surface and underwater unmanned vehicles can patrol, survey, clear mines and carry sensors. India's planned MQ-9B purchase (announced 2024) includes Navy aircraft, following earlier leased SeaGuardian operations. Re-verify numbers and delivery.

AI in the Navy

Public uses include anomaly detection in ship tracks (spotting vessels that go "dark" or behave oddly), image recognition, predictive maintenance and decision-support. The Navy has set up AI work, including a reported centre of excellence at INS Valsura. Re-verify The human always decides on weapons.

Example: AI-assisted maritime picture

A hundred thousand merchant ships cross the Indian Ocean. Almost all are lawful. An AI tool compares each ship's declared route, speed and AIS behaviour with the pattern of normal traffic, flags the few that look wrong (a fishing boat in a restricted area, a tanker that switched off AIS), and asks a human analyst to look. The machine narrows the haystack; the analyst decides what to do.

The limit of the sea picture

Most of the ocean's traffic is not under any one nation's control, and AIS can be switched off or faked. Sensors and AI improve the picture but never give a complete one, so the Navy still needs judgement, patrols and partners.

Navy III: shore, people and industry

A fleet's technology is only as good as the people who run it, the shipyards that build it and the shore establishments that support it.

People and training

The Navy trains specialists across electronics, electrical, communications and weapons: for example, electrical training at INS Valsura (Jamnagar) and engineering at INS Shivaji (Lonavala). The recruitment change to Agniveer, with a short initial tenure, raises a question about how well the Navy can retain the deep technical skills that complex ships need. Interpretation The Navy has said it needs technically able sailors and has expanded training in IT and cyber.

Shipbuilding and indigenisation

The Navy has set a goal of being self-reliant (Atmanirbhar) by 2047. Warships are designed by the Directorate of Naval Design and built at yards such as Mazagon Dock, Garden Reach and Cochin Shipyard, with digital design, simulation and integrated platform management systems. Two Navy-specific innovation bodies are the Technology Development Acceleration Cell (TDAC) and the Naval Innovation and Indigenisation Organisation (NIIO), created in 2020, with the SPRINT initiative (2022) to take start-up solutions into service through iDEX. Established

Strengths

  • A long record of warship design and construction in India.
  • Established structures for working with start-ups and academia.
  • A leading role in regional information-sharing (IFC-IOR).

Challenges

  • Imported sensors, chips and software in many systems.
  • Securing shipboard operational technology from cyber-attack.
  • Retaining skilled technical people in a competitive market.
  • Long ship-building timelines against fast-changing technology: software must be upgradable through the ship's life.

Why Navy gets the special focus

The Navy operates far from base, in a medium where sensors and communications are hard, across a region (the Indian Ocean) where India's interests are growing. Its IT problems (networks at sea, domain awareness, unmanned systems, securing ship systems) are a preview of the issues every service will meet. The wider picture is in the Global Navies handbook.

Part IV · Innovation and AI

Intelligence, new technologies and the people who build them

AI in defence

Artificial intelligence is software that learns patterns from data. In defence it is best seen as a tool for speed and scale in specific tasks, not as a replacement for command.

India created the Defence AI Council and the Defence AI Project Agency in 2019, and in 2022 the Ministry of Defence showcased 75 AI-enabled products and technologies. Established The use cases cluster into a few families.

Where AI is being used or tested in defence
UseWhat AI doesExample task
Intelligence and surveillanceFinds objects and changes in imagery and videoSpotting vessels, vehicles or construction in satellite pictures
Decision supportRanks options and flags risks for a humanSuggesting routes, force packages or threat priorities
Logistics and maintenancePredicts failures and demandReplacing a pump before it breaks at sea
Cyber defenceDetects unusual behaviour in networksFlagging a compromised account
AutonomyLets machines navigate and act with limited controlUnmanned vessels, drones, robotic systems
Language and trainingTranslates, summarises, simulatesIntercept summaries; synthetic opposing forces in wargames

Humans in, on or out of the loop

In the loop

A person approves each action. Slow but controlled; the norm for the use of lethal force.

On the loop

The machine acts, a person supervises and can intervene. Used for fast defensive tasks such as missile defence.

Out of the loop

The machine decides and acts alone. Highly contested for weapons; accepted for non-lethal tasks like navigation.

What AI cannot yet do well

AI is brittle outside the data it was trained on, can be fooled by adversarial inputs, can inherit bias from its data, and often cannot explain its answer. In war, the opponent will deliberately feed it misleading data. Good use means testing under realistic conditions, keeping a human accountable, and having a fallback when the tool fails.

A test for any new technology

Does it solve a real operational problem, can it be secured, can it be sustained and upgraded by our own people, and does it work when degraded? Many "innovations" fail one of these four.

Innovation and indigenisation

India's defence innovation policy aims to turn the armed forces from a buyer of foreign systems into a customer of Indian start-ups, labs and industry.

The main instruments

  • iDEX (2018) funds start-ups and MSMEs to solve service-stated problems; ADITI (2024) is a scheme to back deep-tech defence innovations within it. Established
  • Positive Indigenisation Lists (from 2020) ban imports of listed items to give Indian industry assured demand. Established
  • Defence corridors in Uttar Pradesh and Tamil Nadu, and the Defence Testing Infrastructure Scheme to build test facilities.
  • Make categories in the acquisition procedure to fund indigenous development.
  • FDI in defence up to 74% via the automatic route (2020). Established

The players

  • DRDO, with labs in AI, robotics, electronics and communications.
  • Defence public sector units such as BEL and HAL, long the main suppliers of defence electronics and aircraft.
  • Private industry (large groups and mid-sized firms) increasingly in radars, drones and software.
  • Start-ups and academia, including IIT-based research.
  • Service innovation bodies such as the Army Design Bureau and the Navy's NIIO.

What still slows things down

  • The "valley of death": a start-up wins a prototype contract but struggles to get a production order.
  • Procurement time: long trials and approvals are slow for software that should update every few months.
  • Imported cores: advanced chips, sensors and some software libraries are still foreign.
  • Testing and data: shortage of realistic, shareable military data to train and test AI.
Indigenous is not the same as self-reliant

A system assembled in India from imported cores is a step forward but not full independence. The test is whether India can design, update and secure the system without foreign permission. Interpretation

Lessons that travel

  • Resilience beats elegance: assume networks will be attacked.
  • Software is a weapon that must be updated in weeks, not years.
  • Commercial technology is a real source of advantage, and also of dependency.
  • Cheap and numerous can defeat expensive and few.

Cautions

  • Another country's architecture, built for its geography and alliances, may not fit India.
  • Reliance on foreign commercial services in war can be cut off.
  • Claims about AI performance in combat are hard to verify and are often promotional.

Part V · The future

Where defence IT is heading, and the limits

The future of IT in defence

Forecasts are uncertain, but a few trends are visible and are shaping procurement and doctrine today.

  1. Multi-domain operations

    Land, sea, air, space, cyber and the electromagnetic spectrum planned and fought as one system, supported by a shared data layer.

  2. Human-machine teaming

    Commanders and analysts work with AI assistants; crewed platforms lead groups of uncrewed ones.

  3. Software-defined forces

    Capabilities arrive as updates, not only as new hardware; open architectures let new tools be added quickly.

  4. Mass and autonomy

    Large numbers of low-cost drones and unmanned vessels, with counter-drone defences to match.

  5. Resilient space and quantum layers

    Many small satellites, alternative navigation and quantum-secured communications.

  6. Information and cognitive contest

    Competition to shape perceptions, including deepfakes and automated influence campaigns.

The Navy in 2035-2047 Interpretation

  • A fleet linked by resilient satellite and data networks, with a continuous, AI-assisted picture of the Indian Ocean.
  • Large numbers of unmanned aerial, surface and underwater vehicles working with crewed ships and submarines.
  • Digital shipyards and digital twins that cut build and maintenance time.
  • Indigenous chips, sensors and software in the key systems.
  • Sailors who combine seamanship with data and cyber skills.
The constant

Whatever the technology, command, courage and sound judgement stay human. The officer's task is to use machines well, question them when they are wrong and take responsibility for the result. That is why selection still tests character, not software skills.

Risks, ethics and human control

The same technologies that increase speed and precision also raise questions about accountability, escalation and trust.

Key risks

  • Automation bias: over-trusting a machine's output.
  • Speed and escalation: machine-speed decisions leave less time for judgement and diplomacy.
  • Accountability gap: who is responsible when an autonomous system errs?
  • Data and supply chain: poisoned data, back-doored hardware, foreign dependence.
  • Secrecy against oversight: classified systems are hard to audit.
  • Talent: competition with industry for skilled people.

The legal and ethical frame

  • International humanitarian law requires distinction, proportionality and precaution; these require human judgement.
  • Talks on lethal autonomous weapons have run under the UN Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons since 2014 without a binding treaty. Established
  • The 2023 US-led Political Declaration and the REAIM summits (2023 and 2024) promote responsible military AI as non-binding norms. Established
  • India takes part in the UN discussions and has generally favoured a cautious, non-binding approach so far. Re-verify
The accountability principle

A commander cannot hand responsibility to an algorithm. Whatever the technology, a named human must be answerable for a decision to use force. This principle is the practical heart of the ethics debate.

A balanced view

Advocates argue that AI-enabled precision and faster warning can save lives and avoid mistakes. Sceptics argue that autonomy lowers the threshold for war and diffuses blame. Both are partly right, which is why most states favour testing, human control and clear rules rather than a total ban or unrestricted use.

Glossary

C4ISR
Command, control, communications, computers, intelligence, surveillance and reconnaissance.
Force multiplier
A factor that increases a force's effectiveness without increasing its size.
Kill chain
The steps from detecting a target to engaging and assessing it.
OODA loop
Observe, orient, decide, act; the decision cycle.
MDA
Maritime Domain Awareness; an effective understanding of everything at sea that could affect security.
AIS
Automatic Identification System; ships broadcast identity, position and course.
IFC-IOR
Information Fusion Centre for the Indian Ocean Region, at Gurugram.
NC3I
National Command Control Communication and Intelligence network linking coastal and maritime sensors.
GSAT-7 series
Indian military communications satellites (Navy; Air Force and Army).
CMS
Combat Management System; the ship's tactical computer system.
Tactical data link
A secure link that shares tracks and targets between platforms in real time.
ASCON, AFNET, NFS
Army static communication network, Air Force network and the shared Network for Spectrum fibre backbone.
DCyA, DSA
Defence Cyber Agency and Defence Space Agency; tri-service bodies.
APT
Advanced Persistent Threat; a skilled, long-running intrusion, often state-linked.
Zero trust
A security model that verifies every user and device continually.
Electronic warfare
Use of the electromagnetic spectrum to attack, protect or exploit signals.
PNT
Positioning, navigation and timing.
Human in/on the loop
A person approves each action, or supervises and can intervene.
LAWS
Lethal Autonomous Weapon Systems.
iDEX / ADITI
Innovation schemes that fund start-ups to solve defence problems.
Digital twin
A virtual model of a physical system used for design, training or maintenance.

Last-day summary

  • IT is the nervous system of a force: sense, connect, decide, act, protect.
  • Connected does not mean safe; train and plan to fight degraded.
  • India's structure: CDS and DMA; DCyA, DSA, DIA; DRDO; iDEX; NFS, ASCON, AFNET.
  • Cyber threats: espionage, ransomware, supply chain, jamming, insiders; weakest layer is human.
  • Army: Signals, ASCON, Shakti, drones and counter-drone, Akashteer. Air Force: IACCS, AFNET, AEW&C, S-400.
  • Navy: GSAT-7, networks at sea, NC3I and IMAC, IFC-IOR, P-8I, sonars, unmanned systems, NIIO and SPRINT, a 2047 self-reliance goal.
  • AI: useful for imagery, maintenance, cyber and decision support; limited by data, brittleness and accountability.
  • A human must remain accountable for the use of force.
  • Innovation: iDEX, ADITI, Positive Indigenisation Lists, 74% FDI; problems: valley of death, imported chips.
  • Global: JADC2 and Replicator (US), intelligentised warfare (China), cheap drones and software in Ukraine.
  • Future: multi-domain operations, human-machine teaming, software-defined forces, unmanned mass, resilient space, quantum.
